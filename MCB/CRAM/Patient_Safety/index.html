1. Patient Safety and Healthcare-Associated Infections MUST KNOW Page 1

Core idea: Patient safety in infectious disease means stopping patients from acquiring infections they did not have when they walked in. Those infections are HAIs, and the whole lecture is built on defining, recognizing, and blocking them.

Patient safety

  • Defined as the absence of preventable harm to a patient and reduction of risk of unnecessary harm associated with healthcare to an acceptable minimum.
  • Primum non nocere = First, do no harm.
  • In infectious diseases it refers to the systematic efforts to prevent harm from HAIs and reduce risk of unnecessary harm with medical care.

Healthcare-associated infection (HAI)

  • Also called nosocomial infection.
  • CDC definition: infections that occur in patients during the process of receiving healthcare, which were NOT present or incubating at the time of admission.
  • Applies to hospital, nursing home, hospice, and rehabilitation centers for long-term care, not just hospitals.
THE TIMING RULE — HIGHEST-YIELD FACT Patient admitted, no infection present or incubating
↓
Infection appears 48 hours or more after admission OR within 10 days after discharge
↓
Consider and rule in or out: is this a nosocomial infection or HAI?

Why patient safety from infectious disease matters

  • Incidence: 1 in 10 admitted patients — fairly common.
  • HAIs are more aggressive than usual and community-acquired infections.
  • Impact on outcomes:
    • Increased risk for sepsis (23.5%)
    • Increased risk for antimicrobial resistance (AMR)
    • Increased mortality and morbidity (poor prognosis)
    • Prolonged hospital stay
    • Increased healthcare cost
  • Causal link the lecturer draws: it is the aggressive nature of HAIs that drives the sepsis risk and the antimicrobial resistance.

Clinical relevance

  • Advocate early discharge when there is no reason to prolong the stay. This is the prevention measure the lecture names, and the answer key calls HAIs preventable on this basis.
  • When an HAI such as HAP grows Pseudomonas, A. baumannii, MRSA, or Enterococcus faecalis on culture, the patient becomes harder to treat.
    • Stronger antibiotics are needed → cost more, are more toxic, and have greater side effects.
COMMON CONFUSION An infection that is a complication of the primary disease, or one that was present or incubating on admission, is not an HAI. Neither is an infection contracted by a healthcare worker. Only the >48 hour after admission option is correct.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Patient safety = absence of preventable harm plus reduction of unnecessary risk to an acceptable minimum.
  • HAI = nosocomial = not present or incubating on admission.
  • 48 hours or more after admission, or within 10 days after discharge.
  • 1 in 10 admitted patients. Sepsis risk 23.5%.
  • Prevention named in the lecture: early discharge.

2. Surgical Site Infections (SSI) MUST KNOW Page 2

Core idea: Infection at the surgical site of the procedure. Depth determines severity, and S. aureus (MRSA) is the leading cause.

The five types of HAI covered by this lecture

  • Surgical site infections (SSI)
  • Catheter-associated UTI (CAUTI)
  • Hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP) or ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP)
  • Bloodstream infections (CLABSI)
  • Clostridium difficile infections (CDI)

Depth of involvement

  • Superficial — affecting only the skin
  • Deeper tissues, organs, or implanted materials → more serious infections
  • Soft tissue infections

Organisms

Organism groupNamed organismsExam clue from the lecture
Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)S. aureusLeading cause of SSI, most common
Coagulase-negative staphylococci (CONS)S. epidermidisAssociated with clean wounds
Enterococcus speciesE. faecalisPatients with underlying conditions or after complex surgeries
Gram-negative bacilliE. coli, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniaeNotorious in contaminated wounds and immunocompromised patients
Anaerobic bacteriaBacteroides speciesDeep wound infections

Risk factors

  • Type of surgery — clean contaminated or contaminated surgeries all pose a risk of developing SSI.
  • Patient factors — immunocompromised state, comorbidities such as diabetes and heart failure.
  • Surgical technique → reduces risk of infection.
  • Antibiotic prophylaxis → reduces risk of infection.
RECALL CUE (study aid, not from the PDF) Depth of the wound points to the organism. Skin and clean wound → staph (S. aureus, S. epidermidis). Contaminated wound → gram-negative bacilli. Deep wound → Bacteroides.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • S. aureus (MRSA) is the leading and most common cause of SSI.
  • CONS / S. epidermidis pairs with clean wounds.
  • Bacteroides pairs with deep wound infections.
  • The two risk factors that lower risk are surgical technique and antibiotic prophylaxis.

3. Catheter-Associated UTI (CAUTI) MUST KNOW Page 2

Core idea: Urinary tract infection caused by the use of catheters. One of the most common causes of HAI, and E. coli is the most common organism.
  • Leads to significant morbidity and increased healthcare cost.

Organisms

  • Gram-negative bacteria
    • E. coli — most common
    • K. pneumoniae and P. aeruginosa
  • Enterococci → E. faecalis
  • Staphylococcus aureus
  • Fungal → Candida spp.

Risk factors

  • Indwelling catheter duration — the single factor the formative quiz tests, prolonged duration increases risk.
  • Patient factors — immunocompromised state, comorbidities.
  • Catheter care practices → decrease risk of infection.
  • Antibiotic use → decrease risk of infection.
EXAM TRAP The PDF lists catheter care practices and antibiotic use under the heading risk factors, yet annotates both with decrease risk of infection. Read the option carefully. Proper catheter care, immune competence, and frequent changing of catheters are the wrong answers to the question on what increases CAUTI risk — the answer is prolonged catheter duration.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • E. coli is the most common CAUTI organism. Compare with SSI where S. aureus leads.
  • CAUTI is a fungal-capable HAI (Candida).
  • The risk factor that increases risk is indwelling catheter duration.

4. Hospital-Acquired and Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (HAP / VAP) MUST KNOW Pages 2–3

Core idea: Lung infection in patients, particularly those on mechanical ventilation. Bacteria enter the lungs through the endotracheal tube used for ventilation. Unlike SSI and CLABSI, the dominant organisms here are gram-negative.
MECHANISM Patient intubated, endotracheal tube in place
↓
Natural protection and clearance system impaired, colonization with bacteria at the tube
↓
Aspiration of microorganisms directly through the ET tube or around the cuff
↓
Bacteria reach the lungs → ventilator-associated pneumonia

5. Bloodstream Infections and CLABSI MUST KNOW Page 3

Core idea: Bacteria enter the bloodstream through a central line, defined in the lecture as a Type IV catheter placed into a large vein (typically neck, chest, or groin) where fluids and medications are administered.
  • CLABSI is similar to CAUTI in the sense that it shares the same common risks and the same common organisms.

Organisms

  • Gram-positive bacteria
    • S. aureus (MRSA)
    • CONS — S. epidermidis
    • Enterococcus
  • Gram-negative bacteria
    • P. aeruginosa
    • K. pneumoniae
    • E. coli
  • Fungal → Candida spp.

Risk factors

  • Duration of central line placement
  • Patient factors
  • Central line care practices
  • Antibiotic use

Two named bloodstream conditions

ConditionWhat it isKey point
MRSA bacteremiaSerious bloodstream infection caused by a serious type of Staphylococcus aureusResistant to many antibiotics
EndocarditisInfection of the inner lining of the heart chambers and valvesOften results from bacteria entering the bloodstream through medical procedures and use of catheters
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Central line = Type IV catheter in a large vein of the neck, chest, or groin.
  • CLABSI organism list leads with gram-positives (S. aureus, CONS, Enterococcus), unlike HAP and VAP which lead with gram-negatives.
  • Endocarditis = inner lining of heart chambers and valves, linked to procedures and catheters.

6. Clostridium difficile Infection (CDI) MUST KNOW Pages 3–4

Core idea: CDI follows many antibiotic treatments that disrupt gut flora. C. difficile is a spore-forming bacterium that secretes toxins, causing severe diarrhea and colitis. It is a common cause of HAIs and a major concern in healthcare facilities.
MECHANISM Normal healthy gut
↓ Antibiotic therapy disrupts gut flora
Colonization by C. difficile spores
↓ spores germinate, bacteria secrete toxins
Development of C. difficile infection → severe diarrhea and colitis
↓ after antibiotic therapy, C. difficile spores remain
Recurrence

Symptoms

  • Diarrhea, abdominal pain, fever
  • Most severe symptom: pseudomembranous colitis — very hard to treat

Risk factors

  • Antibiotic use — prolonged, recent, frequent
  • Advanced age
  • Recent surgery
  • Underlying medical conditions

Note on the source: this risk-factor list sits under a heading the PDF labels Risk factors for CLABSI on page 4, but the content and surrounding text follow C. difficile. The PDF does not resolve the mislabel.

EXAM TRAP C. difficile is acquired fecal-orally and by touching infected items. It is NOT airborne and NOT droplet. In a patient with bloody diarrhea on meropenem diagnosed with C. difficile, the precaution that does not apply is respiratory hygiene. Handwashing before and after contact, gowns and gloves on entering the room, and disinfecting linens and gowns all do apply.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Trigger: antibiotics disrupting gut flora. Recurrence happens because spores remain.
  • Spore-forming, toxin-secreting.
  • Worst symptom: pseudomembranous colitis.
  • Transmission: contact. Placement: single patient isolation.

7. Causes and Transmission of HAIs MUST KNOW Page 4

Core idea: HAIs are caused by pathogens spreading through various routes in healthcare settings and are acquired while patients are receiving care. Three components must line up for spread, and there are three named causes and four modes.

3 components required for infection spread

ComponentWhat it covers in the lecture
SourcePlaces where infectious agents survive as fomites — sinks, hospital equipment, countertops, medical devices
EnvironmentPatient care areas, equipment, devices — ICU, patient wards, operating room
PeoplePatients, healthcare workers, visitors can spread infection

3 causes of HAIs

  • Person-to-person transmission → via direct contact through the hands of healthcare providers, patients, and visitors
  • Environmental contamination → indirect transmission when touching fomites from surfaces, equipment, and devices
  • Airborne transmission → pathogens spread through air via respiratory droplets or droplet nuclei

4 modes of infection transmission

  • Direct contact
  • Indirect contact
  • Droplet
  • Droplet nuclei (airborne)

Chain of infection

MECHANISM — recreated from Figure 7, Page 4 Infectious agents (bacteria, fungi, parasites, prions)
↓
Reservoirs — where the agent lives, grows, and reproduces (people, water, food)
↓
Portals of exit — how the agent leaves the reservoir (blood, secretions, excretions, skin)
↓
Modes of transmission — how the agent travels to the susceptible host
├── Physical / direct contact   ├── Droplets   └── Airborne
↓
Portals of entry — mucous membrane, respiratory system, digestive system, broken skin
↓
Susceptible host — immune deficiency, diabetes, burns, surgery, age
↓
Host becomes a new reservoir → the chain closes into a cycle

Breaking any single link stops transmission. This is the logic behind every prevention measure in Section IV.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Components: source, environment, people.
  • Causes: person to person, environmental contamination, airborne.
  • Modes: direct contact, indirect contact, droplet, droplet nuclei.
  • Chain order: agent → reservoir → portal of exit → mode → portal of entry → susceptible host.

8. Direct and Indirect Contact MUST KNOW Pages 4–5

Core idea: Direct = touching contaminated individuals. Indirect = touching surfaces through fomites. Contact is the mode responsible for the spread of many HAIs, and it is the answer to the lecture's question on the most common form of transmission of pathogens for hospital-acquired infections.

Direct contact

  • Examples: MRSA infection and C. difficile.
  • MRSA is transmitted person to person by skin-to-skin contact when a HCW or visitor touches an infected patient and then touches another patient without proper hand hygiene.
  • Risk factors:
    • Colonization — patients already colonized by MRSA are at high risk of developing infections
    • Invasive procedures — surgery, catheterization, breaks in the skin
    • Prolonged or inappropriate antibiotic use — disrupts normal skin flora and creates more opportunities for MRSA to colonize and cause infection
  • Prevention: hand hygiene, isolation precautions, decontamination.
    • For MRSA specifically: isolate the patient, limit visitors coming in and out, and thoroughly disinfect surfaces and equipment used for patient care.
LECTURE NOTE — HOW DIRTY OUR SKIN IS We shed 106 flat keratinized dead squamous epithelial cells containing bacteria daily. Shed skin cells become fomites that can be transmitted through direct contact.

  • MRSA direct-contact risk factors: colonization, invasive procedures, prolonged or inappropriate antibiotics.

9. Droplet and Airborne Transmission MUST KNOW Pages 5–6

Core idea: The whole distinction is particle size. Droplet is larger than 5 μm and falls within 2 m or 6 ft. Airborne droplet nuclei are smaller than 5 μm, stay suspended, and travel far. Everything else — mask type, room type, distance — follows from that one number.
FeatureDropletAirborne (droplet nuclei)Exam clue
Size>5 μm — large organism<5 μm — small particlesThe single most tested number in this lecture
DistanceShort distance, 2 m or 6 ftGreater distanceFigure 12 puts the boundary at 1 m, airborne runs to 10 m+
Time in airDeposited, fallsRemains suspended for extended periods"Spreads far and stays long" = droplet nuclei
Generated byCoughing, talking, sneezingSame respiratory acts, but the residue stays aloft—
Organisms namedS. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas, influenza, rhinovirusM. tuberculosis, varicella-zoster virus (VZV), measles virusTB, chickenpox, measles are the airborne three
MaskSurgical maskN-95 respirator, door kept closed at all timesN-95 should not be used for personal protection of patients in droplet precautions

Risk factors — identical list for both

  • Crowded environments
  • Immunocompromised patients
  • Outbreaks
  • Invasive procedures (intubation, mechanical ventilation)

Prevention of droplet transmission

  • Vaccination — example given is annual vaccination for influenza
  • Isolation — isolate the infected patient to prevent spread
  • Respiratory hygiene — wear masks, cover nose and mouth when coughing or sneezing
  • Hand hygiene
  • Environmental cleaning
EXAM TRAP Patients with suspected airborne transmission at the ER should be immediately isolated — do not wait for confirmation. This point is flagged in the transcription as coming from a previous trans.
RECALL CUE (study aid, not from the PDF) Airborne = the three childhood-classic "M, T, V" — Measles, Tuberculosis, Varicella-zoster. Anything not on that list in this lecture falls under droplet or contact.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Droplet >5 μm, 2 m or 6 ft, surgical mask.
  • Airborne <5 μm, suspended and far, N-95, door closed.
  • Airborne organisms: TB, VZV, measles. Droplet: S. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas, influenza, rhinovirus.
  • The thing that spreads far and stays long is the infectious droplet nucleus.

Agents actually used in the hospital SHOULD KNOW

  • Povidone iodine (Betadine) — good antimicrobial property

EXAM TRAP You cannot just use any disinfectant on hands. Zonrox and Cide are disinfectants but are NOT hand friendly — they may cause protein degeneration that damages your skin. Compare with Cidex (glutaraldehyde), which is listed as a disinfectant S. aureus is susceptible to, not as a hand agent.

Nail hygiene

Described as one of the most important and most often forgotten measures. It matters because habitual flora lives under the fingernails.

  • Keep nails short — reduces the likelihood of harboring bacteria and other microorganisms
  • Avoid artificial nails — they harbor bacteria and make hand hygiene difficult to perform
  • Clean under nails — wash with soap and water or use alcohol-based hand sanitizer before and after every patient encounter, which drastically decreases infection risk
  • Wear gloves when performing procedures involving contact with bodily fluids or contaminated surfaces
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Handwashing = transient only. Hand antisepsis = transient plus resident.
  • Alcohol-based hand rub is the preferred method at 60–70% alcohol.
  • Triclosan = handwashing. Chlorhexidine gluconate = the OR agent.
  • Zonrox and Cide are not hand friendly.

11. Standard Precaution MUST KNOW Pages 6–7

Core idea: A set of CDC guidelines that applies to ALL PATIENTS, regardless of their diagnosis or suspected infection status, ALL THE TIME. Its key component is to decrease droplet transmission and direct contact transmission.

Key elements

ElementWhat the lecture requires
Hand hygieneThe most important aspect of the standard precaution
Personal protective equipment
(gloves, gown, masks, eye protection)

12. Expanded Precaution and Levels of Protection MUST KNOW Pages 7–8

Core idea: Expanded precaution is standard precaution plus extra measures for a specific transmission route. The three route-specific cards are the highest-yield image in the lecture because several review questions are answered directly from them.

Expanded precaution — the three additional measures

  • Patient isolation — patients may be placed in isolation rooms to prevent the spread of infection
  • Expanded PPEs — healthcare workers may be required to wear a gown, gloves, mask, and eye protection
  • Environmental disinfection — services and equipment in the patient's room disinfected more frequently
PrecautionAnyone entering the room must wearExtra rule
ContactGloves and gownApplies whether or not contact with the patient or the patient's environment is anticipated. Visitors do not need gloves and gown but must wash hands on entering and leaving.
AirborneN-95 respiratorThe door to the patient's room remains closed at all times. Visitors wear a blue surgical mask in the room.
DropletSurgical maskN-95 respirators should NOT be used for personal protection of patients in droplet precautions.

Levels of protection

Depends on the specific type of infection and the risk for exposure.

Basic PPE

  • Gloves — worn through all patient contacts to prevent transmission through touch
  • Gown — worn when there is significant risk of exposure to bodily fluids or secretions
  • Mask — worn to prevent inhalation of respiratory droplets containing infectious agents
  • Eye protection or face shield — protects the eyes from splashes or sprays from bodily fluids

Respiratory protective equipment (RPE)

DeviceProtects againstLevel
Surgical maskLarge particlesLowest
N95 maskAirborne particles smaller than 3 or 5 (units as written in the PDF)Middle
HEPA respiratorAirborne particles including those smaller than 0.3Highest level of protection

Source note: the PDF writes these three thresholds in mm while the transmission section of the same lecture uses μm for particle size. The PDF does not reconcile the two. What is testable is the ranking — surgical mask, then N95, then HEPA as the highest.

Additional protective equipment SHOULD KNOW

  • Tyvek suit — full body protective suit, high level of protection against hazardous materials
  • Shoe covers — prevent spread of germs through foot traffic, used in the OR and ICU
  • Head cover or hair net — protects hair from contamination
  • It is important to follow Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) guideline recommendations of the healthcare facility regarding use of protective gear.
  • During COVID, expanded PPE meant gloves, respirator mask, airtight face shield, and gown.
  • Figure 17 shows an actual IPC memo on updated masking guidelines, noting that even where masking is optional, transmission-based precautions (contact, droplet, airborne) remain in place.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Contact = gloves + gown. Airborne = N-95 + closed door. Droplet = surgical mask.
  • No mask is required by contact precautions.
  • No gown is required by droplet precautions.
  • Protection ranking: surgical mask < N95 < HEPA respirator.
  • Expanded precaution = isolation + expanded PPE + more frequent environmental disinfection.

14. Patient Placement, Isolation Rooms and Equipment MUST KNOW Pages 8–9

Core idea: Isolation is a public health measure that separates infected individuals from others to minimize risk. It splits into single patient isolation and cohorting, and cohorting is only valid when every patient has the same infectious disease.

EXAM TRAP Two false statements the lecture sets up deliberately. An isolation room is NOT under positive pressure — it is negative. And cohorting is NOT pooling together all patients with all kinds of respiratory infections — it requires the same infectious disease.

15. Commonly Confused Concepts MUST KNOW Whole lecture

COMMON CONFUSION 1 Do not confuse: Droplet vs Airborne (droplet nuclei)
  • Droplet — larger, >5 μm, falls within 2 m or 6 ft, blocked by a surgical mask.
  • Airborne — smaller, <5 μm, stays suspended and travels far, needs an N-95 and a closed door.
  • Fast distinction: if the question says it spreads far and stays long, the answer is droplet nuclei.
COMMON CONFUSION 2 Do not confuse: Handwashing vs Hand antisepsis
  • Handwashing — transient bacteria only, non-surgical settings, soap and water with triclosan.
  • Hand antisepsis — transient plus resident bacteria, before surgical procedures, alcohol-based hand rub at 60–70%.
  • Fast distinction: "Handwashing removes transient and habitual normal flora" is FALSE. Only antisepsis reaches the resident flora.
COMMON CONFUSION 3 Do not confuse: Habitual (normal) flora vs Transient flora
  • Habitual — CONS and diphtheroids, living in sweat glands and under fingernails. S. aureus and S. epidermidis are about 90% of normal skin flora.
  • Transient — E. coli, MRSA, Serratia, Enterococci, plus P. aeruginosa in moist areas, sitting on the entire surface of the skin of the hands, picked up by contact with patients and objects.
  • Fast distinction: MRSA is transient, which is exactly why handwashing works against it.
COMMON CONFUSION 4 Do not confuse: Standard precaution vs Expanded precaution
  • Standard — all patients, all the time, regardless of diagnosis. Targets droplet and direct contact.
  • Expanded — route-specific add-ons: isolation, expanded PPE, more frequent environmental disinfection.
  • Fast distinction: standard is who you are treating. Expanded is what they are infected with.
COMMON CONFUSION 5 Do not confuse: Single patient isolation vs Cohorting
  • Single — 1 patient per room, for CDI, unconfined drainage, stool incontinence, suspected measles, varicella, meningitis.
  • Cohorting — multiple patients with the SAME infectious disease, >3 ft apart, curtain drawn, hand hygiene between patients.
  • Fast distinction: same syndrome is not enough. Different strains of the same clinical picture can cross-infect, which is why grouping a meningococcemia patient with "people who have the same condition" is marked wrong.

16. Exam Traps MUST KNOW Pages 9–10 answer keys

Every trap below is drawn from the lecture's own review questions, formative quiz, and their answer keys.

EXAM TRAP — PPE BY ROUTE Masks are not needed in contact precautions. In a neonate with a skin infection such as HSV, hand hygiene, gown, and gloves are applied. The mask is not.
EXAM TRAP — PPE BY ROUTE, REVERSED A hospital gown is not recommended in diseases requiring droplet precautions. For a 5-month-old with meningococcemia, isolation room placement, hand washing, and a surgical mask all apply. The gown does not.
EXAM TRAP — COHORTING Placing a meningococcemia patient with people who have the same condition is inappropriate because of the potential for cross-infection with different strains of pathogens.
EXAM TRAP — C. DIFFICILE ROUTE C. difficile is acquired fecal-orally and by touching infected items, not airborne. Respiratory hygiene is the wrong precaution. Gowns, gloves, handwashing, and disinfecting linens are all right.
EXAM TRAP — MOST COMMON ROUTE The most common form of transmission of pathogens for hospital-acquired infections is contact, not droplet and not airborne. Direct and indirect contact is responsible for the spread of many HAIs.
EXAM TRAP — SPREADS FAR AND STAYS LONG The answer is the infectious droplet nucleus, not a small or large infectious droplet. Justification given: tiny particles that remain suspended in the air for extended periods and can travel over long distances.
EXAM TRAP — KOPLIK SPOTS Koplik spots point to measles, which is airborne. The correct measure is all of the above — N95 or surgical mask, proper hand hygiene, and patient isolation. Do not pick just one.
EXAM TRAP — TRUE OR FALSE SET
  • HAIs are preventable → TRUE, on the basis of advocating early discharge.
  • Handwashing removes transient and habitual normal flora → FALSE, handwashing removes transient bacteria only.
  • Cough etiquette is part of standard precaution → TRUE.
  • An isolation room is set up under positive pressure → FALSE, it is negative pressure.
  • Cohorting pools together all patients with all kinds of respiratory infections → FALSE, it requires the same infectious disease.
EXAM TRAP — WHAT AN HAI IS NOT Not a complication of the primary disease. Not an infection present or incubating on admission. Not an infection contracted by a healthcare worker. Only "develops more than 48 hours after admission" is correct.
EXAM TRAP — PROGNOSIS AND SUSCEPTIBILITY For HAIs, prognosis is poor and antimicrobial susceptibility is NOT common. They are severe and common, not severe and uncommon. The only true statement offered is increased risk of sepsis.
SOURCE ISSUES TO BE AWARE OF SHOULD KNOW

17. Cram Sheet — 5 to 10 minute run-through MUST KNOW Pages 1–10

Read this section on its own if you have ten minutes left. It introduces nothing new.

1. Definitions

  • Patient safety = absence of preventable harm plus reduction of unnecessary risk to an acceptable minimum. Primum non nocere.
  • HAI = nosocomial infection = occurs during the process of receiving healthcare, not present or incubating at the time of admission.

2. The timing rule

48 hours or more after admission  OR  within 10 days after discharge → suspect HAI

3. Numbers to know

  • 1 in 10 admitted patients get an HAI
  • 23.5% increased risk for sepsis
  • Droplet >5 μm, deposited within 2 m or 6 ft. Airborne <5 μm, suspended and far
  • 60–70% alcohol concentration for hand rubs
  • >3 ft physical separation when cohorting
  • S. aureus survival: skin 30 days, coins and phones and floor 7 days, glass 2 days, sunlight 17 hours
  • 106 squamous epithelial cells shed daily
  • About 90% of normal skin flora is S. aureus and S. epidermidis

4. Five HAIs and their lead organisms

  • SSI → S. aureus (MRSA), leading cause. CONS with clean wounds, Bacteroides in deep wounds.
  • CAUTI → E. coli, most common. Risk factor that raises risk is indwelling catheter duration.
  • HAP / VAP → gram-negatives, P. aeruginosa most problematic from innate antibiotic resistance. Entry is through the ET tube or around the cuff. Bacteroides = aspiration.
  • CLABSI → central line is a Type IV catheter in a large vein of the neck, chest, or groin. Gram-positives lead. Related: MRSA bacteremia and endocarditis of the inner lining of heart chambers and valves.
  • CDI → follows antibiotics disrupting gut flora, spore-forming and toxin-secreting, worst symptom pseudomembranous colitis, spread by contact, placed in a single room.

5. Transmission framework

  • Components: source, environment, people.
  • Causes: person to person, environmental contamination, airborne.
  • Modes: direct contact, indirect contact, droplet, droplet nuclei. Contact is the most common.
  • Chain: agent → reservoir → portal of exit → mode → portal of entry → susceptible host.
  • Airborne organisms: M. tuberculosis, VZV, measles. Droplet: S. pneumoniae, Pseudomonas, influenza, rhinovirus.

6. Hand hygiene

  • Handwashing removes transient bacteria only, soap and water, triclosan, non-surgical.
  • Hand antisepsis removes transient plus resident bacteria, alcohol-based rub, before surgical procedures.
  • Chlorhexidine gluconate (Hyclens) is the operating room agent. Zonrox and Cide are not hand friendly.
  • Nail hygiene matters because habitual flora lives under the fingernails. Keep nails short, avoid artificial nails.

7. Precautions

  • Standard precaution applies to all patients, all the time, regardless of diagnosis. Hand hygiene is its most important element. Cough etiquette is part of it.
  • Expanded precaution = isolation + expanded PPE + more frequent environmental disinfection.
  • Contact = gloves + gown. Droplet = surgical mask. Airborne = N-95 with the door closed.
  • Protection ranking: surgical mask < N95 < HEPA respirator (highest).

8. Placement

  • Single room for CDI, unconfined drainage, stool incontinence, suspected measles, varicella, meningitis.
  • Cohorting only for the SAME disease, >3 ft apart, curtain drawn, hand hygiene between patients.
  • Isolation room = NEGATIVE pressure, air flows in, contaminated air cannot escape.

9. Classic traps in one line each

  • No mask in contact precautions.
  • No gown in droplet precautions.
  • Respiratory hygiene is wrong for C. difficile.
  • Handwashing does not remove habitual flora.
  • Isolation rooms are not positive pressure.
  • Cohorting is not "any respiratory infection".
  • Prevention named by the lecture: advocate early discharge.

1. Definition of patient safety in one phrase?

2. Latin phrase for patient safety?
Primum non nocere — first, do no harm.
3. Other name for HAI?
Nosocomial infection.
4. Two timing thresholds for calling an infection an HAI?
48 hours or more after admission, or within 10 days after discharge.

1 in 10.
6. Increased risk for sepsis in HAIs, as a percentage?
23.5%.
7. Prevention measure the lecture names to reduce HAIs?
Advocate early discharge when there is no reason to prolong the stay.
8. Leading and most common cause of surgical site infection?
Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA).
9. Which SSI organism is associated with clean wounds?
Coagulase-negative staphylococci — S. epidermidis.
10. Which SSI organism is implicated in deep wound infections?
Bacteroides species.
11. Most common organism in CAUTI?
E. coli.
12. Which CAUTI risk factor increases risk?
Indwelling / prolonged catheter duration.
13. Most common organism group in HAP and VAP?
Gram-negative bacteria.
14. Which HAP organism is most problematic and why?
P. aeruginosa, because of its innate antibiotic resistance.
15. Organism implicated in aspiration pneumonia?
Bacteroides.
16. Fungal cause of pneumonia in the immunocompromised, hard to treat?
Aspergillus.
17. How does bacteria reach the lungs in VAP?
Through the endotracheal tube, by aspiration directly through the tube or around the cuff.
18. What is a central line, as defined in the lecture?
A Type IV catheter placed into a large vein, typically the neck, chest, or groin, for fluids and medications.
19. What is endocarditis and how is it acquired here?
Infection of the inner lining of heart chambers and valves, from bacteria entering the bloodstream through medical procedures and use of catheters.
20. What kind of bacterium is C. difficile?
Spore-forming, secretes toxins causing severe diarrhea and colitis.
21. Most severe symptom of CDI?
Pseudomembranous colitis, very hard to treat.
22. Three components required for infection spread?
Source, environment, people.
23. First and last links of the chain of infection?
First is the infectious agent, last is the susceptible host.
24. Most common form of transmission for hospital-acquired infections?
Contact — direct and indirect.
25. How long does S. aureus survive on coins, cell phones, and the floor?
Up to 7 days if not disinfected. Only 17 hours in sunlight.
26. Size cut-off separating droplet from airborne?
5 μm. Droplet is >5 μm, airborne droplet nuclei are <5 μm.
27. How far are droplets deposited?
A short distance, 2 m or 6 ft.
28. Three airborne organisms named in the lecture?
M. tuberculosis, varicella-zoster virus, measles virus.
29. Which spreads far and stays long?
The infectious droplet nucleus.
30. Difference between handwashing and hand antisepsis in one line?
Handwashing removes transient bacteria. Hand antisepsis removes transient plus resident bacteria.
31. Preferred hand hygiene agent and its alcohol concentration?
Alcohol-based hand rub at 60–70%.
32. Which agent is used in operating rooms?
Chlorhexidine gluconate (Hyclens).
33. Most important aspect of standard precaution, and who does it apply to?
Hand hygiene. It applies to all patients, regardless of diagnosis or suspected infection status, all the time.
34. PPE required for contact, droplet, and airborne precautions?
Contact = gloves and gown. Droplet = surgical mask. Airborne = N-95 respirator with the door kept closed.
35. Which protective device offers the highest level of protection?
The HEPA respirator, above N95, above the surgical mask.
36. Pressure type of a proper isolation room, and the rule for cohorting?
Negative pressure, so contaminated air cannot escape. Cohorting requires the same infectious disease and >3 ft separation.
