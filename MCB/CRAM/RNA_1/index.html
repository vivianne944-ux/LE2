If You Only Remember These

  • Must know Picornaviruses are naked, icosahedral, nonsegmented +ssRNA. Their genome can act directly as mRNA. p. 1–2
  • Must know Picornavirus replication is in the cytoplasm: +RNA → polyprotein → protease cleavage → −RNA template → new +RNA. p. 2
  • Must know Poliovirus targets anterior horn lower motor neurons → asymmetric flaccid paralysis with no sensory deficit. p. 3–4
  • Must know Polio does not multiply in muscle. Muscle atrophy is secondary to denervation. p. 3
  • Must know Nonparalytic polio = aseptic meningitis. Paralytic polio = destruction of anterior horn motor neurons. p. 4
  • Must know Salk IPV prevents CNS disease but does not create strong gut immunity. Sabin OPV gives mucosal IgA and interrupts transmission but can rarely cause vaccine-associated paralysis. p. 5
  • Must know Coxsackie A = Above the neck clues like herpangina and HFMD. Coxsackie B = Below the neck/body organs like pleurodynia and myocarditis. p. 6–7
  • Must know HAV is not directly cytopathic. Liver injury is mainly immune-mediated by CD8+ T cells and NK cells. p. 8
  • Must know Acute HAV: IgM anti-HAV. Prior infection or vaccination: IgG anti-HAV when IgM is negative. p. 8
  • Must know Rhinovirus likes the cooler upper airway: 33°C, acid labile, common cold, highest rates in children. p. 1, 9–10
  • Must know Parechovirus = former Echovirus 22/23. Neonate + high fever + irritability + diffuse red rash + sepsis-like illness = “Red, Angry Baby.” p. 10
  • Must know Reoviridae are naked, segmented dsRNA. Rotavirus has 11 segments and a triple-layered capsid. p. 10–11
  • Must know Rotavirus infects small-intestinal villus cells. NSP4 acts as a viral enterotoxin and inhibits SGLT1 → secretory diarrhea plus malabsorption. p. 11
  • Must know Rotavirus VP6 is the group-specific antigen detected by ELISA. VP4 = P type. VP7 = G type. p. 11
  • Must know Norovirus = sudden vomiting + watery diarrhea + outbreaks in contained settings. RT-PCR is gold standard. p. 12
  • Must know Caliciviruses are naked, nonsegmented +ssRNA with cup-shaped capsid depressions and major capsid protein VP1. p. 12

1. Picornavirus Foundationsp. 1–2
Genome+ssRNA, nonsegmented, ~7–8 kb
EnvelopeNaked
CapsidIcosahedral, VP1–VP4
ReplicationCytoplasm, rapid 5–10 h

Core structure

  • Capsid shell has 60 subunits, each built from four proteins VP1–VP4.
  • The genome is directly infectious because positive-sense RNA can be translated immediately.
  • 5′ end carries VPg, used as a primer or starting point for RNA synthesis.
  • 3′ end has a poly-A tail that contributes to RNA stability.
  • VP1, VP2 and VP3 form a surface “canyon.” The receptor-binding site lies deep within it, helping shield it from antibodies.

Major human groups

Enteroviruses
Gut + throat, 37°C, acid stable. Includes poliovirus, Coxsackie viruses, echoviruses and EV71.
Rhinoviruses
Nose + throat, best at 33°C, acid labile. Taxonomically within Enterovirus, still separated clinically/traditionally.
Hepatovirus
Hepatitis A virus, major replication in liver after intestinal entry.
Parechovirus
Former Echovirus 22/23, distinct group.
Receptor = tropism = organ = syndromeExamples in the lecture: PVR for poliovirus, CAR for Coxsackie B and DAF for echoviruses.
Why can a +ssRNA picornavirus begin protein production immediately after uncoating?
Answer: Its positive-sense RNA genome can function directly as messenger RNA and can be translated by host ribosomes.
What is the purpose of the temporary negative-sense RNA made during replication?
Answer: It is the template from which the viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase makes many new positive-sense genomes.
2. Enterovirus Frameworkp. 3

Physical behavior

  • Stable over a wide pH range, approximately pH 3–10, so it can survive gastric conditions.
  • No lipid envelope → resistant to ether, chloroform and alcohol.
  • Inactivated by radiation, formaldehyde and phenol.
  • MgCl₂ reduces thermolability, a point linked in the lecture to vaccine stability.

Pathogenesis pattern

RNA evolution mattersHigh replication error rate leads to mutation and genetic drift. If different strains coinfect the same cell, recombination can occur when the polymerase jumps between RNA templates.
ImmunitySerotype-specific neutralizing IgG can persist for life. IgM appears early. IgA protects mucosa. Maternal antibodies give early infant protection.
Where do enteroviruses initially replicate before systemic spread?
Answer: In the pharynx and distal small bowel, especially Peyer patches, before spread through lymph nodes and viremia.
3. Poliovirusp. 3–6
ReservoirHumans only
ReceptorPVR / CD155
Main targetAnterior horn LMNs
Incubation7–14 days
Classic neurologic pictureAsymmetric flaccid paralysis, hypotonia and reduced or absent reflexes from lower motor neuron destruction. Sensory function is spared.

Clinical spectrum

≈95%: no illness
Subclinical or asymptomatic infection.
Minor illness
Nonspecific fever, sore throat, headache, malaise, GI symptoms with full recovery.
Major illness
Nonparalytic aseptic meningitis or paralytic myelitis.

Nonparalytic myelitis

  • Meningeal invasion without major anterior horn destruction.
  • Nuchal rigidity, headache and possible mental status changes.
  • Usually complete recovery in about 2–10 days.

Paralytic / bulbar disease

  • Asymmetric weakness and flaccid paralysis, often lower limbs.
  • Bulbar myelitis involves medulla and can impair breathing, swallowing and airway protection.
  • Lecture highlights phrenic and intercostal motor pathways plus CN IX, X and XII.
  • No sensory deficits.
Post-polio syndrome is NOT reactivationDecades later, new weakness, pain, atrophy and fatigue reflect late failure of compensatory motor-neuron sprouts after the original injury.

Diagnosis

ItemHigh-yield point
Best specimen timingThroat swab early. Stool gives the longest yield. Virus is rarely recovered from CSF.
RT-PCRRapid, sensitive and specific.
Cell cultureObserve cytopathic effect, then neutralization with specific antiserum can confirm.
CSFViral meningitis pattern: lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose, normal or increased protein.
MRICan show anterior horn involvement.

Salk vs Sabin

Lecture epidemiology snapshotThe lecture notes the Philippines was declared polio-free in 2000, followed by a circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus outbreak in 2019, with no new confirmed cases since 2020 in the lecture’s summary.
Polio patient has fever and nuchal rigidity but no weakness. What syndrome is this?
Answer: Nonparalytic poliomyelitis presenting as aseptic meningitis, which usually resolves without permanent paralysis.
Why can an IPV-vaccinated person still shed poliovirus in stool?
Answer: IPV produces systemic humoral protection against CNS disease but does not generate the same strong local intestinal immunity as oral live vaccine.
4. Coxsackieviruses A & Bp. 6–7
Fast divider: A = Above, B = BelowCoxsackie A points you toward oral, pharyngeal, eye and skin findings. Coxsackie B points you toward chest, heart and severe systemic organ disease.

Coxsackie A

  • Herpangina: abrupt fever + sore throat + vesicles/ulcers on posterior palate, pharynx, tonsils or tongue.
  • HFMD: oral ulcers + vesicular rash on palms and soles. CVA16 is common.
  • Acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis: Coxsackie A24 variant. Lecture notes Enterovirus 70 as the classic cause.

Coxsackie B

  • Pleurodynia / Bornholm disease: abrupt fever + stabbing chest or abdominal pain.
  • Acute myocarditis/pericarditis: major viral heart-disease association in the lecture, may be fatal in neonates.
  • Generalized neonatal disease: simultaneous involvement of heart, liver and brain.
Herpangina vs strep pharyngitisHerpangina gives discrete vesicles and ulcers. The lecture contrasts this with streptococcal exudative tonsillitis, which gives white/yellow tonsillar exudate rather than ulcers.
HFMD vesiclesThey heal without crusting, unlike the vesicular lesions emphasized for herpesviruses and poxviruses.

Transmission and shedding: fecal–oral and respiratory routes, direct or indirect contact with infected fluids. Virus may be in blood early, throat for days and stool for 5–6 weeks.

Diagnosis: collect from the involved site. The lecture calls RT-PCR the method of choice. Cell culture and type-specific serology are alternatives.

Child with fever, sore throat and vesicles on posterior pharynx. Coxsackie A or B?
Answer: Coxsackie A, classically herpangina.
Neonate with myocarditis and systemic heart-liver-brain disease. Which Coxsackie group is the stronger clue?
Answer: Coxsackie B.
5. Hepatitis A Virusp. 7–8
GenusHepatovirus
Genome+ssRNA, naked
TransmissionFecal–oral
ChronicityNo chronic infection
Main mechanism of liver injuryHAV is not directly cytopathic. Hepatocyte injury appears as the adaptive immune response develops. CD8+ T cells and NK cells lyse infected cells, while antibodies mainly limit spread.

Clinical clues

  • Incubation approximately 4 weeks.
  • Fever, malaise, nausea, vomiting and jaundice.
  • Loss of appetite and weight loss.
  • Dark urine can be the first sign that prompts consultation. Pale or clay-colored stool can occur.
  • Children under 5 are often asymptomatic. Symptomatic infection and jaundice become more likely with age.
  • No chronic infection and no cirrhosis from HAV.

Exposure clues

  • Raw or undercooked shellfish and contaminated food or water.
  • Outbreak settings include daycares, camps, schools, military settings and nursing homes.
  • Risk groups in the lecture include travelers, MSM, drug users, clotting-factor disorders and chronic liver disease.

Serology + diagnosis

FindingInterpretationTiming / note
IgM anti-HAVAcute infectionAppears early and lasts about 3–6 months.
IgG anti-HAV with IgM negativePrior infection or vaccinationLong-term immunity marker.
HAV in stoolDirect detection windowAbout 2 weeks before through 2 weeks after jaundice.
ELISATest of choice in lectureUsed for anti-HAV testing.
AST / ALT elevationLiver injuryNonspecific.

Complications

Cholestatic
Fever, pruritus, prolonged jaundice.
Relapsing HAV
Improvement followed by a usually milder relapse.
Fulminant hepatitis
Very rare severe liver failure with encephalopathy and coagulopathy.

Prevention: inactivated HAV vaccine, hygiene and safe food/water. Immune globulin can be used for post-exposure prophylaxis within 2 weeks. The lecture notes HAV vaccine is recommended from age 1 year but is not part of the Philippine National Immunization Program in its lecture snapshot.

Treatment: supportive only. No specific antiviral therapy. Rare fulminant cases may require liver transplantation.

Patient returns from a shellfish exposure with jaundice, IgM anti-HAV positive. What actually damages the liver?
Answer: Immune-mediated killing of HAV-infected hepatocytes, especially by CD8+ T cells and NK cells, rather than direct viral cytolysis.
IgG anti-HAV positive, IgM anti-HAV negative. What does this suggest?
Answer: Prior infection or vaccination rather than acute HAV.
6. Echovirus · Rhinovirus · Parechovirusp. 8–10

Echovirus

ECHO historically means Enteric Cytopathogenic Human Orphan.

Picornaviridae, Enterovirus genus, +ssRNA.

Clinical associations in the lecture include aseptic meningitis, encephalitis/febrile illness, infantile diarrhea and acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis.

The summary table lists throat swab, stool and CSF specimens, with PCR as a key diagnostic method.

Rhinovirus

33°C + acid labile + nasal mucosa = common cold.

Highest rates in children, who often introduce infection into families.

Transmission is mainly direct contact, hand-to-eye inoculation and contaminated objects. Self-inoculation is emphasized over airborne spread.

No vaccine because of many serotypes and short-lived type-specific protection. No specific antiviral.

PCR gives specific diagnosis but is rarely needed for routine management.

Parechovirus

Former Echovirus 22 and 23. Types 1 and 2 correspond to those former classifications.

Divergent from enteroviruses with less than 30% protein sequence identity in the lecture.

Capsid has 3 proteins because VP0 is not cleaved.

Red, Angry Baby: high fever + irritability + diffuse red rash + sepsis-like presentation.

Can cause meningitis and neonatal sepsis-like illness.

Routine enterovirus PCR can miss it if primers are not specific for parechovirus.

Exam phrase recognition“Former Echovirus 22/23” is practically a direct arrow to Parechovirus.
Why can routine enterovirus PCR be negative in a neonate with parechovirus?
Answer: PCR requires primers that match the target. Routine enterovirus primers may not amplify parechovirus.
7. Reoviridae & Rotavirusp. 10–12
GenomeSegmented dsRNA
EnvelopeNaked
Rotavirus11 segments
CapsidTriple-layered
Segmented genome → reassortmentCoinfection can mix whole genome segments and create new hybrids. The lecture links this to genetic shift and increased diversity.

Segment counts: Rotavirus 11, Orthoreovirus and Orbivirus 10, Coltivirus 12. The virion core contains enzymes for transcription and RNA capping.

Subfamilies: Spinareovirinae has a spiked inner capsid and includes Orthoreovirus. Sedoreovirinae has a smooth inner capsid and includes Rotavirus.

Rotavirus antigens

ProteinWhat to remember
VP4Spike/attachment protein. Defines P type. Neutralizing, type-specific antigen.
VP7Outer capsid antigen. Defines G type. Neutralizing, type-specific antigen.
VP6Group-specific antigen. Detected by ELISA and IEM.
VP1Polymerase involved in transcription and genome replication.
NSP4Viral enterotoxin linked to secretory diarrhea.

Clinical

  • Major cause of diarrheal illness in infants and young children.
  • Incubation 1–3 days.
  • Watery diarrhea, vomiting, fever, abdominal pain → dehydration.
  • Severe/prolonged in immunodeficient children. Often mild or asymptomatic in adults.
  • Group B rotavirus is linked to adult outbreaks.

Diagnosis

  • Stool sample early in illness.
  • EIA/ELISA detects VP6 antigen.
  • PCR is most sensitive and specific and enables genotyping, but is not routinely done in the lecture because of cost.
  • Rapid immunochromatographic lateral-flow tests are point-of-care antigen tests.

Prevention

Treatment is supportive rehydration and electrolyte replacement. Vaccines are the best control strategy in the lecture.

VaccineHigh-yield description
RotaTeqLive attenuated, oral, pentavalent human-bovine reassortant.
RotarixLive attenuated, oral, monovalent human vaccine.
RotaShieldOlder rhesus-based vaccine withdrawn because of intussusception risk.
ROTA mnemonic from lectureR = RNA, Reoviridae, dsRNA segmented. O = oral-fecal spread + oral vaccine. T = toxin NSP4 + tiny people. A = acute diarrhea in infants.
Which rotavirus protein is the group-specific antigen detected by ELISA?
Answer: VP6.
Why is oral rehydration still mechanistically sensible if rotavirus inhibits SGLT1 in the lecture?
Answer: The lecture’s treatment recommendation remains rehydration and electrolyte replacement. For exam purposes, separate the NSP4/SGLT1 pathogenesis fact from the supportive treatment fact rather than overextending the mechanism.
8. Caliciviruses: Norovirus & Sapovirusp. 12–13, 16
Genome+ssRNA, nonsegmented
EnvelopeNaked
CapsidIcosahedral, cup-like
Major proteinVP1
Calyx = cupCaliciviruses show distinctive cup-shaped capsid depressions under electron microscopy.

Norovirus

  • Prototype: Norwalk virus.
  • Incubation 24–48 h. Illness is short and self-limited.
  • Sudden-onset vomiting, watery diarrhea, low-grade fever, cramps and malaise.
  • Dehydration is the major complication, especially in young and elderly patients.
  • Highly infectious, as few as about 10 particles in the lecture.
  • Outbreak settings: cruise ships, nursing homes, schools and disaster shelters.
  • Transmission also includes food, water, fomites, person-to-person spread and aerosolized vomitus.
  • RT-PCR = gold standard.
  • Supportive fluids and electrolytes. No vaccine in the lecture.

Sapovirus

  • Sapporo-like viruses.
  • Caliciviridae, +ssRNA, naked, nonsegmented.
  • Associated with childhood gastroenteritis and diarrheal illness in infants and young children. The lecture summary also includes elderly adults.
  • Fecal–oral transmission.
  • Stool and vomitus are listed specimens in the summary.
  • RT-PCR is the most used technique for detecting human caliciviruses in the lecture summary.
NORO mnemonic from lectureN nausea/vomiting, O outbreaks, R rapid onset and recovery, O oral-fecal transmission.
Sudden vomiting outbreak on a cruise ship. Most likely virus and best test?
Answer: Norovirus. RT-PCR is the gold-standard diagnostic test in the lecture.

Commonly confused

Do Not Mix These Up

Polio nonparalytic vs paralytic: meninges only → nuchal rigidity and recovery. Anterior horn LMN destruction → flaccid paralysis.
Salk vs Sabin: Salk = injected inactivated systemic protection. Sabin = oral live, gut IgA + community transmission interruption, rare reversion risk.
Coxsackie A vs B: A = herpangina/HFMD/eye. B = pleurodynia/heart/neonatal organs.
Herpangina vs strep: herpangina = vesicles/ulcers. Strep clue in lecture = tonsillar exudate.
HAV IgM vs IgG: IgM = acute. IgG without IgM = prior infection or vaccination.
Rotavirus vs Norovirus: Rotavirus = infants, 11-segment dsRNA, VP6, NSP4. Norovirus = explosive outbreaks, +ssRNA, RT-PCR.
Exam traps

Trap Radar

“Flaccid paralysis” does not mean muscle infection. In polio, the primary lesion is the lower motor neuron.
“Aseptic meningitis” does not automatically mean paralysis. Nonparalytic polio can invade meninges without destroying anterior horn neurons.
HAV injury is not direct cytolysis. Pick immune-mediated hepatocyte destruction when that option appears.
Positive-sense does not mean every RNA virus has the same replication shortcut. Here, the direct-mRNA rule is being used specifically for the +ssRNA viruses discussed.
Rotavirus briefly buds into ER but ends mature and non-enveloped. The temporary membrane is lost during maturation.
Parechovirus can be missed by routine enterovirus PCR. “Former ECHO22/23” is the clue that you need a specific primer set.
