1 Picornaviruses — Overview, Structure, Replication Pages 1–2

Core idea

Pico = small, RnA = RNA virus. Picornaviruses are the smallest RNA viruses — tiny virions with simple genomes, but a very diverse family. The lecturer stresses that small size does not mean mild disease, these viruses can produce terrible symptoms.

MECHANISMReplication cycle Table 3 + Figure 8, Page 2

Occurs entirely in the cytoplasm. Total cycle time is rapid, 5–10 hours.

StepWhat happens
1. AttachmentVirus binds host cell receptors, and the receptor determines tropism — PVR (poliovirus), CAR (coxsackievirus B), DAF (echoviruses)

LECTURER'S POINT — receptors decide the disease Different viruses need different receptors to attach. The target receptor determines the tropism, tropism determines which organ is affected, and that in turn determines the clinical symptoms of that particular disease.

MUST KNOWPositive sense vs negative sense Figures 9–10, Page 2

POSITIVE SENSE (picornaviruses)
  • Genome is already a messenger RNA
  • Translated as soon as it enters the cell
  • Genome is therefore directly infectious on its own
NEGATIVE SENSE
  • Needs RNA-dependent RNA polymerase first
  • Must be converted to positive sense RNA before translation
  • Extra step required, so the genome alone is not directly infectious

SHOULD KNOWClinical spectrum of picornaviruses Page 1

  • Myelitis is the most serious enterovirus disease.
  • Also aseptic meningitis, myocarditis, pleurodynia, conjunctivitis, skin and mucosal lesions, febrile illness, respiratory infections.
  • Subclinical (asymptomatic) infections are much more common than overt disease.
  • The same virus can cause multiple syndromes, and different viruses can cause similar syndromes. Overlapping symptoms make diagnosis on clinical grounds alone difficult, so additional diagnostic tests are needed.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY — PICORNAVIRUSES
  • Smallest RNA viruses, family Picornaviridae with 12 genera, human genera are Enterovirus, Hepatovirus, Parechovirus.
  • Naked, icosahedral, 60 subunits of VP1–VP4, genome is +ssRNA, non-segmented, ~7–8 kb, directly infectious.
  • VPg at 5′ end primes RNA synthesis, poly-A at 3′ end gives stability.
  • Canyon formed by VP1+VP2+VP3 hides the receptor-binding site and is too narrow for antibodies.
  • Replication is cytoplasmic and fast (5–10 hours), single polyprotein cleaved by viral proteases, release by cell lysis.
  • Receptor determines tropism determines clinical syndrome — PVR, CAR, DAF.
  • Enterovirus = acid stable, 37°C. Rhinovirus = acid labile, 33°C.

  • pH 3–10 stable → survives stomach. No envelope → resists ether, chloroform, alcohol.

MECHANISMPathogenesis, step by step Table 4, Page 3

StepDescription
1. EntryVirus enters via the mouth → multiplies in the oropharynx (tonsils) and the intestines (Peyer's patches). Acquired by eating food or drinking water contaminated with feces from an infected person.
2. SpreadBlood → CNS invasion, which can happen via blood or via axons of peripheral nerves → spinal cord → brain. Virus multiplies in nerve cells and damages them by cytolysis.

3. Target (neurotropism)Motor neurons in the spinal cord and brainstem → flaccid paralysis
4. ExcretionVirus shed in stool for weeks, the major source of spread. A person who is no longer symptomatic can still carry virus in stool and contaminate food and water.

Paralytic myelitis Page 4

Myelitis means "inflammation of the gray matter of the spinal cord". It occurs in only a small percentage of cases, but as the lecturer notes, a small percentage of a very large number is still a large absolute number, which is why polio remains dangerous without protection.

  • Asymmetric flaccid paralysis, commonly the lower limbs — indicative of lower motor neuron damage from viral replication in and destruction of the LMNs in the anterior horn.
  • Residual deficits are common.
  • Incoordination → indicates brainstem involvement.
  • Painful spasms of non-paralyzed muscles.
  • NO sensory deficits.
  • Most recover within 6 months, but others are left with permanent paralysis.
RESPIRATORY PARALYSIS = BULBAR MYELITIS Infection of the medulla oblongata destroys motor nerves that keep breathing and the airway safe:
  • Phrenic nerve → diaphragm
  • Intercostal nerves → intercostal muscles
  • CN 9, 10, 12 → swallowing and airway protection
Historically managed with the iron lung, a negative-pressure ventilator invented in the 1920s–30s that rhythmically changes air pressure to force air into the lungs.

MUST KNOWPost-myelitis syndrome (PPS) Figure 17, Page 4

SHOULD KNOWImmunity Page 5

  • Type-specific lifelong immunity, mostly humoral neutralizing antibodies.
  • Cross-protection is limited — being infected by one type still leaves you open to the other types.
  • Maternal antibody protects infants up to 6 months.
  • Virus already in the brain is not affected by high antibody levels in blood, therefore immunization is only of value if it precedes the onset of nervous system symptoms.

EXAM TRAP — problems specific to the ORAL vaccine Page 5
  • Oral vaccine interference — immunity may be blocked if the child is simultaneously infected with another enterovirus at the time of vaccination.
  • In an immunocompromised person the vaccine virus may activate and become active.
  • Mutation → a rare cause of paralytic myelitis, one vaccine-associated case for every 2 million persons vaccinated.
  • Continued use of OPV may lead to re-emergence of the virus.

SHOULD KNOWEpidemiology Page 5

  • Before vaccines, 21,000 paralytic cases per year in the US.
  • Transmission — fecal-oral, pharyngeal secretions, contaminated water.
  • Affects all age groups, but children are usually more susceptible than adults because adults have acquired immunity.
  • Year-round in the tropics, summer and fall in temperate regions.
  • Risk rises with poor sanitation and crowding.
  • WHO campaign since 1988 dropped cases from 350,000 to under 2,000 per year.
  • Polio-free regions declared — Americas 1994, Western Pacific 2000, Europe 2002.
  • Wild virus type 2 eradicated, last case 1999.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY — POLIOVIRUS
  • 3 types, Type 1 causes most paralysis, receptor is PVR / CD155, humans are the only reservoir.
  • Kills anterior horn lower motor neurons → asymmetric flaccid paralysis, hypotonia, hyporeflexia, NO sensory loss.
  • Does not multiply in muscle — atrophy is from denervation.
  • 95% no illness, ~4–5% minor abortive illness, 1% major illness. Incubation 7–14 days.
  • Bulbar myelitis hits the medulla → phrenic, intercostal, CN 9, 10, 12 → respiratory paralysis.
  • PPS in 20–30%, 25–35 years later, NOT reactivation but failure of compensatory axonal sprouts.
  • Best specimen is stool (longest yield), best method is RT-PCR, CSF shows lymphocytic pleocytosis, normal glucose.
  • Salk/IPV = injected, humoral only, no gut immunity. Sabin/OPV = oral, gut IgA, spreads immunity, rare vaccine-associated paralysis (1 in 2 million).
  • No antivirals, supportive care only, quarantine useless because most infections are silent.

MUST KNOWCoxsackie A: Herpangina Page 6

  • Severe febrile pharyngitis with abrupt onset of fever and sore throat.
  • Discrete vesicles and ulcers on the posterior half of the palate, pharynx, tonsils or tongue.
COMMON CONFUSION — herpangina vs streptococcal pharyngitis The PDF's stated difference is that streptococcal pharyngitis shows exudative tonsillitis, meaning white or yellow pus on the tonsils, NOT an ulcer. Herpangina gives discrete vesicles and ulcers.

MUST KNOWCoxsackie A: Hand-foot-and-mouth disease Pages 6–7

  • Coxsackie A Type 16 (CVA16) is the common cause, but other strains can also cause it.
  • Rash of hands, feet and mouth with oral and pharyngeal ulcerations.
  • Vesicular rash of the palms and soles that may spread to arms and legs, sitting on an erythematous base.
  • Vesicles heal WITHOUT crusting, unlike herpesviruses and poxviruses.
  • Occurs worldwide, usually affects children (common in schools and daycares), but adults may also be affected.
  • Prodrome 24–48 hours before the main symptoms — fever, sore throat, malaise.
  • Rash lasts 5–10 days.

MUST KNOWCoxsackie A: Acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis Page 7

  • Highly contagious.
  • Virus infects the conjunctival epithelium → subconjunctival hemorrhage, edema and intense inflammation.
  • Self-limited, 5–7 days.

MUST KNOWCoxsackie B: Pleurodynia (Bornholm disease) Page 7

  • Sometimes preceded by malaise, headache and anorexia.
  • Lasts 2 days to 2 weeks.
  • Self-limited with complete recovery, but it may relapse.

  • A = Above the neck (Triple H: HFMD, Herpangina, Acute Hemorrhagic conjunctivitis). B = Below the neck (pleurodynia, myocarditis/pericarditis, neonatal systemic disease).

  • Aseptic meningitis occurs in ALL type B and many type A.
  • RT-PCR is the method of choice, cell culture CPE takes 5–14 days. No vaccine, no antiviral.

EXAM TRAP — one serotype, three genotypes Three genotypes but only one serotype. This is why a single vaccine and a single past infection give lifelong protection, unlike rhinovirus with its 150+ serotypes.

MUST KNOWTransmission and epidemiology Page 8

  • Fecal-oral route — contaminated food, water and close contact.
  • Outbreaks in daycares, camps, schools, military and nursing homes.
  • Major sources — raw or undercooked shellfish and contaminated food or water.
  • Large epidemics, for example Shanghai 1988 with 300,000 cases from clams.
  • NO chronic carrier state, unlike the other hepatitis viruses.
  • Risk groups — travelers, MSM, drug users, people with clotting factor disorders, chronic liver disease.
WHY CLOTTING FACTOR DISORDERS ARE A RISK GROUP Those patients use plasma-derived clotting factor concentrates. HAV is stable in these products and is not routinely screened for in blood products, so the virus can slip through.

MUST KNOW — the liver injury is IMMUNE-MEDIATED, not viral cytopathic effect
  • HAV is NOT directly cytopathic — it does not usually kill hepatocytes by itself.
  • The immune response, not direct viral damage, is the main cause of liver injury.
  • Most hepatocytes show HAV antigen at peak replication, yet there is no widespread necrosis.
  • Liver damage only appears as the adaptive immune responses develop.
  • CD8+ T cells are cytotoxic against HAV-infected cells, NK cells can lyse them, and antibodies mainly limit viral spread rather than drive pathology.

MUST KNOWClinical features Page 8

  • Incubation approximately 4 weeks, described as uncertain because the early symptoms are vague.
  • Acute inflammatory condition of the liver.
  • Non-specific symptoms — fever, malaise, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, plus loss of appetite and weight loss is common.
  • Dark urine is usually the first sign that makes patients seek consult, caused by build-up of bilirubin.
  • Pale or clay-colored feces.
  • Usually by the third week most patients feel better.
  • NO chronic infection and NO cirrhosis.
AGE RULE — older means sicker The proportion of symptomatic infections increases with age, and the probability of jaundice increases with age. Children under 5 years are often asymptomatic, while adults are more symptomatic. Rare complications carry a higher risk in chronic liver disease.

MUST KNOWGeneral features of Reoviridae Page 10

  • Medium-sized, dsRNA, segmented genome with 10–12 segments.
  • Non-enveloped, icosahedral capsid.
  • Includes Rotavirus, Orthoreovirus, Coltivirus, Orbivirus.
WHY DOUBLE STRANDED MATTERS
  • Has both negative and positive strands of RNA
  • Requires a special polymerase to transcribe mRNA — it cannot be read directly like a picornavirus
WHY SEGMENTED MATTERS
  • Genome is split into multiple separate RNA molecules
  • Reassortment — segments of two different strains can mix during co-infection, producing new hybrids
  • This results in GENETIC SHIFT and drives genetic diversity
EXAM TRAP — shift vs drift Segmented genome (rotavirus) → reassortment → GENETIC SHIFT. High polymerase error rate (enterovirus) → point mutation → GENETIC DRIFT. Picornaviruses are non-segmented, so they cannot reassort — they recombine by polymerase template-jumping instead.

COMMON CONFUSIONDo not confuse these pairs

ENTEROVIRUS
  • Acid STABLE, survives the stomach
  • Replicates at 37°C
  • Gut and throat → systemic disease
RHINOVIRUS
  • Acid LABILE, dies below pH 5–6
  • Replicates best at 33°C
  • Nose and throat only → URTI

Fast distinction: acid stable goes down and spreads, acid labile stays up in the nose.

HERPANGINA
  • Discrete vesicles and ULCERS
  • Posterior palate, pharynx, tonsils, tongue
  • Coxsackie A
STREPTOCOCCAL PHARYNGITIS
  • Exudative tonsillitis
  • White or yellow PUS on tonsils, NOT ulcers

Fast distinction: ulcers mean herpangina, pus means strep.

NONPARALYTIC MYELITIS
  • Invades the meninges
  • NO significant destruction of anterior horn neurons
  • Nuchal rigidity, headache, mental status change
  • Recovery in 2–10 days
PARALYTIC MYELITIS
  • Destroys anterior horn LMNs
  • Asymmetric flaccid paralysis, usually lower limbs
  • Residual deficits common
  • Most recover in 6 months, some are permanent

13 Exam Traps Whole lecture
TRAP 1 — Poliovirus does NOT infect muscle The virus does not multiply in muscle. Muscle wasting is secondary to denervation after the lower motor neurons die. A stem offering "viral replication within skeletal muscle causing myocyte necrosis" is wrong.
TRAP 2 — Sensation is NORMAL in paralytic polio The target is motor neurons in the anterior horn and brainstem. No sensory deficits. If a stem describes sensory loss, it is not this disease.
TRAP 3 — Post-myelitis syndrome is NOT viral reactivation It is the late effect of the original motor neuron damage plus the ageing body's failure to support compensatory axonal sprouts, appearing 25–35 years later in 20–30% of paralytic cases.
TRAP 4 — Hepatitis A liver injury is IMMUNE-mediated HAV is not directly cytopathic. CD8+ T cells and NK cells do the damage, and antibodies mainly limit spread. Hepatocytes carry viral antigen at peak replication without widespread necrosis.
TRAP 5 — Three genotypes but ONE serotype for HAV Genotypes I, II and III infect humans, but there is only one serotype, which is why infection or vaccination gives lifelong immunity. Do not swap these two words.
TRAP 6 — Rhinovirus antibodies appear AFTER recovery Neutralizing antibodies develop 7–21 days after infection, which is after the person has already recovered, so recovery is not antibody dependent. Antibody may, however, accomplish final clearance.
TRAP 7 — Rain and wet clothes do not cause colds The PDF is explicit that being drenched does not directly produce a cold or increase susceptibility. The association comes from staying indoors in close contact.
TRAP 8 — Rotavirus transient ER membrane ≠ envelope Immature particles bud into the ER and temporarily acquire a membrane, then lose it at maturation. The mature virion is naked.
TRAP 9 — Rotavirus spares the stomach and colon It infects the villus cells of the small intestine. Choices naming the stomach or the colon are wrong.
TRAP 10 — Segmented vs non-segmented decides reassortment Only rotavirus in this lecture is segmented, so only rotavirus reassorts (genetic shift). Picornaviruses are non-segmented and instead recombine when the polymerase jumps templates, and they drift via their ~1 error per genome replication.
TRAP 11 — Acid stability, not the envelope, explains stomach survival Picornaviruses are non-enveloped, and being naked is why they resist ether, chloroform and alcohol. What lets enteroviruses survive gastric acid is stability across pH 3–10. VPg is a primer for RNA synthesis, and the 3′ poly-A tail gives RNA stability — neither confers acid resistance.
TRAP 12 — The oral polio vaccine is the one with problems OPV carries vaccine-associated paralysis (1 per 2 million), interference from a concurrent enterovirus infection, activation in the immunocompromised, and cVDPV re-emergence. IPV's weakness is different — it does not block gut infection or immunize contacts.
TRAP 13 — Quarantine does not work for polio Because 90–95% of infections are asymptomatic, the people spreading it are not identifiable, so quarantine is ineffective. Vaccination is the only effective prevention.
TRAP 14 — Norovirus survives cooking and chlorine It survives mild chlorine, heating to 60°C and steamed oysters, and needs only about 10 particles to infect. "It was steamed so it was safe" is a wrong assumption.
TRAP 15 — Echovirus and infantile diarrhea is an UNPROVEN association The PDF explicitly words it as "association possible but unproven". Do not upgrade it to a definite cause.
TRAP 16 — Mouse paralysis patterns do not apply to humans Coxsackie A = flaccid, Coxsackie B = spastic — in MICE. The PDF states symptoms are different in humans.
TRAP 17 — RotaShield was withdrawn, not simply replaced The 1998 rhesus-based RotaShield was withdrawn because of intussusception risk. The current vaccines RotaTeq and Rotarix are both safe and effective.
TRAP 18 — Vaccinating late is useless in polio CNS disease Virus already in the brain is not affected by high antibody levels in blood, so immunization only helps if it precedes the onset of nervous system symptoms.

15 Rapid-Fire Final Recall Whole lecture

Answer out loud first, then open. 36 prompts covering the whole lesson.

What does "Picorna" mean and what is the genome type?
Pico = small, RnA = RNA virus. Genome is single stranded, positive sense, non-segmented RNA (~7–8 kb) and it is directly infectious.
How many subunits and proteins make up the picornavirus capsid?
60 subunits, each with four proteins VP1–VP4, arranged with icosahedral symmetry.
What are the roles of VPg and the poly-A tail?
VPg at the 5′ end is the primer or starting point needed for RNA synthesis. The poly-A tail at the 3′ end gives RNA stability.
What is the "canyon" and what is its functional advantage?
A surface depression formed at the junction of VP1, VP2 and VP3. The receptor-binding site sits deep inside it, and the canyon is too narrow to permit antibody penetration, so the binding site is shielded.
Where does picornavirus replication occur and how long does one cycle take?
Entirely in the cytoplasm, taking a rapid 5–10 hours. Release is by host cell lysis.
Name the receptor used by poliovirus, coxsackievirus B, echoviruses and rhinovirus major group.
PVR (CD155) for poliovirus, CAR for coxsackievirus B, DAF for echoviruses, ICAM-1 for the rhinovirus major group (minor group uses the LDLR family).
Why can rhinovirus not cause gut disease?
It is acid labile, inactivated below pH 5–6, so it cannot survive the stomach, and it grows best at 33°C, the nasopharynx temperature, with limited replication beyond that.
What property lets enteroviruses survive the stomach?
Stability across a wide pH range of 3–10. Not the envelope (they are naked), not VPg, not the poly-A tail.
What chemicals do naked picornaviruses resist, and what inactivates them?
They resist ether, chloroform and alcohol because there is no lipid envelope. They are inactivated by radiation, formaldehyde and phenol.
What does MgCl₂ do for enterovirus vaccines?
It reduces thermolability, which allows the production of vaccines that do not need refrigeration.
Give the enterovirus pathogenesis chain in order.
Fecal-oral or respiratory entry → pharynx and distal small bowel (Peyer's patches) → lymph nodes → minor viremia → major viremia → organ dissemination to CNS, heart and skin.
How many poliovirus antigenic types, and which causes most paralysis?
Three types (1, 2 and 3), and Type 1 most commonly causes paralysis.
Does poliovirus multiply in muscle?
No. Muscle damage is secondary to nerve damage — motor neurons die, muscle fibres lose innervation, and denervated muscles atrophy.
Breakdown of poliovirus clinical spectrum by percentage?
95% no illness. Minor (abortive) illness ~4% of all myelitis cases and 90–95% of symptomatic cases, recovering in a few days. Major illness 1%, splitting into nonparalytic and paralytic myelitis.
What is bulbar myelitis and which nerves does it destroy?
Infection of the medulla oblongata causing respiratory paralysis, by destroying the phrenic nerve (diaphragm), the intercostal nerves, and CN 9, 10 and 12 (swallowing and airway protection).
What causes post-myelitis syndrome and when does it appear?
NOT reactivation. It is the late effect of the original motor neuron damage — surviving neurons sprout new axonal branches, and the ageing body cannot support these unstable sprouts. Appears 25–35 years after the acute illness in 20–30% of paralytic cases.
Best specimen and best method for poliovirus diagnosis?
Stool has the longest yield (throat swab early, CSF rarely positive). RT-PCR is rapid, sensitive and specific. Neutralization with specific antiserum is confirmatory for cell culture.
What are the CSF findings in polio?
Those of aseptic viral meningitis — increased WBCs (pleocytosis), lymphocyte predominant (normal is 0–5 WBCs/µL), protein increased or normal, and glucose normal.
Which polio vaccine gives gut immunity, and what is its main risk?
OPV (Sabin, live attenuated, oral) gives IgG, IgM and gut IgA and interrupts transmission. Its risk is vaccine-associated paralysis, one case per 2 million persons vaccinated, plus cVDPV re-emergence.
Why is quarantine ineffective for polio?
Because 90–95% of infected people are asymptomatic, so the spreaders cannot be identified. Vaccination is the only effective prevention, and there are no antiviral drugs.
What triggered the 2019 Philippine polio outbreak?
Circulating vaccine-derived poliovirus (cVDPV), arising because low immunization rates allowed the vaccine virus to mutate in unvaccinated children. Cases in Mindanao, NCR and parts of Luzon, with no new confirmed cases since 2020.
Coxsackie A "Triple H" — name all three.
Hand-foot-and-mouth disease, Herpangina, Acute Hemorrhagic conjunctivitis.
How do you tell herpangina from streptococcal pharyngitis?
Herpangina gives discrete vesicles and ulcers on the posterior half of the palate, pharynx, tonsils or tongue. Streptococcal pharyngitis gives exudative tonsillitis — white or yellow pus, not ulcers.
Which strain classically causes HFMD, and how do the vesicles heal?
Coxsackie A Type 16 (CVA16), though other strains also cause it. Vesicles heal without crusting, unlike herpesviruses and poxviruses. Rash lasts 5–10 days after a 24–48 hour prodrome.
Which virus causes acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis, and how long does it last?
Coxsackievirus A24 variant, but classically Enterovirus 70. It is highly contagious, infects the conjunctival epithelium, and is self-limited over 5–7 days.
Most common viral cause of myocarditis, and by which receptor does it enter?
Coxsackievirus Type B, entering myocytes via the transmembrane coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor (CAR). It may be fatal in neonates and may cause permanent heart damage.
What is the main mechanism of liver injury in hepatitis A?
Immune-mediated. HAV is not directly cytopathic. CD8+ T cells are cytotoxic against infected cells and NK cells lyse them, while antibodies mainly limit viral spread. Damage appears only as adaptive immunity develops.
How many HAV genotypes and serotypes infect humans, and what follows from that?
Three genotypes (I, II, III) but only ONE serotype, which is why infection produces lifelong immunity.
Which serology confirms acute hepatitis A, and for how long is it positive?
IgM anti-HAV, which appears early and lasts 3–6 months. IgG anti-HAV with a negative IgM means prior infection or vaccination. ELISA is the test of choice.
What does ECHO stand for?
Enteric Cytopathogenic Human Orphan — first isolated from stool, caused cytopathic effects in cell culture, isolated from humans, and originally linked to no known disease.
Enterovirus 70 vs Enterovirus 71 — what does each cause?
EV70 → acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis, pandemics 1969–1971, resolving in about 10 days. EV71 → major cause of CNS disease (meningitis, encephalitis, polio-like paralysis) and severe or fatal HFMD outbreaks.
Why is parechovirus missed by routine enterovirus PCR?
PCR requires primers that flank the target template. Routine enterovirus primers do not match parechovirus, so the template is not amplified. It needs PCR with specific primers.
What is the "Red, Angry Baby Virus" and its four features?
Parechovirus. High fever, irritability, diffuse reddish rash, sepsis-like presentation in a neonate.
Why can rotavirus undergo genetic shift but picornaviruses cannot?
Rotavirus has a segmented dsRNA genome (11 segments), so segments of two strains can reassort during co-infection, producing new hybrids — that is genetic shift. Picornaviruses are non-segmented and instead show genetic drift from a ~1 error per genome replication rate, plus recombination when the polymerase jumps templates.
Which rotavirus protein is the enterotoxin and exactly what does it do?
NSP4. It inhibits Sodium-Glucose Transporter 1 (SGLT1), so sodium and glucose stay in the gut lumen and drag water out, causing secretory diarrhea. Alongside this, villus destruction with replacement by immature crypt cells causes malabsorptive diarrhea.
Which rotavirus antigen is detected by EIA in stool, and what do VP4 and VP7 define?
VP6, the group-specific antigen, detected by ELISA and IEM. VP7 defines the G type and VP4 defines the P type, and both are neutralizing, type-specific antigens.
Which rotavirus vaccine was withdrawn and why, and what replaced it?
RotaShield, the 1998 rhesus-based vaccine, withdrawn for intussusception risk. Current vaccines are RotaTeq (live attenuated, oral pentavalent, human-bovine reassortant) and Rotarix (live attenuated, oral monovalent, human), both safe and effective.
What is the infectious dose of norovirus and where does it typically strike?
As few as 10 particles. Classic settings are cruise ships, nursing homes, schools and disaster shelters, and it survives mild chlorine, 60°C heating and steamed oysters.
Norovirus incubation, duration and hallmark symptom?
Incubation 24–48 hours, duration 12–60 hours and self-limited, with sudden-onset vomiting as the hallmark alongside watery diarrhea, low-grade fever, cramps and malaise. Shedding may last up to 1 month.
Give the gastroenteritis virus for infants, for children and for adults.
Rotavirus = infantile gastroenteritis (daycare, winter). Sapovirus = childhood gastroenteritis (Sapporo-like). Norovirus = epidemic viral gastroenteritis in adults (outbreak settings).
Why are caliciviruses named that, and what is the capsid made of?
From calyx = cup, because of the distinctive cup-shaped depressions seen on the capsid under electron microscopy. The capsid is made of a single major structural protein, VP1, and the virion is 27–40 nm, naked, non-segmented +ssRNA.
Which viruses in this lecture have no vaccine and no antiviral?
Coxsackievirus, echovirus, rhinovirus and norovirus. Poliovirus and hepatitis A have vaccines but no antiviral, and rotavirus has RotaTeq and Rotarix. Intranasal interferon-α for rhinovirus is experimental only.
