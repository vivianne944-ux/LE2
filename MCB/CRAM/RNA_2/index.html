1. Review: Classification of Viruses

Pages 1–2
Core idea: Viruses are sorted three ways — by genetic material (DNA or RNA, single or double stranded), by structure (capsid symmetry icosahedral / helical / complex, and enveloped or naked), and by the Baltimore system. On top of formal taxonomy there is an informal epidemiologic grouping based on tropism and transmission.

The two "in nature" rules that generate most exam questions

DNA viruses
  • In nature DNA is double stranded, so all DNA viruses are ds EXCEPT Parvovirus (Baltimore II).
  • ALL single stranded DNA viruses are naked (non-enveloped).
  • dsDNA viruses may be either naked or enveloped.
  • No positive or negative sense classification exists for DNA viruses.
RNA viruses
  • In nature RNA is single stranded, so all RNA viruses are ss EXCEPT Reoviridae (Baltimore III).
  • RNA viruses do have positive or negative sense classification.
  • Further split into segmented vs non-segmented and enveloped vs non-enveloped.
  • Segmented genome → genetic reassortment is easy → increased mutant or variant strains.
Lecturer's shortcut for placing an RNA family (Page 2):
  • Enteric (fecal-oral) = the Group IV non-enveloped families Picornaviridae*, Caliciviridae, Hepeviridae, Astroviridae plus Group III non-enveloped Reoviridae.
  • Respiratory = Matonaviridae, Coronaviridae, Orthomyxoviridae, Paramyxoviridae.
  • The rest = oncogenic viruses or arboviruses.
  • *Some Picornaviridae are not enteric, for example Rhinoviruses.
SHOULD KNOW

Groupings on the Basis of Epidemiologic Criteria

These are informal, practical categories in common usage, distinct from the formal universal taxonomic system and formal nomenclature. They are based on virus tropism and modes of transmission.

Most human pathogens are transmitted by: ingestion · inhalation · injection (including arthropod bites) · close contact (including sexual contact) · congenitally.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Baltimore = genome type + replication method, non-phylogenetic, comprehensive.
  • All DNA viruses are ds except Parvovirus. All ssDNA viruses are naked.
  • All RNA viruses are ss except Reoviridae.

2. Togaviridae / Matonaviridae — Rubella Virus

Pages 2–4
Core idea: Rubella is a single stranded, positive sense, non-segmented, enveloped RNA virus. Postnatal rubella is a mild 3-day rash illness, but maternal infection in the first trimester is teratogenic and produces congenital rubella syndrome.
MUST KNOW

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Rubella = sole member of Rubivirus, one serotype, not arthropod borne despite Togavirus morphology.
  • Positive sense → genome acts directly as mRNA → infectious.
  • Postnatal: incubation ~12 days, viremia at 7–9 days until antibody on day 13–15, rash face → trunk → limbs, lasts ≤3 days. 20–50% subclinical.
  • Rash is immunologically based because antibody appearance coincides with it.
  • IgM gone by 6 weeks, IgG for life. Maternal antibody protects infant 4–6 months.
  • CRS triad = heart + eye + hearing. Worst damage in the 1st trimester, rare after week 20.
  • CRS diagnosis = infant IgM (maternal IgM cannot cross the placenta).
  • Vaccine = live attenuated, one dose → >95% immunity.

Clinical, pathological and serological manifestations in COVID-19

Timing and symptoms
  • Incubation period 1–14 days, mostly 3–7 days
  • Common symptoms at onset: fever, dry cough, myalgia, fatigue, dyspnea, anorexia
  • A few patients present initially with atypical symptoms — diarrhea and nausea
Disease course
  • Most patients have mild and moderate symptoms with good treatment results
  • Elderly patients and those with chronic diseases usually have poor prognoses
  • Severe and critically ill patients progress rapidly to acute respiratory failure, ARDS, metabolic acidosis, coagulopathy, septic shock and multiple organ failure
Autopsy findings (The Lancet Respiratory Medicine)
  • Typical ARDS-like lung injury with lymphocyte infiltration
  • Liver injury with moderate micro-vesicular steatosis
Laboratory pattern — know which cell line goes which way.
  • COVID-19 patients usually show significantly reduced lymphocyte count (lymphocytopenia) and increased inflammatory factors, especially in severe illness.
  • Patients who died showed increased neutrophil count and D-dimer (protein fragments produced during DIC), with a decreasing lymphocyte count until death.
  • Neutrophilia may be related to cytokine storms induced by virus invasion, and coagulation activation may be related to sustained inflammatory responses.

Laboratory diagnosis

1. Nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)
  • RT-qPCR remains the foundational NAAT for SARS-CoV-2 detection
  • Typically targets multiple viral genes, for example ORFab, N and E
2. Antigen detection methods
  • For rapid diagnosis, as a complementary diagnostic
  • Several rapid antigen tests (RADs) have been evaluated on saliva and other sample types
  • These are immunochromatographic assays, not NAATs, and are rapid screening tools rather than standalone replacements for NAATs

Detection of SARS-CoV-2 by RT-PCR

1. Nasopharyngeal swab collected→ 2–3. Transported to viral transport medium for RNA extraction→ 4. Reverse transcription of viral RNA into cDNA and amplification in a thermocycler→ Read fluorescence vs copies per reaction curve — positive results cross the detection threshold
Why reverse transcription? PCR amplifies DNA, but SARS-CoV-2 has an RNA genome. The viral RNA must first be extracted and reverse transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA), which can then be amplified by PCR.

Lateral flow test

  • Test line: mouse anti-nucleocapsid protein antibody conjugated to 150 nm gold particles (anti-AuNP) detects anti-NP.
  • Control line: chicken IgY antibodies conjugated to 40 nm gold particles (cIgY-AuNP) detect goat anti-chicken IgY.
  • The red control line (C) must be present for the test to be valid — it monitors proper liquid flow and confirms the bio-reagents are active.
  • A visible blue test line (T) = positive for SARS-CoV-2. Absence of the blue test line = negative or below the detection limit.
  • The cassette can also be read using a cassette reader for quantitative evaluation.
Management and treatment. The lecture does not list a drug regimen. The lecturer's framing is that for every process or step in the pathogenesis of the disease, there is a therapy or drug — the cytokine storm figure labels potential therapeutic targets such as an IL-1 blocker (anakinra or canakinumab), colchicine and blood purification therapy. Genetic and antigenic stability

  • The nonsegmented genome negates the possibility of gene segment reshuffling (genetic reassortment), the process that is so important to the natural history of influenza viruses [Jawetz].
  • The HN/H/G and F surface proteins exhibit minimal antigenic variation over long periods of time.
  • Nearly all the amino acids in the primary structures of paramyxovirus glycoproteins may be involved in structural or functional roles, leaving little opportunity for substitutions that would not markedly diminish the viability of the virus.

5. Human Parainfluenza Virus

Page 8
Core idea: Replication is limited to the respiratory epithelia in an immunocompetent host, and viremia, if it occurs at all, is uncommon. The disease produced depends entirely on how far down the airway the virus gets, which differs by type.

Memory cue (study aid, not from the PDF): 1 and 2 sit UP high → croup in toddlers. 3 goes DOWN deep → pneumonia and bronchiolitis in tiny infants. The younger the infant, the deeper the disease.
SHOULD KNOW

Immunity — why reinfection keeps happening

  • Types 1, 2 and 3 are distinct serotypes that lack significant cross-neutralization. Developing antibodies against type 1 will not protect you from infection by type 2.
  • Virtually all infants have maternal antibodies in serum, yet these antibodies do not prevent infection or disease.
  • Reinfection of older children and adults occurs even in the presence of antibodies from an earlier infection. Antibodies modify the course of disease, and reinfections usually present as nonfebrile upper respiratory infections (colds).
  • Secretory IgA is most important for protection against reinfection, but it disappears within a few months — which is why reinfections are common even in adults.
  • Serum antibodies target HN and F surface proteins, and their relative roles in resistance are unknown.
  • The antibody response becomes less specific with successive reinfections, because of shared antigens among parainfluenza and mumps viruses. This makes it difficult to diagnose the specific paramyxovirus using serologic assays.

Diagnosis and epidemiology

Diagnosis
  • Nucleic acid amplification — RT-PCR using nasopharyngeal washes
  • Antigen detection — rapid diagnosis via direct or indirect immunofluorescence on exfoliated nasopharyngeal cells
  • Serology — paired sera measured by neutralization, hemagglutination-inhibition (HI) or ELISA
  • Paired sera compares acute stage serum (first few days) with convalescent serum (during recovery)
  • Lecturer: a four-fold increase in antibody titer = NEUTRALIZATION
Epidemiology
  • Type 3 is the most prevalent — infection usually acquired within the 1st year, antibodies by age 2
  • Types 1 and 2 are less prevalent — infection acquired by age 5
  • Reinfection throughout childhood and adulthood, usually mild URTI
  • Transmission: direct person-to-person contact or large respiratory droplets
  • Common in school children and hospitals
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Replication limited to respiratory epithelia, viremia uncommon, and transmission is strictly person-to-person (so it is not zoonotic).
  • Types 1 and 2 → croup, 6–18 months. Type 3 → pneumonia or bronchiolitis, under 6 months. Type 4 → mild.
  • Otitis media is the most common complication.
  • Types 1, 2, 3 do not cross-neutralize, and secretory IgA wanes in months, so reinfection is the rule.
  • Type 3 is the most prevalent and is acquired earliest.

Why mumps is so hard to contain (three reasons given in the lecture):
  • Variable incubation period
  • Presence of virus in saliva before symptoms — shedding runs from 3 days before to 9 days after the onset of salivary gland swelling
  • Large number of asymptomatic but infectious cases — 1/3 have inapparent infections but can still shed the virus

Clinical findings and symptoms

Clinical findings
  • 1/3 are subclinical, and it is subclinical in the majority of children less than 2 years old
  • Swelling of one parotid gland and other salivary glands, with pain
  • CNS involvement in 10–30%
  • Can cause aseptic meningitis, which can spontaneously resolve
  • Mumps after puberty: testes and ovaries may be affected
Symptoms
  • Mild pain in the front of the neck (salivary glands) or right in front of the ears (parotid glands)
  • Either gland may become swollen and sore
  • Trouble chewing
  • Testicle pain and tenderness
  • Fever, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, loss of appetite
Orchitis — why it hurts so much. Among males mumps may cause orchitis, which is unilateral, with abundant inflammatory cell infiltration resulting in marked swelling. The pain is due to swelling of the inflamed testes confined inside the tunica albuginea — a non-expandable capsule, so pressure rises sharply.

Immunity

  • Immunity is permanent after a single infection, because there is only 1 antigenic type and it does not exhibit antigenic variation.
  • Subclinical infections can generate lifelong immunity.
  • Passive immunity is transferred from mother to offspring, so mumps is rare in infants younger than 6 months old.

11. Orthomyxoviridae — Influenza Virus

Pages 13–19, 22
Core idea: Influenza is single stranded, negative sense, SEGMENTED and enveloped. The segmented genome is the single fact that explains reassortment, antigenic shift, pandemics and the need for a yearly vaccine. Ortho = correct, true group of mucus-binding viruses. Myxa = mucus.
MUST KNOW

Why a segmented genome matters

Co-infection of one cell by two different strains
↓
Segments are exchanged among the different viral strains
↓
Reassortment creates progeny containing genes derived from more than one parent, potentially conferring important fitness advantages or disadvantages
↓
New viral variants, frequently after reassortment between human and avian or swine strains, with the ability to cause global pandemics
Reassortment is not automatic. For segmented RNA viruses that package multiple genome segments into a single virion, reassortment also requires genetic compatibility between parental strains, which takes the form of conserved packaging signals and the maintenance of RNA and protein interactions.

Structure

  • Virions are usually spherical, and it is an enveloped virus whose outer layer is a lipid membrane taken from the host cell.
  • Inserted into the lipid membrane are "spikes" about 10 nm long — the glycoproteins HA (hemagglutinin) and NA (neuraminidase). NA is the target of the antiviral drug Tamiflu.
  • HA and NA determine the antigenic variation of influenza viruses and host immunity, and the subtypes and strains (for example A/H1N1).
  • Protection against re-infection is mainly due to antibodies to HA, although antibodies to NA are also protective.

Hemagglutinin vs Neuraminidase

Hemagglutinin (HA) — entry
  • Binds virus particles to susceptible cells
  • Major antigen against which neutralizing (protective) antibodies are directed
  • Variability in HA is primarily responsible for the continual evolution of new strains and subsequent influenza epidemics
  • Named for its ability to agglutinate erythrocytes under certain conditions
  • Cleavage of HA into HA1 and HA2 is necessary for the virus to be infectious, and is mediated by cellular proteases
  • The amino terminal of HA2 generated by cleavage is necessary for the viral envelope to fuse with the cell membrane
Neuraminidase (NA) — exit
  • Functions at the END of the viral replication cycle
  • A sialidase enzyme that removes sialic acid from glycoconjugates
  • Facilitates the release of virus particles from infected cell surfaces during budding
  • Helps prevent self-aggregation of virions by removing sialic acid residues from viral glycoproteins
  • May help the virus negotiate through the mucin layer in the respiratory tract to reach target epithelial cells
  • Target of Tamiflu
EXAM TRAPWhy influenza stays in the respiratory tract: the protease enzymes that cleave HA are expressed only at those sites. No cleavage elsewhere means no infectious virus elsewhere. This is the same logic as paramyxovirus F0 cleavage — the host protease, not the virus, decides tissue range.

MECHANISMThe three routes to antigenic shift

1. Direct transmission and adaptation
  • Direct transmission of avian influenza A, especially a highly pathogenic strain, from birds to humans, then adaptation of the virus in humans
  • Adaptation occurs by changing the receptor-binding site of HA from α2,3-linked sialic acid receptors in avian cells into α2,6-linked sialic acid receptors in human cells, by mutation
  • Result: a novel human strain capable of transmitting among the population and causing a pandemic
  • Example: the "Spanish flu" in 1918/1919, thought to have been transmitted by direct contact from avian to human beings, based on genetic material isolated from victims
2. Genetic reassortment
  • A virus previously adapted to another host, such as birds or humans, co-infects a pig along with a common swine-adapted strain
  • Cross-linking between two different influenza A strains in a single host cell mixes the RNA segments during reassembly of new virus particles
  • A third viral strain arises containing the combined genes of both strains, with an HA and/or NA antigenically different from those previously circulating
  • Pigs and humans possess both human and avian receptors, so they can be infected by both strains at the same time — the "mixing vessel"
  • Examples: "Asian flu" 1957/1958, "Hong Kong flu" 1968/1969, Pandemic H1N1 2009–2010. The lecture diagram portrays reassortment between human seasonal H1N1 and swine H3N2
3. Re-emergence of old strains
  • Once the population's immunity to a specific strain becomes weakened, that virus can re-emerge, causing a pandemic outbreak
  • Example: the Russian flu in 1950 and 1977 was found to have probably been caused by the re-emergence of the same H1N1 virus subtype

Clinical findings

01 Uncomplicated influenza
  • Symptoms of classic influenza usually appear abruptly
  • Chills, headache, dry cough, high fever lasting approximately 3–5 days, generalized muscular aches, malaise, anorexia
  • Mild or asymptomatic infections may also occur
02 Pneumonia
  • Serious complications usually occur only in elderly adults and debilitated individuals, especially with underlying chronic disease
  • Pneumonia may be viral, secondary bacterial, or a combination of both
  • Increased mucus secretion helps carry infectious agents to the lower respiratory tract, attributed to loss of ciliary clearance, dysfunction of phagocytic cells, and provision of a bacterial growth medium by the alveolar exudate
  • Common secondary bacterial pathogens: Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae
03 Reye's syndrome
  • An acute encephalopathy in children and adolescents, usually between 2 and 16 years of age
  • A rare disorder that affects all organs of the body but is most harmful to the brain and liver
  • Causes an acute increase in pressure within the brain
  • Often causes massive accumulation of fat in the liver and other organs

Immune response

01 Innate immune response
  • Cellular arm: macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, dendritic cells
  • NK cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes critical for elimination of influenza virus
  • The initial phase of infection activates alveolar macrophages and monocytes
  • Alveolar macrophages limit viral spread by phagocyte-mediated opsonophagocytosis of influenza virus
02 Humoral immune response
  • Produces antibodies against different influenza antigens
  • HA-specific antibody is the most important for virus neutralization, preventing illness by binding to the trimeric globular head of HA and inhibiting virus attachment to host cells
  • HA-specific antibodies can also bind to infected Fc receptor-expressing cells to facilitate phagocytosis of virus particles
  • The main entrance for influenza is the mucosal tissues, where IgA and to some extent IgM act as the main antibodies, neutralizing mucosal pathogens and preventing pathogen entry and virus replication

Immunity

  • The three types of influenza viruses are antigenically unrelated and therefore induce no cross-protection.
  • When a viral type undergoes antigenic drift, a person with preexisting antibody to the original strain may have only mild infection with the new strain.
  • "Original antigenic sin" — subsequent infections or immunizations reinforce the antibody response to the FIRST subtype of influenza experienced years earlier.
  • The primary role of cell mediated immune responses is clearance of an established infection, with cytotoxic T cells lysing infected cells.
  • The CTL response is cross reactive, able to lyse cells infected with any subtype of virus, and appears directed against both internal proteins (NP, M) and the surface glycoproteins.
CONTRASTAntibody responses are subtype-specific and drift-sensitive. The cytotoxic T-cell response is cross reactive across subtypes. That is why vaccines chase HA every year while T cells give broader but non-sterilizing protection.

  • Hendra: bats → HORSES → humans, and that is the only human route described. Onset 9–16 days. Seven human cases ever, 57% mortality. Australia only.
Fast distinction: N for Nipah, N for "nanay ng baboy" — pigs. H for Hendra, H for horses. (Study aid, not from the PDF.)
DO NOT CONFUSEhMPV vs RSV
  • Symptoms and sex distribution are identical — you cannot separate them clinically.
  • Peak age: MPV 6–12 months, RSV under 6 months.
  • Season: MPV February–April, RSV November–January.
  • Daycare is a listed risk factor only for RSV.
Fast distinction: RSV hits younger and earlier in the season. MPV hits older and later.
DO NOT CONFUSENatural reservoir host vs Intermediate host (coronaviruses)
  • Natural reservoir for SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV and SARS-CoV-2 is the SAME — the bat.
  • Intermediate host differs: civet cats (SARS), dromedary camel (MERS), pangolins (COVID-19).
Fast distinction: the question word decides the answer. "Reservoir" is always bats. "Intermediate" is the species-specific one.
DO NOT CONFUSEInfant IgM vs IgG in congenital rubella
  • Infant IgM = diagnostic of congenital rubella, because maternal IgM cannot cross the placenta, so it must have been made in utero.
  • Infant IgG proves nothing early on, because maternal IgG does cross and is lost over about 6 months.
  • In postnatal rubella the marker of lifelong immunity is IgG, since IgM does not persist beyond 6 weeks.
Fast distinction: IgM answers "is this a NEW infection in this person?" IgG answers "is this person immune?"
DO NOT CONFUSEHA vs NA function
  • HA works at the START — attachment, fusion after HA1/HA2 cleavage, and the main neutralizing antibody target.
  • NA works at the END — release from the cell surface, prevention of self-aggregation, penetration of mucin. Tamiflu target.
Fast distinction: HA gets you in, NA gets you out.
DO NOT CONFUSEMost common vs most common life-threatening complication of measles
  • Most common overall = otitis media.
  • Most common life-threatening = pneumonia from secondary bacterial infection.
  • Giant cell pneumonia is the one specific to deficient cell-mediated immunity.
DO NOT CONFUSEF0 cleavage (paramyxovirus) vs HA cleavage (influenza)
  • Both are host protease events required for infectivity, and both are performed by cellular enzymes, not viral ones.
  • Paramyxovirus: F0 → F1, fusion at neutral pH at the cell surface, so endosomes are bypassed and syncytia form.
  • Influenza: HA → HA1 + HA2, and the amino terminal of HA2 drives fusion after receptor-mediated endocytosis.
Fast distinction: paramyxovirus fuses at the door, influenza fuses inside the endosome.

14. Exam Traps

Whole lecture
EXCEPTIONThe two "in nature" exceptions run in opposite directions. DNA is normally double stranded, so the exception is the single stranded one — Parvovirus. RNA is normally single stranded, so the exception is the double stranded one — Reoviridae. Getting these backwards is the single easiest point to lose.
EXCEPTIONReplication site reversals. RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm EXCEPT influenza (nucleus). DNA viruses replicate in the nucleus EXCEPT Poxvirus (cytoplasmic factories). Both exceptions are commonly swapped in distractors.
SIMILAR NAMESHepatitis B is the only DNA hepatitis virus. Everything else in the hepatitis group is RNA. Also note the families are completely unrelated — grouping them together is purely about the liver being the target organ.
TIMINGRubella rash timing traps. Antibody appears on day 13–15, coinciding with the rash, which is why the rash is considered immunologically based. Serum IgG becomes detectable by day four after rash onset. Do not mix the two timelines — one is about viremia ending, the other about the IgG assay turning positive.
NOT IN THE TRIADMicrocephaly is NOT part of the classic congenital rubella triad, even though it is listed under CNS abnormalities and under the general associations. The triad is strictly congenital heart disease, ocular defects, hearing loss.
ORGAN DIFFERENCEParotid involvement is NOT obligatory in mumps. A mumps question describing orchitis, aseptic meningitis or pancreatitis with no parotid swelling is still mumps.
REVERSED CAUSE AND EFFECTThe measles rash is immune-mediated, not directly cytopathic. It results from interaction of sensitized lymphocytes with specific antibodies and complement against viral antigens on endothelial cells. The same logic applies to the rubella rash, whose appearance coincides with antibody development.
ZOONOSISHuman parainfluenza virus is NOT zoonotic. It is transmitted strictly person-to-person by direct contact or large respiratory droplets. Nipah, Hendra and MERS-CoV are the zoonoses in this lecture.
OPPOSITE EFFECTSCOVID-19 blood picture. The general finding is lymphocytopenia, but the finding specifically associated with severe and fatal cases is neutrophilia with raised D-dimer. A question asking about severe COVID wants neutrophilia, not lymphopenia.
TYPE SELECTIVITYInfluenza A and C cannot reassort with each other. Antigenic shift requires two strains of the same type, typically two influenza A strains such as a human and an avian or swine strain. The three types have no cross-reactive internal proteins, so no reassortment between types.
CONSEQUENCE, NOT MECHANISMAntigenic drift consequence. The correct consequence is that existing antibodies fail to recognize and neutralize the newer virus because of point mutations in key viral epitopes. Drift does not produce a new subtype — that is shift.
HOST CELL OUTCOMEInfluenza causes CELL DEATH, not persistent infection, latency or transformation. Cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells actively kill infected cells, and the virus destroys the superficial mucosa (sparing the basal layer).
SIMILAR ROUTESRaw date palm sap is Nipah, not Hendra. Hendra reaches humans only from horses.
CELL TROPISMRSV targets ciliated cells of the large airways — not mucus-secreting cells, not laryngeal epithelium, not alveolar cells. Goblet cells increase later, but through infection of basal cells that then differentiate, which is a downstream effect rather than the initial tropism.
ALL OF THE ABOVENeuraminidase questions are usually "all of the above." NA removes sialic acid from glycoconjugates, facilitates release of virions from infected cell surfaces, and helps the virus negotiate the mucin layer. All three are stated in the lecture.
DOSE AND SCHEDULEVaccine numbers worth keeping exact. Rubella: live attenuated, single dose, more than 95% long-lasting immunity, given in the Philippines at the 9th month because maternal antibodies persist until the 6th month. Influenza: children from 6 months of age and yearly thereafter. MMR is live attenuated for all three components, never a killed virus, toxoid or immunoglobulin.
SEROLOGY THRESHOLDA fourfold rise in paired sera is the shared answer for parainfluenza, mumps, measles and influenza serology. The lecturer specifically notes that a four-fold increase in antibody titer = NEUTRALIZATION in the parainfluenza section. Also remember that for mumps, detection of antibody alone is not adequate to diagnose infection.

No cleavage = not infectious.

16. Rapid-Fire Final Recall

Answer out loud first, then open. 47 prompts covering the whole lecture.

1. What two things does the Baltimore system classify viruses by?
Type of genome and method of replication. It is non-phylogenetic, and its advantage is being comprehensive enough that every known virus can be assigned a group.
2. Which DNA virus is the single stranded exception, and what envelope rule follows?
Parvovirus, Baltimore group II. All single stranded DNA viruses are naked or non-enveloped.
3. Which RNA family is the double stranded exception?
Reoviridae, Baltimore group III.
4. What does a segmented genome allow that a non-segmented one does not?
Genetic reassortment, which leads to increased mutant or variant strains of that virus.
5. Which hepatitis virus is not an RNA virus, and what family is it in?
Hepatitis B, which is a DNA virus in the family Hepadnaviridae.
6. What must an arbovirus always do before it can be transmitted onward?
Replicate in the arthropod vector. The vertebrate then develops viremia of sufficient magnitude to infect other blood-feeding arthropods, which perpetuates the cycle.
7. Why is rubella no longer in Togaviridae, and what is it now?
Genus Rubivirus now belongs to a separate family, Matonaviridae. Rubella is its sole member. It keeps Togavirus morphology and physicochemical properties but is not transmitted by arthropods.
8. Why is the naked rubella genome considered infectious?
It is positive sense, so the RNA genome can function immediately as mRNA and be translated into proteins without a transcription step first.
9. What is the evidence in the lecture that the rubella rash is immunologically based?
The development of antibody coincides with the appearance of the rash, on day 13 to 15.
10. What proportion of postnatal rubella infections are subclinical?
20 to 50 percent of cases.
11. Name the classic triad of congenital rubella syndrome.
Congenital heart disease, ocular defects, and hearing loss. Failure to thrive, developmental delay and microcephaly are associated but not part of the triad.
12. What single finding is diagnostic of congenital rubella, and why?
Rubella-specific IgM in the infant. Maternal IgM does not cross the placenta, so its presence in infant blood means the infant synthesized it in utero.
13. When does the Pediatric Society of the Philippines recommend rubella vaccination, and why then?
At the 9th month, because maternal antibodies persist until the 6th month.
14. Match each coronavirus to its receptor: 229E, SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV.
229E uses aminopeptidase N. SARS-CoV uses angiotensin converting enzyme 2. MERS-CoV uses dipeptidyl peptidase 4, also called CD26.
15. Which coronavirus structural protein determines the shape of the viral envelope?
The membrane protein M, which is also the central organiser of coronavirus assembly.
16. Which RNA virus replicates in the nucleus, and which DNA virus replicates in the cytoplasm?
Influenza virus replicates in the nucleus. Poxvirus replicates in cytoplasmic factories.
17. In severe or fatal COVID-19, what happens to neutrophils, lymphocytes and D-dimer?
Neutrophil count increases and D-dimer increases, while the lymphocyte count keeps decreasing until death. Neutrophilia may be related to the cytokine storm, and coagulation activation to sustained inflammatory responses.
18. What are the natural reservoir and the intermediate host of SARS-CoV-2?
Natural reservoir is the bat. Intermediate host is the pangolin. For comparison, civet cats are intermediate for SARS-CoV and the dromedary camel for MERS-CoV.
19. Why must reverse transcription precede PCR for SARS-CoV-2?
PCR amplifies DNA, but SARS-CoV-2 has an RNA genome, so the viral RNA must be extracted and reverse transcribed into cDNA first.
20. On a SARS-CoV-2 lateral flow strip, which line must always appear and what does it prove?
The red control line C. It shows the test is valid by monitoring proper liquid flow and confirming the bio-reagents are active. A visible blue test line T means positive.
21. What happens if the paramyxovirus F0 precursor is never cleaved?
No fusion activity, so no virion penetration, so no infection.
22. Why can paramyxoviruses bypass endosomes?
Fusion by F1 occurs at the neutral pH of the extracellular environment, releasing the nucleocapsid directly into the cell.
23. Which two paramyxovirus genera have neuraminidase activity, and what is it thought to do?
Respirovirus and Rubulavirus, that is the HN protein of parainfluenza and mumps viruses. It presumably prevents self-aggregation of virus particles.
24. Which paramyxovirus produces intranuclear as well as intracytoplasmic inclusions?
Measles virus. All other paramyxovirus inclusions are cytoplasmic, because RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm.
25. Which parainfluenza types cause croup, in what age group, and what is the cough like?
Types 1 and 2, in children aged 6 to 18 months. Croup is respiratory obstruction from swelling of the larynx and related structures, with a barking cough that usually worsens at night.
26. Which parainfluenza type is most prevalent, and what disease does it cause in whom?
Type 3. It spreads to the lower trachea and bronchi, causing pneumonia or bronchiolitis in young infants less than 6 months old.
27. Is parotid gland involvement required to call it mumps?
No. Involvement of the parotid glands is not an obligatory process. Viremia can seed testes, ovaries, pancreas, mammary glands and the central nervous system.
28. Which mumps antibody correlates with immunity, and when does it appear?
Antibody to the HN glycoprotein. It develops slowly, about 4 weeks after onset, and persists for years. Anti-NP appears earliest at 3 to 7 days but is gone within 6 months.
29. Describe Koplik spots precisely.
Small bluish white ulcerations on the buccal mucosa opposite the lower molars. They appear slightly before the rash, contain giant cells and viral antigens, and are pathognomonic for measles.
30. What are the most common and the most common life-threatening complications of measles?
Otitis media is the most common. Pneumonia caused by secondary bacterial infection is the most common life-threatening complication.
31. What is SSPE and what is found in the brain?
Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis, a rare complication developing years after the initial measles infection from virus that remained in the body. Large amounts of measles antigen are present within inclusion bodies in infected brain cells.
32. Give the three transmission routes of Nipah virus.
Direct animal-to-human contact with infected animals such as bats or pigs, which is the spillover event. Foodborne, from fruit or raw date palm sap soiled by infected animals. Human-to-human, through close contact with body fluids.
33. How does Hendra virus reach humans, and how lethal is it?
Only from horses, when a person handles the horse. Bats infect horses through bites, urine, droppings and saliva. Only seven human cases have ever been reported, but it kills more than half of those infected, 57 percent.
34. Which cells does RSV mainly target, and what is the final lung pathology?
Ciliated cells of the large airways, causing cell rounding and detachment. Accumulated mucus, cell debris and NET components obstruct the narrow bronchial ducts, leading to alveolar collapse, acute inflammation and lung pathology.
35. At what age does severe RSV disease appear, and why then?
At 2 to 4 months old, as maternal neutralizing antibody falls.
36. Name the two features that separate hMPV from RSV.
Peak age, older infant 6 to 12 months for MPV versus young infant under 6 months for RSV, and seasonality, February to April for MPV versus November to January for RSV. Symptoms and sex distribution are the same.
37. Which influenza proteins divide the virus into types A, B and C?
The internal structural proteins, the nucleocapsid NP and the matrix M proteins. They have no cross-reactivity among the three types.
38. State three functions of neuraminidase.
It removes sialic acid from glycoconjugates, it facilitates release of virus particles from infected cell surfaces during budding while preventing self-aggregation, and it may help the virus negotiate the mucin layer of the respiratory tract.
39. Why does influenza stay confined to the respiratory tract?
Cleavage of HA into HA1 and HA2 is required for infectivity, and the protease enzymes that cleave HA are expressed only at respiratory tract sites.
40. Contrast the mechanism of antigenic drift and antigenic shift in one sentence each.
Drift is a gradual change from point mutations affecting major antigenic sites, primarily on hemagglutinin. Shift is an abrupt major change from genetic reassortment with an unrelated strain, where HA and NA change independently.
41. What receptor change lets an avian influenza strain adapt to humans?
Mutation of the HA receptor-binding site from alpha 2,3-linked sialic acid receptors in avian cells to alpha 2,6-linked sialic acid receptors in human cells.
42. Why is the pig called the mixing vessel?
Pigs and humans possess receptors for both human and avian influenza strains, so they can be infected by both at the same time, allowing reassortment and the emergence of mixed gene strains.
43. What is original antigenic sin?
Subsequent infections or immunizations reinforce the antibody response to the first subtype of influenza a person experienced years earlier.
44. Describe Reye's syndrome.
An acute encephalopathy in children and adolescents usually between 2 and 16 years of age. It is rare, affects all organs but is most harmful to the brain and liver, causes an acute increase in intracranial pressure, and often causes massive accumulation of fat in the liver and other organs.
45. Which three bacteria commonly cause secondary pneumonia after influenza?
Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae.
46. What are the classic isolation methods for influenza, and how is growth detected?
Embryonated eggs and primary monkey kidney cells. Cell cultures are tested by hemadsorption 3 to 5 days after inoculation, or the culture fluid is examined after 5 to 7 days by hemagglutination or immunofluorescence.
47. Why are paired sera necessary for influenza serology?
Because normal individuals usually already have influenza antibodies, so a single titer cannot prove recent infection.
