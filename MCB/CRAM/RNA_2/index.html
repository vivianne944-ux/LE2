Pages 1–2

DNA versus RNA genome patterns

DNA pattern

  • DNA normally appears as a double-stranded structure.
  • Parvovirus is the single-stranded exception in Baltimore Group II.
  • All single-stranded DNA viruses are described in the lecture as nonenveloped.
  • DNA viruses are not classified as positive or negative sense.

RNA pattern

  • RNA normally exists as a single-stranded structure.
  • Reoviridae is the double-stranded RNA exception in Baltimore Group III.
  • RNA viruses may be positive or negative sense.
  • RNA viruses may be segmented or nonsegmented and enveloped or nonenveloped.
Why Segmentation MattersA segmented genome can undergo genetic reassortment.The lecture links reassortment to increased mutant or variant strains.

Postnatal rubella — immunity and laboratory diagnosis

Immunity

  • Rubella antibodies appear as the rash fades.
  • Antibody titers rise over 1–3 weeks.
  • IgM is the initial antibody and does not persist beyond 6 weeks.
  • IgG persists for life.
  • One attack confers lifelong immunity because there is one antigenic type.
  • Maternal antibodies protect offspring for about 4–6 months.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Little antigenic variation allows antigen from one strain to detect antibodies induced by other rubella strains.
  • Specimens and tests listed include nasopharyngeal or throat swab, urine, IgM serum, and IgG serum.
  • Rubella IgM develops shortly after primary infection or vaccination.
  • Rubella IgG becomes detectable by day 4 after rash onset and peaks during the next 1–2 weeks.
Lecture-Specific Vaccine Timing NoteThe lecture states that the Pediatric Society of the Philippines recommends rubella vaccination at the 9th month because maternal antibodies persist until the 6th month.

Clinical manifestations and laboratory diagnosis

Clinical and pathologic pattern

  • Incubation period: 1–14 days, most often 3–7 days.
  • Common onset symptoms: fever, dry cough, myalgia, fatigue, dyspnea, anorexia.
  • Atypical initial symptoms may include diarrhea and nausea.
  • Severe and critical disease may progress to acute respiratory failure, ARDS, metabolic acidosis, coagulopathy, septic shock, and multiple organ failure.
  • Autopsy findings cited include ARDS-like lung injury with lymphocyte infiltration and liver injury with moderate microvesicular steatosis.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • RT-qPCR remains the foundational nucleic acid amplification test.
  • Targets may include ORFab, N, and E genes.
  • Rapid antigen tests are immunochromatographic assays used as rapid screening tools.
  • Antigen tests are complementary to NAATs rather than standalone replacements.
RT-PCR LogicPCR amplifies DNA, while SARS-CoV-2 has an RNA genome.Viral RNA is first extracted and reverse transcribed to cDNA, then amplified.A positive amplification curve crosses the detection threshold.
Lateral Flow InterpretationControl line C must be present for the test to be valid.A visible test line T indicates a positive sample in the lecture’s described assay.Absence of the test line indicates a negative result or a level below the detection limit.

Page 7

Replication, assembly, budding, and neuraminidase activity

  • Transcription and replication occur in the cytoplasm through viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.
  • Progeny nucleocapsids form in the cytoplasm and migrate to the cell surface.
  • Nucleocapsids are attracted to plasma membrane sites containing HN, H, or G plus F0 glycoprotein spikes.
  • M protein is essential for particle formation and links the envelope to the nucleocapsid.
  • During budding, most host proteins are excluded from the viral membrane.
Neuraminidase ActivityThe HN protein of parainfluenza and mumps has neuraminidase activity.The lecture states that this presumably prevents self-aggregation of virus particles.Other paramyxoviruses do not possess neuraminidase activity.
Pages 7–8

Genetic and antigenic stability

  • The nonsegmented genome prevents gene-segment reshuffling by reassortment.
  • HN, H, G, and F surface proteins show minimal antigenic variation over long periods.
  • The lecture states that paramyxoviruses do not undergo the antigenic drift pattern emphasized for influenza.
  • The lecturer notes that many amino acids in paramyxovirus glycoproteins may have structural or functional roles, leaving less room for viable substitutions.
Do Not ConfuseParamyxovirus: nonsegmented → no reassortment.Influenza: segmented → reassortment is possible.

Human parainfluenza virus

Parainfluenza Virus

Respiratory-limited pathogenesis, disease by type, immunity, diagnosis, and epidemiology.

Page 8

Pathogenesis in the immunocompetent host

  • Replication is limited to respiratory epithelia.
  • Viremia is uncommon, if it occurs at all.
  • Infection limited to the nose and throat can produce a common cold syndrome.

Types 1 and 2May involve the larynx and upper trachea.Cause croup, laryngotracheobronchitis, especially in children 6–18 months.Croup causes respiratory obstruction from swelling of the larynx and related structures.Classic cue: barking cough, often worse at night.

Page 8

Immunity and reinfection

  • Types 1, 2, and 3 are distinct serotypes with little significant cross-neutralization.
  • Antibody to one type does not reliably protect against another type.
  • Maternal serum antibodies do not prevent infection or disease.
  • Reinfection occurs even in older children and adults with prior antibodies.
  • Antibodies can modify disease, so reinfections often present as nonfebrile upper respiratory infections.
  • Secretory IgA is most important for protection against reinfection, but disappears within a few months.
  • Serum antibodies target HN and F surface proteins.
  • Successive reinfections can make the antibody response less specific because parainfluenza and mumps share antigens.
Why Reinfection Is CommonMucosal IgA protection is short-lived.Reinfection can therefore occur even when serum antibody is present.
Page 8

Diagnosis and epidemiology

Diagnosis

  • RT-PCR using nasopharyngeal washes.
  • Rapid antigen detection through direct or indirect immunofluorescence on exfoliated nasopharyngeal cells.
  • Serology uses paired sera with neutralization, hemagglutination-inhibition, or ELISA.
  • A fourfold rise in antibody titer is the key paired-sera signal emphasized in the lecture.

Epidemiology

  • Type 3 is most prevalent and is usually acquired in the first year of life, with antibodies by age 2.
  • Types 1 and 2 are less prevalent and are usually acquired by age 5.
  • Reinfection occurs through childhood and adulthood, usually as mild upper respiratory disease.
  • Transmission is through direct person-to-person contact or large respiratory droplets.
  • Common in school children and hospitals.

Pages 8–9

Clinical findings and complications

  • Mild pain may occur in front of the neck or in front of the ears as salivary or parotid glands become involved.
  • Swollen and sore salivary glands may cause trouble chewing.
  • Other symptoms include fever, headache, muscle aches, tiredness, loss of appetite, and testicular pain or tenderness.
  • About one third of infections are subclinical.
  • Subclinical disease is common in children under 2 years.
  • CNS involvement occurs in about 10–30%.
  • Mumps can cause aseptic meningitis that may resolve spontaneously.
Postpubertal ComplicationAfter puberty, testes and ovaries may be affected.In males, orchitis is usually unilateral and causes marked swelling from inflammatory-cell infiltration.Pain is linked to swelling of the inflamed testis within the tunica albuginea.
Page 9

Immunity

  • Immunity is permanent after a single infection.
  • There is only one antigenic type.
  • The virus does not exhibit antigenic variation in the lecture framework.
  • Antibodies develop against HN, F, and internal NP proteins.
  • HN antibody develops slowly, about 4 weeks after onset, persists for years, and correlates with immunity.
  • NP antibody appears earliest, around 3–7 days after symptom onset, and is gone within about 6 months.
  • Subclinical infection can generate lifelong immunity.
  • Passive maternal immunity helps explain why mumps is rare in infants under 6 months.
Page 9

Laboratory diagnosis and prevention

Diagnosis

  • RT-PCR can detect mumps genome sequences.
  • Serology may use ELISA or hemagglutination inhibition.
  • Single antibody detection is not enough for diagnosis.
  • Paired sera should demonstrate a fourfold rise in antibody titer.
  • IgM can be present in early illness until about 60 days.

Prevention and control

  • Best approach: immunization with attenuated live mumps virus vaccine.
  • The vaccine produces a subclinical, noncommunicable infection.
  • Mumps vaccine is available in the live-virus MMR combination.

  • Only one antigenic type.
  • Infection confers lifelong immunity.

Laboratory diagnosis and prevention

  • RT-PCR detects antigen or nucleic acid in epithelial cells from respiratory secretions, nasopharynx, conjunctiva, and urine.
  • Serology uses ELISA and hemagglutination inhibition with a fourfold increase in antibody titer.
  • Virus can be isolated in monkey or human kidney cells.
  • Cytopathic effects include multinucleated giant cells with intranuclear and intracytoplasmic inclusion bodies.
  • Prevention uses a live attenuated vaccine in the MMR combination.

Zoonotic reservoirs, transmission chains, clinical disease, and prevention cues.

Pages 10–11

Nipah virus — outbreak, distribution, and disease

  • The name Nipah comes from a Malaysian village.
  • First outbreak described in the lecture: 1999 in Malaysia and Singapore.
  • About 300 people became ill and more than 100 died in that outbreak.
  • Bats initially spread the virus to pigs, then people working closely with infected pigs became infected.
  • Outbreaks have been reported from Bangladesh, India, Malaysia, the Philippines, and Singapore.
  • Fruit bats carrying Nipah are found throughout Asia, the South Pacific, and Australia.

RSV immunity and diagnosis

Immunity

  • Maternal neutralizing antibody can protect against lower respiratory tract infection.
  • As maternal antibody falls, severe RSV disease can occur around 2–4 months.
  • Primary infection can induce cross-reactive antibodies to other paramyxovirus subgroups.
  • RSV does not generate an effective memory response that reliably protects against future exposures.
  • During convalescence, RSV IgG-producing memory B cells are present, but IgA-producing memory B cells are deficient in the lecture discussion.

Diagnosis

  • Clinical symptoms are nonspecific and overlap with other respiratory infections.
  • rRT-PCR is more sensitive than culture and antigen testing.
  • Antigen tests are sensitive in children but less sensitive in adults.
  • Viral culture is less commonly used.
  • Serology is generally reserved for research and surveillance.

Human metapneumovirus

  • Human metapneumovirus infects only humans.
  • Incubation period: about 4–9 days.
  • Viral shedding: about 5 days in children and several weeks in immunocompromised hosts.
  • Replication is limited to respiratory epithelial cells.
  • Cell-surface receptor listed: αvβ1 integrin.
  • Cytopathic effects in cultured cells resemble those of RSV.
  • Symptoms cannot be distinguished clinically from RSV.
  • Children may have rhinorrhea, cough, fever, otitis media, bronchiolitis, pneumonia, croup, or asthma exacerbation.
  • Severe disease can occur in immunocompromised patients and institutionalized elderly persons.
  • Healthy adults tend to develop cold or flu-like symptoms.

Influenza — Genome, Structure, Classification, and Naming

Why the segmented negative-sense genome matters and how HA and NA organize influenza biology.

Pages 13–14

Orthomyxoviridae and segmented genome

  • Orthomyxoviridae is a single-stranded, segmented, enveloped RNA virus family.
  • Influenza viruses are negative-sense RNA viruses.
  • The lecture states that influenza encodes its genome across eight genomic segments.
  • Segment exchange during coinfection can generate new viral variants.
  • Reassortment can produce progeny containing genes from more than one parent.
  • Reassortment also requires genetic compatibility, including conserved packaging signals and maintained RNA-protein interactions.
Why Segmentation MattersSegmented genome → reassortment during coinfection.The lecture links reassortment between human and avian or swine influenza strains to the ability to generate pandemic viruses.
Pages 14–15

Influenza virion structure

  • Virions are usually spherical, although the electron micrograph demonstrates pleomorphism.
  • The envelope is a host-derived lipid membrane.
  • Surface spikes are about 10 nm long.
  • The major surface glycoproteins are HA, hemagglutinin, and NA, neuraminidase.
  • NA is identified as a target of Tamiflu in the lecture.
  • HA and NA determine antigenic variation, host immunity, subtypes, and strains such as A/H1N1.
  • Protection against reinfection is mainly due to antibodies to HA, with antibodies to NA also protective.

Innate and humoral immune responses

Innate response

  • Macrophages, NK cells, and dendritic cells are highlighted.
  • NK cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes important for eliminating influenza virus.
  • Early infection activates alveolar macrophages and monocytes.
  • Alveolar macrophages help limit spread through phagocytic and opsonophagocytic mechanisms described in the lecture.

Humoral response

  • Antibodies are produced against multiple influenza antigens.
  • HA-specific antibody is most important for neutralization and prevention of illness.
  • HA antibody blocks attachment to host cells.
  • HA antibodies may also support phagocytosis of virus particles through Fc-receptor pathways.
  • IgA and some IgM act at mucosal surfaces to neutralize pathogens and prevent entry and replication.
Page 18

Immunity and original antigenic sin

  • Influenza A, B, and C are antigenically unrelated and induce no cross-protection.
  • After antigenic drift, preexisting antibody may still make infection with a new strain milder.
  • Repeated infection or immunization can reinforce the antibody response to the first influenza subtype experienced. The lecture calls this original antigenic sin.
  • The major role of cell-mediated immunity is clearance of established infection.
  • Cytotoxic T cells lyse infected cells.
  • CTL responses can be cross-reactive across subtypes and target internal proteins NP and M as well as surface glycoproteins.

Final rapid review

Cram Sheet

Fast recall of the lecture’s highest-yield distinctions, mechanisms, values, and must-know cues.

Genome rules

  • All RNA viruses are single stranded except Reoviridae.
  • Positive sense can act as mRNA immediately.
  • Negative sense must first be transcribed to mRNA.
  • Segmented genome → reassortment.
  • Influenza is the RNA-virus nuclear-replication exception.

Rubella

  • One serotype and lifelong immunity after infection.
  • Postnatal rash: face → trunk → extremities, usually under 3 days.
  • IgM fades by about 6 weeks.
  • IgG persists for life.
  • Congenital triad: heart disease + ocular defects + hearing loss.
  • First trimester = greatest fetal damage.
  • Infant rubella IgM = congenital infection cue.

SARS-CoV-2

  • S protein is the most potent entry determinant.
  • ACE2-dependent alveolar infection is central to severe pneumonia pathogenesis.
  • Lymphopenia + cytokine storm are major severe-disease patterns.
  • Fatal cases in the lecture show neutrophilia + increased D-dimer + falling lymphocytes.
  • RT-qPCR is the foundational NAAT.
  • Antigen testing is a rapid complementary screening method.

Paramyxovirus core

  • Negative-sense, nonsegmented, enveloped.
  • F0 cleavage → active F1 → fusion.
  • No F0 cleavage means no fusion and no infection.
  • Syncytia are a hallmark.
  • Acidophilic cytoplasmic inclusions are common.
  • Measles can also produce intranuclear inclusions.
  • Nonsegmented genome means no gene-segment reassortment.

Parainfluenza

  • Types 1 and 2 → croup, especially 6–18 months.
  • Barking cough often worsens at night.
  • Type 3 → bronchiolitis or pneumonia, especially under 6 months.
  • Replication is limited to respiratory epithelia in immunocompetent hosts.
  • Secretory IgA is the most important protection against reinfection but fades quickly.

Mumps

  • Humans are the only natural host.
  • Parotitis is not obligatory.
  • Virus may disseminate to salivary glands, testes, ovaries, pancreas, mammary glands, and CNS.
  • Orchitis is an important postpubertal complication.
  • One antigenic type and lifelong immunity.
  • Best control: live attenuated vaccine in MMR.

Measles

  • Koplik spots are pathognomonic.
  • Rash begins on the head and descends.
  • Most common complication: otitis media.
  • Most common life-threatening complication: secondary bacterial pneumonia.
  • Rare delayed complication: SSPE.
  • Dual immune effect: immune suppression plus lifelong immunity to measles reinfection.

Nipah and Hendra

  • Both use Pteropus fruit bats or flying foxes as natural reservoirs.
  • Nipah: animal contact, contaminated fruit or raw date palm sap, and person-to-person body-fluid spread.
  • Nipah can progress to encephalitis and coma.
  • Hendra: bat → horse → human transmission chain.
  • Hendra can cause severe flu-like lung disease and encephalitis.

RSV and hMPV

  • RSV targets ciliated airway cells.
  • Mucus + sloughed cells + NETs drive bronchiolar obstruction.
  • Severe RSV disease can occur around 2–4 months as maternal antibody falls.
  • RSV reinfection is common because protective memory is limited.
  • hMPV peak age: 6–12 months.
  • RSV peak age: under 6 months.
  • hMPV symptoms can be clinically indistinguishable from RSV.

Influenza structure

  • Negative-sense, segmented, enveloped.
  • 8 genomic segments in the lecture.
  • HA binds cells and is the major neutralizing-antibody target.
  • HA cleavage to HA1 + HA2 is required for infectivity.
  • NA removes sialic acid and facilitates release.
  • NA also prevents self-aggregation and may help passage through mucin.
  • Influenza A subtypes are defined by HA and NA.

Drift versus shift

Memory AidDrift = gradual point mutation.Shift = sudden major change.
  • Shift can occur through genetic reassortment.
  • Direct avian-to-human adaptation can involve receptor preference change from α2,3 to α2,6 sialic acid.
  • Pigs and humans can support mixing of human and avian strains in the lecture discussion.
  • Reassortment examples: Asian flu, Hong Kong flu, pandemic H1N1.

Influenza clinical and lab

  • Classic influenza is abrupt.
  • High fever commonly lasts 3–5 days.
  • Secondary bacterial pneumonia pathogens: S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae.
  • HA antibody is the most important humoral neutralizing response.
  • RT-PCR is highly sensitive and specific.
  • Serology uses HI or ELISA with paired acute and convalescent sera.
  • Lecture prevention anchor: yearly influenza vaccination.
