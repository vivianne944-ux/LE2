1. Arbovirus overview and classification

MUST KNOWPage 1
Core idea. Arbovirus and robovirus are ecological classifications. They are NOT a viral family and are NOT based on genetics. Many virus families are lumped together as arboviruses because they all transmit via arthropods.

Arbovirus vs Robovirus MUST KNOW

FeatureARBOvirusROBOvirus
NameArthropod-BorneRodent-Borne
How spreadBlood-feeding arthropods carry virus from one vertebrate host to anotherRodent is the host, no arthropod vector. Spread by contact with body fluid or excretions
Examples of vectorsMosquitoes, ticks, sandfliesNone

Significance SHOULD KNOW

  • Millions of infections annually, emerging infectious diseases, cause epidemics worldwide
  • Major in tropical and subtropical countries
  • Cause encephalitis and hemorrhagic fever
  • Distribution depends on vector species, reservoir hosts, climate, rainfall, human activity
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Arbovirus = ecological group, not a family, not genetic
  • Arbovirus uses arthropods. Robovirus uses rodents with no arthropod
  • Culex = JE, SLE, WNV. Aedes = YF, dengue, Zika. YF also uses Haemagogus
  • Flavi and Toga = +ssRNA. Filo, Bunya, Arena = −ssRNA

2. Arbovirus transmission ecology

MUST KNOWPage 2
Core idea. Arboviruses cycle between a reservoir and a vector. Humans often get infected but frequently cannot pass the virus back to mosquitoes (dead-end hosts). Unlike influenza, arboviruses have distinct geographic distributions.

  • Virus passes to offspring through the eggs (shown for A. aegypti)
  • Eggs hatch into mosquitoes that are already infected

RECALL CUE (study aid) Encephalitis viruses (Culex group, equine encephalitis) end in humans. The Aedes group (Dengue, Zika, Chikungunya, urban YF) keeps going through humans.

Reservoir hosts and vectors SHOULD KNOW

Drivers of arboviral disease (Figure 2) NICE TO KNOW

  • Drivers: urbanization, climate change, global travel, wildlife spillover, Aedes mosquitoes, genetic change
  • Control and surveillance: vaccines, surveillance systems, transgenic mosquitoes and Wolbachia
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Endemic in PH: Dengue and JE. Present: Zika, Chikungunya
  • Transovarial = virus passed through mosquito eggs to offspring
  • Dead-end host = viremia too low to reinfect mosquitoes
  • Humans are NOT dead-end in Dengue, urban YF, Zika, Chikungunya
  • Pigs matter in JE. Birds are major reservoirs for mosquito-borne encephalitis

3. Clinical syndromes, pathogenesis, diagnosis, and management

MUST KNOWPages 2 to 3
Core idea. Arboviruses produce three major syndromes. The degree of viral multiplication and its predominant site decide which syndrome appears. Some viruses, like dengue, can cause more than one.

Three major clinical syndromes (Table 7) MUST KNOW

1. Fever of an undifferentiated type
2. Encephalitis (inflammation of the brain)
3. Hemorrhagic fever
LECTURER EMPHASIS ‼ Always use these syndromes to distinguish arboviruses. Example from the lecture: Hemorrhagic fever = Dengue, Encephalitis = Japanese encephalitis.

Encephalitis pathogenesis (Table 8) MECHANISMPages 2 to 3

Virus crosses BBB→Neuronal infection→Neuronal degeneration→Inflammation→Neurologic symptoms
  • Exact mechanism unknown
  • Many arboviruses cause encephalitis. In the Philippines, Japanese encephalitis is the common cause of arboviral encephalitis

General clinical manifestations SHOULD KNOW

Fever, headache, myalgia, rash, nausea and vomiting, confusion, seizures and coma (seizures and coma are usually present when the arbovirus causes encephalitis).

Diagnosis, treatment, prevention (Table 10) MUST KNOW

Laboratory diagnosisTreatmentPrevention
  • PCR
  • Serologic test (antibody or antigen)
  • Virus isolation
  • Supportive care
  • Fluid management

  • Vector control, vector avoidance

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY (lecture checkpoint)
  • Arboviruses = ecological group spread by blood-feeding arthropods
  • Humans usually dead-end hosts
  • Three syndromes: febrile illness, encephalitis, hemorrhagic fever
  • Diagnosis relies on PCR and serology
  • Prevention = vector control, individual protection, vaccination (where available)
  • Pathogenesis ends in viremia → target organ by tropism

Flavivirus replication "APUSAR" (Table 12, ‼) MECHANISMPage 4

Mnemonic APUSAR is from the PDF: Attachment, Penetration, Uncoating, Synthesis, Assembly, Release.

Attachment
1. Virus attaches to its receptor
Penetration
2. Enters via receptor-mediated endocytosis → 3. enclosed in endosome → 4. endosome acidifies → 5. pH-dependent fusion
Uncoating
6. Uncoating → 7. nucleocapsid released into cytoplasm
Synthesis
8. Genome acts directly as mRNA → 9. one large polyprotein → 10. cleaved into structural and non-structural proteins → 11. RNA replication
Assembly
12. Structural proteins assemble with RNA → 13. envelope from the ER → 14. through the Golgi → 15. maturation
Release
16. Released via exocytosis
LECTURER In dengue, NS1 = Non-Structural protein 1 (links step 10 to the NS1 test). Maturation = further cleavage of viral proteins into mature proteins.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Flavivirus = spherical, 50 nm, enveloped, linear +ssRNA, infectious genome
  • Entry by receptor-mediated endocytosis with pH-dependent fusion
  • Genome → one polyprotein → cleaved (e.g., NS1)
  • Envelope from ER, passes Golgi, exits by exocytosis

5. Yellow fever

MUST KNOWPages 4 to 5
FamilyFlaviviridae
DistributionTropical Africa and Americas
ReservoirHumans, monkeys
VectorsA. aegypti, Aedes spp., Haemagogus
DiseaseHemorrhagic fever and Jaundice
Core idea. The prototypical flavivirus and the first flavivirus discovered. Named for the jaundice it causes. A biphasic disease that damages the liver, with Councilman bodies as the pathognomonic histologic finding.

Distribution MUST KNOW

  • Only in tropical South America and Africa
  • Distribution due to the Transatlantic slave trade

Why no yellow fever in the Philippines?

YF does not occur in East and Southeast Asia despite A. aegypti populations that sustain dengue. Nobody really knows. Possible answers:

  1. Success of quarantine and vaccination
  2. Biological incompatibility
  3. Partial herd cross-immunity from endemic dengue
  4. Sheer luck

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • First / prototypical flavivirus, S. America and Africa only, none in PH
  • Jungle vector Haemagogus, urban vector A. aegypti

  • RT-PCR best early. 17D: one dose, 95%, 30+ years. Not for <9 mo, pregnancy, egg allergy, immunocompromised

6. Dengue

MUST KNOWPages 5 to 7
FamilyFlaviviridae
DistributionTropics worldwide
ReservoirHumans, monkeys
VectorsA. aegypti, other Aedes
DiseaseFever, arthralgia, myalgia, rash, hemorrhagic fever
Core idea. The most important human arthropod-borne viral disease worldwide (over 40% of the world's population at risk). Endemic in the Philippines. Severe dengue is mostly caused by antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) on a second infection with a different serotype.

Epidemiology SHOULD KNOW

  • Tropical and subtropical. Endemic in South-East Asia, the Pacific, East and West Africa, the Caribbean, the Americas
  • Increasing problem because of: sharply rising severe dengue with high mortality, wider geographic spread (widespread A. aegypti plus recent spread of A. albopictus), and continuing evolution of dengue viruses

Vector and transmission MUST KNOWPages 5 to 6

  • Aedes = black mosquito with white stripes
  • Mostly A. aegypti, also A. albopictus
  • Bites during daytime
  • Breeds in clean stagnant water
  • Transmission cycle is similar to YF, with urban and jungle cycles
  • Humans and mosquitoes both spread dengue. Mosquitoes first become infected when they bite an infected person

Serotypes MUST KNOW

  • Four serotypes: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, DENV-4
  • Infection with one serotype protects only against that serotype
  • Cross-reactivity with other serotypes is very limited and short-lived
CLINICAL PEARL (lecturer) Over a decade ago a fifth serotype was discovered, but it is more on a jungle cycle. (Table 5 for the Philippines lists dengue 1 to 4.)

Treatment and prevention (Table 27) MUST KNOW

Treatment

  • Paracetamol
  • AVOID ibuprofen, mefenamic acid, aspirin (may increase bleeding risk)

  • Qdenga (Takeda): no need for prior dengue infection. Under FDA approval

  2. Secure self-protection
  3. Seek early consultation
  4. Support fogging only during outbreaks

4T strategy

Taob, Taktak, Tuyo, Takip

Other

Figure 26 "No mosquitoes = no dengue": empty and scrub water containers, clean vases and pet bowls, clean drains and gutters, dispose of containers that hold water, turn over containers, even a bottle cap can breed mosquitoes.

EXAM TRAP Pain or fever relief in dengue = paracetamol. NSAIDs and aspirin are the wrong answer because of bleeding.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Aedes: black with white stripes, day-biter, clean stagnant water
  • 4 serotypes, protection is serotype-specific
  • Classic: saddle back fever, retro-orbital headache, breakbone pain, measles-like rash
  • Severe dengue: ~5%, 90% from secondary heterologous infection, 2 to 3 days after fever resolves
  • Hallmark: platelets <100,000/µL plus plasma leakage
  • ADE: non-neutralizing antibodies → more monocyte/macrophage infection
  • Tourniquet >20 petechiae positive. NS1 and RT-PCR early, IgM later
  • Paracetamol, avoid NSAIDs and aspirin. 4S and 4T. No FDA-approved vaccine in PH

7. Japanese encephalitis

MUST KNOWPage 8
FamilyFlaviviridae
DistributionSouth and Southeast Asia
ReservoirBirds, pigs (amplifying)
VectorCulex spp.
DiseaseAcute viral encephalitis
Core idea. One of the most important causes of arthropod-transmitted viral encephalitis globally, and the common cause of arboviral encephalitis in the Philippines, where it is found year-round in all regions. Most infections are mild, but >50% of severe cases are fatal or leave devastating sequelae.

History NICE TO KNOW

  • Epidemics described in Japan in the 1870s. First recovered in 1935 from a human brain in Tokyo
  • Outbreaks in Japan, China, Taiwan, Korea, the Philippines, India, parts of Southeast Asia, far-eastern Russia

Enzootic cycle MUST KNOW

  • Maintained between waterbirds (egrets and herons) and mosquitoes
  • Pigs = amplifying host (amplifying means produces more virus)
  • Pigs develop high and prolonged viremia, so mosquitoes pick up enough virus when feeding
  • Humans and other vertebrates (e.g., horses) = incidental, dead-end hosts
EXAM TRAP Birds AND pigs are reservoirs, but the amplifying host is the pig (Review Question 3).

Vector: Culex MUST KNOW

  • Especially Culex tritaeniorhynchus
  • Brownish or gray, relatively uniform color
  • Breeds in rice fields, marshes, standing water
  • Evening or night-biting
  • Also transmits West Nile and St. Louis encephalitis

Clinical manifestations MUST KNOW

  • Asymptomatic, or mild febrile illness, aseptic meningitis, seizures, encephalitis, poliomyelitis-like flaccid paralysis
  • Death can occur, especially in children, within 3 to 5 days of CNS symptoms
  • Sequelae: chronic seizures, motor dysfunction, neuropsychiatric problems (mental retardation, psychiatric disorders)

Brain pathology

  • Neuronal degeneration, necrosis
  • Microglial nodules
  • Perivascular parenchymal leukocyte infiltrates
  • Focal hemorrhage

Cause: direct cytopathic effect plus inflammation. Most affected: gray matter of cortex, midbrain, brainstem.

Lecturer: the brain has limited regenerative capacity, so damage is irreversible → sequelae.

Treatment MUST KNOW

No specific antiviral. Supportive. Effective vaccines are available for at-risk populations.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Commonest arboviral encephalitis in PH, year-round, all regions
  • Cycle: waterbirds, amplified by pigs. Humans and horses = dead-end
  • Culex tritaeniorhynchus: brown, rice fields, night-biter
  • >50% of severe cases fatal or sequelae. Children can die within 3 to 5 days of CNS signs
  • Vaccine available, no antiviral

8. Zika virus

MUST KNOWPage 8
FamilyFlaviviridae
DistributionTropical and subtropical
ReservoirNonhuman primates, humans
VectorsA. aegypti, A. albopictus
DiseaseRash, arthralgia, conjunctivitis, microcephaly, GBS

What sets Zika apart MUST KNOW

  • Non-mosquito routes: pregnant woman → fetus, and sexual transmission
  • Most infected people have no symptoms
  • Symptoms: rash, fever, nonpurulent conjunctivitis, muscle and joint pain, malaise, headache lasting 2 to 7 days
  • Pregnancy: microcephaly, other congenital malformations, preterm birth, miscarriage
  • Guillain-Barré syndrome, neuropathy, myelitis in adults and children
  • Transmission cycles (Figure 31): enzootic/sylvatic with nonhuman primates, and urban/epidemic with humans

Management MUST KNOW

No specific treatment. No vaccine yet. Planning pregnancy.

In the PDF this block sits under a repeated "Transmission" heading, but its content is about management.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Aedes-borne, plus sexual and maternal-fetal spread
  • Hallmarks: congenital microcephaly and GBS
  • No vaccine, no specific treatment

10. Togaviridae and Chikungunya

MUST KNOWPage 9

Shape and sizeSpherical, 70 nm
Genome+ssRNA
EnvelopeEnveloped
ReplicationCytoplasm
AssemblyBudding through host membranes
  • Three or four major structural polypeptides, two glycosylated
  • All viruses are serologically related

Taxonomy (Table 33) SHOULD KNOW

  • Chikungunya: family Togaviridae, genus Alphavirus (mosquito-borne)
  • ICTV moved genus Rubivirus out of Togaviridae into Matonaviridae
  • Rubella (German measles) is NOT an arbovirus

Chikungunya overview MUST KNOW

DistributionTropical and subtropical worldwide
ReservoirUrban: humans. Sylvatic: nonhuman primates, rodents, birds, small animals
VectorsA. aegypti, A. albopictus
DiseaseAcute febrile illness with severe polyarthralgia

Genetic change and mortality MUST KNOW

  • One amino acid change in the E1 glycoprotein: alanine → valine at position 226
  • This improved viral fitness in Aedes albopictus, which has a wider geographic distribution than A. aegypti
  • Generally not life-threatening. Mortality about 1 in 1,000 infected
PDF NOTE Jawetz lists position 266, which the trans calls a typo. 226 is correct.

Clinical manifestations MUST KNOW

  • Very rapid onset fever, nausea, headache, joint pains especially in the extremities
  • Half of patients get a rash
  • Joint pains may be prolonged and recur
  • Neurologic involvement is not common (children with seizure or encephalopathy)
LECTURER "Chikungunya → synovial tissue of joints → severe polyarthralgia." Polyarthralgia is the distinguishing symptom.

Treatment and prevention MUST KNOW

  • No vaccine. No approved antiviral drugs

12. Filoviridae: Ebola and Marburg

MUST KNOWPages 10 to 11
FamilyFiloviridae
GeneraMarburgvirus, Ebolavirus
GenomeNonsegmented −ssRNA
ShapePleomorphic, filamentous
Suspected hostFruit bats (or rodents)
Core idea. Filoviruses (Latin filum = thread) cause the deadliest viral hemorrhagic fevers (25 to 90% mortality). Spread by direct contact with blood or body fluids. Lab work needs BSL-4. The Reston strain is the Philippine link.

Strains MUST KNOW

  • Two filoviruses in separate genera: Marburg virus and Ebola virus
  • Ebola species listed: Zaire, Bundibugyo, Ivory Coast, Sudan, Reston
EXAM TRAP Ebola strain found in the Philippines = Reston (Review Question 5). PDF mnemonic: "Reston" sounds like "Rustom" Padilla.

Morphology and genome MUST KNOWPage 10

  • Pleomorphic: long filamentous or odd-shaped forms
  • Single-stranded, negative-sense RNA
  • Large genome with seven sequentially arranged genes
NP→VP35→VP40→GP→VP30→VP24→L

NP = nucleoprotein, VP = virion protein, GP = glycoprotein, L = polymerase.

RECALL CUE (study aid) Starts with N, ends with L. In the middle, VP numbers go 35, 40, then GP breaks in, then 30, 24 count down.

Transmission, virulence, disease MUST KNOW

  • Natural hosts and vectors unknown, suspected bats or possibly rodents. Trans adds: fruit bats
  • Highly virulent in humans and nonhuman primates, infections usually end in death
  • Highest mortality (25 to 90%) of all viral hemorrhagic fevers
  • Transmission: direct contact with blood or body fluids
MarburgEbola
Incubation3 to 9 days2 to 21 days
ProgressionFever, headache, sore throat, muscle pain → abdominal pain, vomiting, diarrhea, rash with ‼ both internal and external bleeding → shock and death
  • Tropism: macrophage system, dendritic cells, interstitial fibroblasts, endothelial cells → later liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys
  • Figure 37 labels: monocytes and macrophages release cytokines → recruitment of inflammatory cells, tissue factor synthesis (coagulation), vasodilation and increased permeability, lymphocyte apoptosis, necrosis of hepatocytes and adrenal cortical cells

The Philippine connection (Table 38) MUST KNOW

MonkeysPigs
  • 1989: Ebola-like filovirus in cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis)
  • Imported to the US from the Philippines, held in a quarantine facility in Virginia
  • Reston strain: low pathogenicity for humans, present in PH
  • Pigs in the Philippines found to have Ebola, with high mortality
  • Proved Ebola Reston can be found in animals other than primates

Outbreak dynamics SHOULD KNOWPage 11

  • Ebola spreads slower than COVID-19 because it needs direct contact (COVID-19 spreads via aerosols)
  • Traditional African funeral practices drove outbreaks. Safe body isolation protocols are needed
  • Current outbreak: CDC responding to Bundibugyo Ebola in remote DRC. No US cases, low US risk. Expanding faster than West Africa 2014 and DRC 2018
PDF NOTE Page 10 places the current Bundibugyo outbreak in Uganda. Page 11 places it in the DRC. The PDF does not resolve this.

Diagnosis and biosafety MUST KNOW

  • ELISA for antibodies or antigen, RT-PCR
  • Patient sera may contain live virulent virus → dangerous
  • Tests only under maximum containment: Biosafety Level 4
LECTURER Most clinical labs are BSL-2. Very few are BSL-3 (these handle TB). BSL-4 is extremely rare, reserved for exotic high-risk pathogens like filoviruses.

Control and treatment MUST KNOW

  • Reservoir unknown → no reservoir control possible
  • Isolation facilities in hospitals = most effective means of controlling Ebola outbreaks
  • No specific antiviral. Maintain renal function and electrolyte balance, combat hemorrhage and shock
  • Per the PDF: currently no approved vaccine
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Filo = thread, pleomorphic, −ssRNA, 7 genes (NP to L)
  • 25 to 90% mortality, highest of all VHFs
  • Incubation: Marburg 3 to 9 days, Ebola 2 to 21 days
  • Direct contact with blood or body fluids. Suspected host fruit bats
  • Reston = PH, monkeys (1989) and pigs, low human pathogenicity
  • Lab work = BSL-4. Control = hospital isolation

16. Commonly confused concepts

COMMON CONFUSION
Do not confuse: Aedes vs Culex Pages 5, 8
Aedes
  • Black with white stripes, bites in the daytime
  • Clean stagnant water
  • Dengue, Zika, Chikungunya, YF
Culex
  • Brownish/gray, uniform, bites evening or night
  • Rice fields, marshes
  • JE, West Nile, St. Louis
Fast distinction: daytime striped biter → hemorrhagic and rash group. Night rice-field biter → encephalitis group.
Do not confuse: Reservoir vs amplifying host vs dead-end host vs vector Pages 2, 8
TermDefining featureExample from lecture
ReservoirWhere the virus lives and survives, continuous sourceBirds for JE, SLE, WNV
Amplifying hostHigh, prolonged viremia → produces more virus for mosquitoesPigs in JE
Dead-end hostViremia too low to reinfect mosquitoesHumans in JE, WNV, SLE. Humans and horses in EEE
VectorCarries virus from host to hostMosquitoes, ticks, sandflies
Fast distinction: amplifier makes a lot of virus, dead-end makes too little.
Do not confuse: NS1 vs ELISA antibody tests in dengue Page 7
NS1 (and RT-PCR)
  • Detects viral antigen (RT-PCR detects RNA)
  • Strip antibody captures antigen
  • Early illness
IgM / IgG ELISA
  • Detects the patient's antibodies
  • Plate antigen captures antibody
  • Later illness (IgM). IgG = previous or recent
Fast distinction: NS1 looks for the virus. ELISA looks for the patient's response.
Do not confuse: Uncomplicated vs severe dengue Page 6
Uncomplicated
  • 95% asymptomatic or mild
  • Saddle back fever, breakbone pain, rash
  • Stops at peak viremia, no sequelae
Severe (DHF/DSS)
  • ~5%, mostly children
  • 2 to 3 days after fever resolves
  • Platelets <100,000, plasma leakage, shock. 90% secondary heterologous infection
Fast distinction: fever breaking does NOT mean safe. The critical phase follows defervescence.
Do not confuse: Dengvaxia vs Qdenga Page 7
Dengvaxia (Sanofi)
  • Higher risk in people never infected
  • Vaccine acts as first infection → ADE on later infection
Qdenga (Takeda)
  • No need for prior infection
  • Under FDA approval
Fast distinction: neither is FDA-approved in the Philippines per the PDF.
Do not confuse: Flavivirus vs Togavirus Pages 3, 9
Flavivirus
  • 50 nm, spherical, enveloped +ssRNA
  • Envelope from ER, exits by exocytosis
  • YF, dengue, JE, Zika, SLE, WNV
Togavirus (Alphavirus)
  • 70 nm, spherical, enveloped +ssRNA
  • Assembles by budding through host membranes
  • Chikungunya, EEE, WEE
Fast distinction: both +ssRNA and enveloped. Size (50 vs 70 nm) and exit route differ.
Do not confuse: EEE vs WEE Pages 9 to 10
EEE
  • 30% mortality, half get sequelae
  • Humans and horses dead-end
WEE
  • Milder, lower mortality
  • Humans dead-end
Fast distinction: Eastern is the severe one.
Do not confuse: Ebola vs Marburg Page 10
Marburg
  • Incubation 3 to 9 days
  • Genus Marburgvirus
Ebola
  • Incubation 2 to 21 days
  • Species include Reston (PH), Zaire, Sudan, Bundibugyo, Ivory Coast
Fast distinction: same symptom progression. Incubation window and Reston link separate them.
Do not confuse: HFRS vs HPS Page 11
HFRS
  • Kidney (interstitial nephritis), hemorrhage
  • Urban rats, Korean War
HPS
  • Lungs (pulmonary edema), no hemorrhage
  • Sin Nombre, deer mouse, ribavirin
Fast distinction: R in HFRS = Renal. P in HPS = Pulmonary.
Do not confuse: YF jungle vs urban vs intermediate cycle Page 4
Jungle (sylvatic)
  • Monkey ↔ mosquito, no humans
  • Haemagogus (S. America)
Urban / Intermediate
  • Urban: human ↔ A. aegypti
  • Intermediate (savannah): monkeys or humans
Fast distinction: humans enter only in urban and intermediate cycles.
