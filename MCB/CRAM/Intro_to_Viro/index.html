1. General Characteristics of Viruses

MUST KNOWPage 1
Acellular
No cell organelles and no metabolic processes, which living cells require → viruses are not considered "living".
Submicroscopic
Too small for a compound (light/optical) microscope, so they cannot be shown by Gram stain or other light-microscopy techniques. An electron microscope (electron beams instead of visible light) is required.
No synthesis machinery
No genes encoding a complete protein synthesis machinery, and no genes for proteins of energy production and membrane biosynthesis → this is exactly why they must live inside a host cell.
Obligate intracellular parasite
Outside a living cell a virus cannot self-replicate. Same habit as the atypical bacteria Rickettsia, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia.
Drug response
Resistant to antibiotics (no bacterial targets), but inhibited by interferon and antiviral agents.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY

  • 20–300 nm → passes bacterial filters, so filtration alone does not clear water of viruses.
  • Resistant to antibiotics, inhibited by interferon and antivirals.
  • Interferon works on the neighboring cell, telling it to make antiviral proteins before the virus arrives.

C. Spikes

  • Protrude from the viral surface and bind specific receptors on the host cell membrane to facilitate viral entry.
  • Made of glycoproteins, for example gp120 of HIV → attaches to the CD4+ receptor, helped by co-receptors CCR5 and CXCR4.
  • For a virus to gain entry its glycoprotein spikes must interact with the host cell receptor, which is very important for viral tropism.

Jawetz sequence: HIV infects CD4+ T cells using CD4+ plus the CCR5 chemokine receptor → replicates and completes its life cycle → progressive loss of CD4+ T cells → AIDS → susceptibility to opportunistic infections.

D. Capsid

Protein coat that covers and protects the genome. Three kinds of symmetry, and both icosahedral and helical are further split into naked and enveloped forms. Enveloped forms carry a lipid envelope with the virus inside it, plus glycoprotein spikes.

Icosahedral (cubic)
  • Polyhedral with 20 flat sides
  • Mostly DNA and (+) sense RNA
  • Naked form has no envelope, enveloped form adds a lipid envelope and glycoprotein spikes
Helical
  • Spiral or cylindrical, forming a helix
  • Enveloped (−) sense RNA
  • Naked form shows a coiled nucleocapsid, enveloped form adds envelope and spikes
Complex
  • Lacks the symmetry features of common viral capsids
  • Poxvirus — dumbbell shaped, flanked by bilateral bodies inside
  • Rabies — bullet shaped
EXAM TRAP From the trans answer key: in human virology every helical capsid virus is enveloped (rhabdo, paramyxo, filo, corona). Naked helical capsids exist in plant viruses such as tobacco mosaic virus, and human viruses with naked capsids are exclusively icosahedral.

B. Polarity

Polarity describes how the viral genome interacts with the host cell's own replication machinery. The reference point is the reading frame of mRNA, which is (+) sense. Three types exist: positive sense (+), negative sense (−), and ambisense (+, −). A (+) ssRNA strand is the "sense" or "plus-strand", a (−) ssRNA strand is the "antisense" or "minus-strand".

5. Baltimore Classification (Groups I–VII)

MUST KNOWPages 6–9, 19

Core idea

  • In 1971, David Baltimore founded the Baltimore Classification, which sorts viruses into families by type of genome and method of replication.
  • It has 7 groups. It remains highly useful for teaching but is unlikely to be useful as a top rank in official virus taxonomy.
  • MUSTAll viruses must direct the synthesis of mRNA to produce proteins, and all viral protein synthesis is completely dependent on the translational machinery of the cell.
  • Each group reaches mRNA differently, but the goal is always the same two things: produce viral proteins from viral mRNA and replicate its own genome. If genome and host are both (+), the genome is used directly as mRNA. If the genome is (−), an extra step is added to match host polarity.

6. Viral Replication Cycle and Growth Curve

MUST KNOWPages 9–11
MNEMONIC FROM THE PDF · SIX STEPS = "APUSAR" Attachment → Penetration → Uncoating → Synthesis → Assembly → Release
MECHANISM · FULL REPLICATION CYCLE WITH THE STEP-SPECIFIC POINT — Pages 9–11, Figures 33–41
1. Attachment / adsorption — viral proteins (spikes) on the capsid or phospholipid envelope interact with specific receptors on the host cell surface, and this specificity determines the host range or tissue tropism (examples: SARS, HIV) ↓ 2. Penetration — the virus enters the host cell by direct fusion or by viropexis ↓ 3. Uncoating — capsid is degraded or removed by viral or host enzymes, releasing the nucleic acid, and this is the start of the eclipse phase ↓ 4. Synthesis — replication of the viral genome plus protein synthesis by transcription and translation, which is the basis of the Baltimore Classification ↓ 5. Assembly — structural proteins, genomes, and viral enzymes are assembled into virus particles, enclosing the genome in the capsid ↓ 6. Release — budding (creates enveloped viruses from the host cell membrane) or lysis (most naked viruses), and lysis can result in apoptosis with host cell death

Penetration: the two methods

Direct fusion with the host cell membrane
  • Used by some enveloped viruses
  • The virus adsorbs and fuses with the host cell membrane, then releases its nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm
Viropexis = receptor-mediated endocytosis
  • Used by several enveloped and ALL naked viruses
  • The cell membrane invaginates, enclosing the virus in endocytotic vesicles (endosomes) — the cell has engulfed the virus by endocytosis

Uncoating and the eclipse phase

EXAM TRAP Uncoating marks the START of the eclipse phase, the period when infectious particles cannot be recovered from the collected specimen. A negative culture during this window does not mean there is no virus. The trans notes this is similar to the bacterial growth curve.

A. Abortive infection

  • The host cell is non-permissive → no replication, no virions produced, and the infecting virus is defective.
  • Example: Hepatitis D (Delta RNA virus). In the absence of hepatitis B it CANNOT replicate, so it is called a "virozoid" or "satellite" and its replication is incompetent. Inside hepatitis B it can replicate.

B. Lytic infection

  • Virus replicates and produces progeny → cell death and cytopathic effects, with inhibition of cellular protein and nucleic acid synthesis.
  • Evidence that establishes viral infection: thickening (swelling), presence of inclusion bodies, and syncytial bodies (cell fusion of the infected host cell).

C. Persistent infection — three types

Viral transformation
Viral infection or gene product induces unregulated cellular growth, forming tumors in the host, caused by oncogenic viruses.
Chronic infection
Low level of viral production without immune clearance. Example: hepatitis B or hepatitis C.
Latent infection
Viral genetic material remains in the host cell and has been incorporated into the host genome, and may be activated later. Example: varicella zoster virus (HSV-3), dormant in the nerve ganglia.

12. Commonly Confused Concepts

MUST KNOWPages 2–20
DO NOT CONFUSE · ENVELOPED vs NAKED Enveloped: 3 parts, envelope from the host cell membrane, labile to ether and alcohol, direct contact, cannot survive gastric acid, released by budding. Naked: 2 parts, resistant, fecal-oral, can survive gastric acid, released by lysis.
Fast distinction: if the stem mentions survival in the gut or in the environment, the answer is naked.
DO NOT CONFUSE · (+) ssRNA vs (−) ssRNA (+) ssRNA: same polarity as mRNA, genome is infectious and translated immediately, no packaged polymerase needed. (−) ssRNA: antisense, genome is NOT infectious, must carry RNA-dependent RNA polymerase in the virion.
Fast distinction: "ready-to-use genome" = (+), "brings its own polymerase" = (−).
DO NOT CONFUSE · dsDNA (Group I) vs dsRNA (Group III) dsDNA: the (+) strand is transcribed straight into mRNA, no intermediate. dsRNA: the (+) strand cannot act as mRNA because it is hydrogen-bonded to the (−) strand, so the virus needs RNA-dependent RNA polymerase as transcriptase and replicase.
Fast distinction: both are double stranded, but only the DNA one flows conventionally.
DO NOT CONFUSE · GROUP VI vs GROUP VII RETROVIRUSES Group VI: genome is (+) ssRNA, Retroviridae, HIV and HTLV, RNA → dsDNA by reverse transcriptase → integrase inserts it into the host genome. Group VII: genome is gapped dsDNA, Hepadnaviridae, hepatitis B, dsDNA → ssRNA intermediate → reverse transcriptase back to dsDNA.
Fast distinction: both use reverse transcriptase, but VI starts with RNA and VII starts and ends with DNA. Both are enveloped, icosahedral, replicating in the nucleus.
DO NOT CONFUSE · HEMADSORPTION vs HEMAGGLUTINATION Hemadsorption: infected cells in culture acquire the ability to stick to mammalian RBCs. Hemagglutination: free virus clumps RBCs in a well, positive = diffuse mat, negative = button or dot.
Fast distinction: cell sheet involved = adsorption, well or tube only = agglutination.
DO NOT CONFUSE · SYNCYTIUM vs BALLOONING Syncytium: infected cell fuses with neighbors into one multinucleated enlarged cell (RSV, measles, and mumps in Figure 47). Ballooning: the single cell loses its shape and rounds up (enterovirus 71, HSV).
Fast distinction: fusion of many cells versus swelling of one cell.
DO NOT CONFUSE · ABORTIVE vs LATENT vs CHRONIC Abortive: non-permissive cell or defective virus, no virions at all (hepatitis D without hepatitis B). Latent: genome incorporated and dormant, may reactivate later (VZV in nerve ganglia). Chronic: continuous low-level viral production without immune clearance (hepatitis B or C).
Fast distinction: no production = abortive, no production now but genome is parked = latent, small steady production = chronic.
DO NOT CONFUSE · ECLIPSE PERIOD vs LATENT PERIOD (growth curve) Eclipse (phase 2): no virus detectable inside the cell while components are being synthesized, and it begins at uncoating. Latent period (phase 4): a plateau where no additional increase in yield occurs after 100–10,000 virions per cell.
Fast distinction: eclipse is early and empty, the latent plateau is late and full.
DO NOT CONFUSE · PRIMARY vs SEMICONTINUOUS vs CONTINUOUS CULTURE Primary: straight from tissue, limited lifespan, very expensive, vaccine production. Semicontinuous: transferred from primary cells, limited passages, also vaccine production. Continuous: cancer or immortalized cells, grows indefinitely, used for serologic antigens and neutralizing antibody assays.
Fast distinction: vaccine production = primary or semicontinuous, antigen and antibody assays = continuous.
DO NOT CONFUSE · HBsAg vs HBcAg (as taught in this lecture) HBsAg = acute stage. HBcAg = chronic stage. Hepatitis B antigens include HBsAg, HBcAg, and HBeAg, and the antibodies present tell you whether the person is immune, acute, or chronic.
Fast distinction: surface for acute, core for chronic, per Page 5 of this trans.

13. Exam Traps

MUST KNOWPages 2–20
TRAP 1 · THE ENVELOPE SOURCE EXCEPTION All enveloped viruses take the envelope from the host cell membrane, EXCEPT Herpes → nuclear membrane. The appendix adds the matching release detail: Herpesviridae buds from the nuclear membrane, not the plasma membrane.
TRAP 2 · SITE-OF-REPLICATION EXCEPTIONS RUN IN OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS DNA = nucleus, except Pox (cytoplasm). RNA = cytoplasm, except influenza and retroviruses (nucleus). Reversing the exception is the easiest wrong answer to fall for. Note the lecture's own inconsistency flagged in section 11 — Reoviridae appears as an RNA exception only in summary Table 19.
TRAP 3 · ALL NAKED VIRUSES USE VIROPEXIS Penetration by viropexis (receptor-mediated endocytosis) is used by several enveloped AND ALL naked viruses. Direct fusion is limited to some enveloped viruses. So "naked viruses fuse directly" is false.
TRAP 4 · A (+) GENOME THAT IS NOT USED AS mRNA Retroviruses (Group VI) have (+) ssRNA but do NOT use it as mRNA. It is reverse transcribed and integrated into the host genome instead. Group IV is the group that reads its genome directly.
TRAP 5 · dsRNA CANNOT SERVE AS mRNA The reason is mechanical, not polarity: the (+) strand is hydrogen-bonded to the (−) strand. This is why Group III must carry RNA-dependent RNA polymerase even though a (+) strand is present.
TRAP 6 · NO NEGATIVE-SENSE DNA EXISTS There is no (−) ssDNA virus and therefore no Baltimore group for one. Also remember Group II and Group III each have only one family (Parvoviridae, Reoviridae).
TRAP 7 · SEGMENTED MEANS RNA, ALWAYS All known viruses with segmented genomes are RNA viruses — no segmented DNA virus has been discovered. Counts to keep: influenza 8, Hanta 3, Lassa 2. Rhabdoviruses are not segmented, which is the usual distractor.
TRAP 8 · CD4+ DIRECTION IN HIV The lecture audio says CD4+ T cells increase — the trans flags this as an erratum. HIV causes a progressive LOSS of CD4+ T cells, leading to AIDS and opportunistic infections.
TRAP 9 · HEPATITIS D IS DEFECTIVE, NOT LYTIC HDV without HBV = abortive infection, since it needs HBV (HBsAg) to coat its virion. It is called a virozoid or satellite and is not a virus family, yet it is classified under Group V ssRNA (−) with a circular RNA genome.
TRAP 10 · FILTRATION DOES NOT REMOVE VIRUSES At 20–300 nm viruses pass through bacterial filters, so filtration alone is not enough for removal in water. Extra procedures must be added.
TRAP 11 · UNCOATING, NOT ATTACHMENT, STARTS THE ECLIPSE Eclipse begins at uncoating, and during it infectious particles cannot be recovered from the specimen. Do not equate a negative culture with absence of virus.
TRAP 12 · DRIFT vs SHIFT WORDING New HA and NA proteins appearing suddenly during an outbreak = antigenic shift by segment reassortment. Point mutations = antigenic drift with minor seasonal variants. Genetic recombination and lysogeny are distractors in this trans.
TRAP 13 · HELICAL IN HUMANS MEANS ENVELOPED Human viruses with helical capsids are all enveloped, and human naked viruses are exclusively icosahedral. Naked helical capsids belong to plant viruses such as tobacco mosaic virus.
TRAP 14 · TEST TARGETS PCR and RT-PCR detect nucleic acid. Rapid antigen tests detect viral surface proteins such as HA or NA. ELISA and Western blot detect proteins or host antibodies. Only one of these is a direct nucleic acid method.

19. Drift vs shift

Antigenic drift

Antigenic shift

Answer out loud, then open to check.

Why are viruses called acellular and not "living"?
They have no cell organelles and no metabolic processes, which living cells require.
Which microscope is required, and why not a compound microscope?
Electron microscope (electron beams). A compound microscope uses visible light, and viruses cannot be shown by Gram stain or light microscopy.
Virion size range, and the practical problem it creates?
20–300 nm, so they pass through bacterial filters and filtration alone is not enough for removal in water.
Which bacteria share the obligate intracellular habit?
The atypical bacteria Rickettsia, Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia.
What does interferon actually do?
Activated by the IFN gene, it is released and signals the neighboring host cell to produce antiviral proteins that destroy viral particles, keeping the virus from multiplying.
Which body set the virus taxonomy, and in what year?
The ICTV, with names established in 1966.
Give the suffixes for order, family, subfamily, and genus.
-virales, -viridae, -virinae, -virus. Species is generally "(disease) virus".
How many parts does a naked virion have versus an enveloped one?
Naked = 2 (capsid plus nucleic acid). Enveloped = 3 (capsid, nucleic acid, envelope with protruding spikes).
Where does the envelope come from, and what is the single exception?
From the host cell membrane during budding and release. Herpes takes it from the nuclear membrane.
Name the four naked DNA and four naked RNA families.
DNA: Parvo, Papilloma, Polyoma, Adeno. RNA: Picorna, Reo, Calici, Hepe.
Naked versus enveloped: sensitivity, transmission, gastric acid?
Naked = resistant to ether and environmental factors, fecal-oral, can withstand gastric acidity. Enveloped = sensitive and labile, direct contact, cannot withstand gastric acidity.
What are peplomers, and what two jobs do they have?
Viral proteins inserted in the envelope, important for attachment (spike, hemagglutinin, neuraminidase) and induction of protective immunity. Jawetz: virus-encoded glycoprotein projections that determine antigenic characteristics.
Influenza attachment protein and its host receptor?
Hemagglutinin (HA) binds the glycan chain on the host cell, and RBCs also carry an HA receptor site. Neuraminidase (NA) is the other surface enzyme.
SARS-CoV-1 and SARS-CoV-2 receptor and co-receptor?
Spike protein → ACE2 receptor, with TMPRSS2 as co-receptor.
HIV spike, receptor, and co-receptors, and what happens to CD4+?
gp120 → CD4+ receptor, co-receptors CCR5 and CXCR4. CD4+ T cells are progressively lost, leading to AIDS and opportunistic infections. The lecture's statement that CD4+ increases is flagged as an erratum.
Three capsid symmetries and the genome each favors?
Icosahedral (20 flat sides) = mostly DNA and (+) sense RNA. Helical = enveloped (−) sense RNA. Complex = pox (dumbbell) and rabies (bullet).
Which two enzymes are named in the lecture, and what do they do?
RNA polymerase copies DNA into RNA during transcription. Reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy of viral RNA in retroviruses.
Hepatitis B antigens, and the stage each indicates in this lecture?
HBsAg, HBcAg, HBeAg. HBsAg = acute stage, HBcAg = chronic stage.
Segmented-genome mnemonic and the segment counts?
BOAR — Bunyaviruses (Hanta, 3), Orthomyxoviruses (influenza, 8), Arenaviruses (Lassa, 2), Reovirus (rotavirus). All segmented viruses are RNA viruses.
Which genome type does not exist?
Negative sense DNA. There is no (−) ssDNA virus.
Why is (−) ssRNA non-infectious on its own?
It is antisense to mRNA and does not encode mRNA, so it must carry virion-associated RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to transcribe a (+) mRNA before translation.
Ambisense definition and two families?
A segmented RNA strand carrying both (+) and (−) regions in the same strand, mostly (−). Arenaviridae (Lassa) and Bunyaviridae (Hanta).
Who founded the Baltimore Classification, when, and on what basis?
David Baltimore, 1971, based on type of genome and method of replication, in 7 groups.
Which two Baltimore groups have only one family each?
Group II = Parvoviridae and Group III = Reoviridae.
Why can dsRNA's (+) strand not act as mRNA?
Because it is hydrogen-bonded to the (−) strand, unlike dsDNA.
Group VI mechanism in one chain?
(+) ssRNA → reverse transcriptase → ssDNA then dsDNA → integrase splices it into the host genome → host transcribes and translates. The (+) RNA is not used as mRNA.
Why is hepatitis B called a "gapped" virus, and how does it replicate?
Its dsDNA genome has single stranded segments. The gaps are filled to form a closed circle, it replicates via an ssRNA intermediate, then reverse transcriptase converts RNA back to dsDNA.
The six replication steps in order?
APUSAR — attachment, penetration, uncoating, synthesis, assembly, release.
Two penetration methods, and which viruses use each?
Direct fusion with the host cell membrane — some enveloped viruses. Viropexis (receptor-mediated endocytosis) — several enveloped and ALL naked viruses.
Which step starts the eclipse phase, and what defines it?
Uncoating. During eclipse, infectious particles cannot be recovered from the collected specimen and no virus is detectable inside the cell while components are synthesized.
Site of replication rules with exceptions?
DNA viruses in the nucleus except poxviruses. RNA viruses in the cytoplasm except orthomyxoviruses (influenza) and retroviruses (HIV, HTLV, hepatitis B).
Exponential phase duration and plateau yield?
Exponential rise for 8–72 hours, plateau yield of 100–10,000 virions per cell.
Which pattern of infection is hepatitis D alone, and what is it called?
Abortive infection in a non-permissive setting — a defective virus called a "virozoid" or "satellite", which can only replicate inside hepatitis B.
Three types of persistent infection with an example each?
Viral transformation (oncogenic viruses, unregulated growth, tumors), chronic infection (hepatitis B or C, low-level production without immune clearance), latent infection (VZV / HSV-3 dormant in nerve ganglia, reactivated later).
Match the CPE: syncytium, pink cytoplasmic inclusions, nuclear inclusions with stranding, grape-like clusters.
Paramyxovirus (syncytium and faint basophilic cytoplasmic inclusions), poxvirus (pink eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions with swelling), herpesvirus (cytoplasmic stranding and nuclear inclusions), adenovirus (enlargement, rounding, grape-like clusters).
Culture type used for serologic antigens and neutralizing antibody assays?
Continuous culture, for example HeLa, derived from the tissues of Henrietta Lacks, or A549 from human lung carcinoma.
Hemadsorption versus hemagglutination, and how a negative hemagglutination looks?
Hemadsorption = infected cells acquire the ability to stick to mammalian RBCs. Hemagglutination = HA on free virus clumps RBCs. A negative result appears as a dot or button at the bottom of the well or tube.
PCR steps, and what chick embryo inoculation detects?
PCR: denaturing, annealing, extending. Chick embryo detects pocks on the CAM, hemagglutination (influenza), and inclusion bodies (Negri bodies), with 0.2 ml injected into amniotic and allantoic fluid. CAM inoculation is used for suspected herpes simplex or pox virus, and pocks appear as white dots.
