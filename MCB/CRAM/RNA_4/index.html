Viral replication cycleMUST KNOWMECHANISMPages 1–2
Core idea. The same polymerase makes separate mRNAs (stop-start at each gene) until enough N protein accumulates. N protein coats the new RNA. The polymerase then ignores the stop-start signals and copies the whole genome. That is the switch from transcription to replication.

Whole cycle in order MECHANISM

  1. Attachment. G protein binds host cell receptors. Binds neurons best but can use receptors on many other cells (e.g. muscle cells).
  2. Endocytosis. Virus enters inside an endosome.
  3. Fusion. Internal virion components are released into the cytoplasm.
  4. M protein dissociates from the nucleocapsid → exposes the ribonucleoprotein core. Important for viral RNA synthesis to occur.
  5. Primary transcription by the transcriptase complex (L + P), particularly the RdRp. Encapsidated RNA is the template.
  6. Leader RNA made first, then capped, poly-A mRNAs for N → P → M → G → L.
  7. Translation. Ribosomes bind the mRNAs. N protein is made in the highest amount.
  8. Switch to replication. Enough N protein → encapsidates the leader → polymerase ignores junctions → full-length encapsidated antigenome (+).
  9. Trailer promoter on the antigenome → trailer RNA encapsidated → full-length encapsidated (−) genome, identical to the original.
  10. Secondary transcription from progeny genomes. New strands pick up newly made L and P proteins.
  11. Assembly and budding at the plasma membrane → new virion ready to infect new cells.

Transcription rules MUST KNOW

  • Transcription starts at the 3′ end. The first product is the leader. It has no cap and no poly-A tail but has an encapsidation signal (important for replication).
  • 4 intergenic regions sit between the protein-coding genes. They vary in length and sequence and are NOT transcribed.
  • Each junction has a gene end sequence (for the upstream gene) and a gene start sequence (for the downstream gene). This start-stop system regulates the L + P transcriptase and keeps transcription sequential, in genome order.
  • Capping signal adds a cap at initiation of each mRNA. At an intergenic region the polymerase gets the polyadenylation signal → adds poly-A tail → terminates that mRNA → then initiates, caps, and methylates the next mRNA.

  • Fewer polymerases reach the end of the genome → progressively fewer transcripts downstream. N = most abundant. L = least abundant.
  • A general feature of non-segmented, negative-strand RNA viruses.
  • Ensures high structural proteins (nucleocapsid) and low enzymatic proteins (L). The abundance of N mRNA is intentional, not accidental.
  • Much of the N protein accumulates in the cytoplasm and is never used for replication.

Switch from transcription to replication MECHANISM

  1. Enough N protein is produced
  2. N binds the 5′ end of the product RNA where the encapsidation signal lies → leader is encapsidated and elongated
  3. Polymerase ignores intergenic start-stop sequences
  4. Full-length, continuous, encapsidated antigenome (complementary to genome, positive-sense replicative intermediate). Encapsidated as it is synthesized.
  5. 3′ end of the antigenome = trailer section = antigenome promoter → makes trailer RNA
  6. Trailer RNA has an encapsidation signal at its 5′ end that is even more efficient than the leader's
  7. With enough N → full-length, encapsidated, minus-strand RNA identical to the original genome
KEY PRINCIPLE The dependence of the RNA polymerase on encapsidation of the nascent RNA is a fundamental principle of replication in non-segmented, negative-stranded RNA viruses. Encapsidation regulates the switch from mRNA synthesis to antigenome synthesis.

Assembly and budding SHOULD KNOW

G made by ribosomes on rough ER, inserted in type 1 orientation→ER–Golgi transport→Final destination: plasma membrane→G trimers form clusters or micro-domains (independent of M)→M protein binds nucleocapsid and the cytoplasmic tail of G trimers→Budding virion released

Individual components are assembled in different parts of the cell, as with most viruses.

COMMON CONFUSION Leader RNA comes from the genome 3′ end, is the first transcript, and is uncapped with no poly-A. Trailer RNA comes from the antigenome promoter and carries the more efficient encapsidation signal that leads to new (−) genomes.

Pathogenesis: neuroinvasion and spreadMUST KNOWMECHANISMPage 3
Core idea. Virus in saliva enters by bite or scratch, waits near the wound, climbs nerves toward the CNS, spreads through the brain, then travels outward along nerves to the salivary glands so the animal can transmit it.
  • Found in large quantity in the saliva of infected animals.
  • Transmission is almost exclusively by inoculation of infected saliva through a bite or scratch from a rabid mammal.
  • Transmission rate increases with multiple bites and bites in highly innervated parts (face and hands).

Mechanism of neuroinvasion and spread MECHANISM

  1. Bite. Incubation usually 20–90 days. Can be as short as a few days or longer than 1 year.
  2. During incubation the virus stays at or close to the inoculation site.
  3. In muscle, binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on postsynaptic membranes at neuromuscular junctions.
  4. Centripetal spread along peripheral nerves toward spinal cord or brainstem via retrograde fast axonal transport. Rate up to ~250 mm/day, with delays of ~12 h at each synapse.
  5. Enters the CNS.
  6. Rapid dissemination to other CNS regions via fast axonal transport along neuroanatomic connections.
  7. Centrifugal spread along sensory and autonomic nerves to salivary glands, heart, adrenal glands, and skin. Replicates in acinar cells of salivary glands → secreted in saliva of rabid animals (the vectors).
EXAM TRAP There is no well-documented hematogenous spread of rabies. Spread is along nerves.
COMMON CONFUSION Centripetal = toward the CNS, retrograde, happens first. Centrifugal = away from the CNS to organs, happens after CNS infection is established.

Negri body

  • Eosinophilic cytoplasmic inclusions in brain neurons

Immunologic response and immune evasionMUST KNOWMECHANISMPages 3–4
Core idea. Normally, RIG-I senses viral RNA and drives an interferon response. Rabies uses all four structural proteins (N, P, M, G) to block that response, so the virus reaches the nervous system with little immune resistance.

Normal antiviral response MECHANISM

  1. RIG-I (retinoic acid inducible gene-I) detects viral RNA
  2. Activation of kinases
  3. Triggers interferon regulatory factor (IRF/IFN) production
  4. IRF3 / IRF7 activation
  5. Drives IFN-β production
  6. JAK-STAT signaling → ISG expression (enters the nucleus)
  7. Induces antiviral effectors

Other responses SHOULD KNOW Infection also triggers inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, autophagy, and apoptosis to control spread.

Infection
  • NF-κB pathway and chemokines → cytokine production, immune cell recruitment
  • Dendritic cell and macrophage activation, antigen presentation → initiate adaptive immunity

Control, immunization, PEP, and PrEPMUST KNOWPages 4–5
Core idea. Because rabies is always fatal once symptoms appear, vaccination is the cornerstone of prevention. Exposure category decides whether you give nothing, vaccine alone, or vaccine plus rabies immunoglobulin (RIG).

Control measures listed SHOULD KNOW

Human rabies vaccine, diagnostic Negri body, rabies vaccines for dogs, rabies immunoglobulin, culture of rabies virus, diagnostic fluorescent antibody tests.

Immunization

  • Vaccine stimulates a strong cellular immune response: CD4 helper cells and T cells.
  • Plasmablasts and memory cells appear early and correlate with rising antibody levels.
  • Booster dose → faster, stronger reaction and durable protection.
  • Human rabies vaccines are inactivated vaccines that can be used for post-exposure prophylaxis. They cannot treat clinical rabies Pages 17–18, Q3.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY Part I. Rabies

  • Rhabdoviridae, Lyssavirus. Bullet-shaped, enveloped, −ssRNA, non-segmented, 5 proteins N P M G L.
  • Killed by UV, heat, lipid solvents, trypsin, detergents, pH extremes (fragile envelope).
  • First transcript = leader (no cap, no poly-A). Intergenic regions are not transcribed.
  • Transcription attenuation → N mRNA most, L mRNA least.
  • Enough N protein → encapsidation → antigenome → new (−) genome.
  • G protein = attachment, neuroinvasiveness, pathogenicity. Single serotype.
  • Binds nicotinic ACh receptors at NMJ → retrograde fast axonal transport (~250 mm/d) → CNS → centrifugal to salivary glands.
  • No hematogenous spread. Not isolated from patient blood.
  • Negri bodies: pathognomonic, eosinophilic cytoplasmic, Purkinje and hippocampal pyramidal neurons, ~80%.
  • Evasion: N hides RNA from RIG-I, P blocks IRF3/7 and IFN-β, M dampens genes, G ↓ T cells and ↑ IL-10.
  • Earliest specific symptom: paresthesia or pain/pruritus at the bite site.
  • Encephalitic 80% (hydrophobia, aerophobia, lucid intervals) vs paralytic 20% (flaccid weakness, mistaken for GBS).
  • No test before symptoms. No established treatment.
  • Cat 1 wash only. Cat 2 vaccine. Cat 3 vaccine + RIG. Head and neck Cat 2 → Cat 3.
  • HRIG 20 IU/kg, ERIG 40 IU/kg. PrEP day 0 and 7.

Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis (SSPE)

  • Chronic nonpermissive infection with measles virus. Nonpermissive = present in brain tissue but does not allow full viral replication.

Progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML)MUST KNOWPages 5, 11
  • Caused by the JC virus (Polyomaviridae family).
  • Occurs in immunosuppressed individuals. Also a complication of therapeutic monoclonal antibody use.
  • CNS demyelination from reactivation and replication of JC virus in immunocompromised patients.
  • Fatal brain disease. About 5% of AIDS patients develop PML Page 11.

  • Slow virus = chronic, persistent, long asymptomatic period.
  • Conventional: SSPE, PML. Unconventional: kuru, CJD.

Characteristics of prions and TSEsMUST KNOWPage 6
Core idea. A prion is a proteinaceous infectious particle that lacks nucleic acid. Disease comes from PrPSc, a misfolded form of the normal cellular protein PrPC, which forces more PrPC to misfold and accumulate in the brain.
  • Folded proteins that undergo self-propagation. Epigenetically heritable.
  • Only known transmissible pathogens without nucleic acid.
  • Causes: scrapie, mad cow disease, Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (CJD).
  • Manifests as infectious, genetic, or sporadic disease. Genetic = mutation in the PRNP gene. Sporadic = no clear cause.
  • PrPSc = scrapie isoform of the prion protein, a misfolded PrPC. Accumulates in brain → damages neural tissue.
  • Distinct strains have different biologic properties because each folds in a slightly different way.

Prion pathophysiology and diseasesMUST KNOWMECHANISMPage 6

Pathophysiology of spongiform encephalopathy MECHANISM

  1. PrPC synthesis and transport. Made in the ER → Golgi → cell surface (plasma membrane).
  2. Attachment. At the plasma membrane, PrPC is attached to a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchor.
  3. Initiation of misfolding. At the cell surface or inside endocytic vesicles, PrPSc contacts PrPC and forces it to change shape (protein-only hypothesis).
  4. Misfolded clusters. Misfolded PrP joins other clusters → oligomers of PrPSc. Lipid raft microdomains and cofactors such as sulfated glycosaminoglycans or proteoglycans make misfolding more efficient.
  5. Accumulation and deposition. PrPSc builds up in lysosomes, stays in the plasma membrane, or deposits outside the cell as amyloid fibrils and plaques.
  6. Consequence. Accumulation in nervous tissue → neurodegeneration.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY Part III. Prions

  • Prion = protein, no nucleic acid. Only transmissible pathogen without nucleic acid.
  • Disease = PrPC → PrPSc misfolding (protein-only hypothesis).
  • Infectious, genetic (PRNP mutation), or sporadic.
  • Extreme resistance to heat, disinfectants, irradiation. Killed by phenol, periodate, NaOH, NaOCl.
  • No immune or inflammatory response, no interferon.
  • Cannot be cultured in vitro. Incubation up to 35 years.
  • Kuru = ritual cannibalism, Papua New Guinea.
  • CJD = dementia, ataxia, myoclonus. Iatrogenic: growth hormone, instruments, dura mater grafts.
  • No treatment, always fatal.

General features of viral carcinogenesis and tumor virus typesMUST KNOWPages 6–7
Core idea. Viruses cause at least 15–20% of human tumors worldwide (HPV, EBV, HHV8, hepatitis B and C, HIV, HTLV), but infection alone is rarely enough. Persistence, host genetics, and immunity decide whether cancer follows.

12 general features, grouped for recall

About the virus
  • Can cause cancer in animals and humans (1)
  • Strains differ in oncogenic potential (6, 8)
  • Direct (integrate into host genome, drive growth) or indirect (chronic inflammation, tissue damage). Both disrupt growth control (7, 9)
  • One virus → more than one tumor type (HPV → oropharyngeal and cervical) (12)
About the host and time
  • Frequently establish persistent infections (2)
  • Host genetic and immune factors are important determinants (3)
  • Infections are more common than tumors. Viruses are seldom complete carcinogens (4)
  • Long latent periods between infection and tumor (5)
About the evidence
  • Animal models reveal mechanisms applicable to humans (10)
  • Viral markers are usually present in tumor cells (11)

General life cycle MECHANISM

  1. Attachment. Viral proteins bind receptor and co-receptor → fusion with cell membrane. HIV-1: receptor CD4, co-receptor CCR5 or CXCR4.

  4. Integration. Integrase inserts viral DNA into the host genome → provirus.
  5. Transcription. Host machinery transcribes provirus → genomic RNA and mRNAs.
  6. Translation. Proteins made in the cytosol. Env proteins inserted into the ER membrane.
  7. Assembly at the plasma membrane.
  8. Budding off. Immature particles released.
  9. Maturation. Viral protease cleaves Gag → fully infectious virion.

Replication steps SHOULD KNOW

  1. Attachment. Docks on extracellular matrix or basement membrane via heparan sulfate proteoglycans. Host enzymes reshape and free it → moves to membrane areas with entry receptors.
  2. Entry. Endocytosis (similar to macropinocytosis). Uses the actin network → early endosome.
  3. Early endosome. Kept there by CD63 and RAB5. Acidic environment starts capsid breakdown → viral DNA stays with L2. Then recycling endosomes or late endosomes/multivesicular bodies.
  4. Uncoating and trafficking. Secretase cleaves L2. L2 helps DNA cross the endosomal membrane and recruits the retromer complex. Retromer + RAB guide DNA-L2 to the trans-Golgi network. Leftover L1 goes to lysosomes.
  5. Transport to nucleus. DNA-L2 travels on microtubules. During cell division the DNA slips into the mitotic nucleus → PML nuclear bodies → replication begins.
  6. Replication and persistence. Early proteins (e.g. E2) attach viral DNA to host chromosomes during division → HPV copies its DNA every time the host cell divides.

  • Lymphocyte-tropic strains → highly productive infection
  • Macrophage-tropic strains → restricted replication
Disease course
  • Long incubation (months to years)
  • Persists despite antibodies
  • Frequent env mutations → new antigenic variants
  • Faster disease in children (exception to long incubation)

Monocytes and macrophages as "Trojan horses"

  • Can infect non-dividing, terminally differentiated cells. Replication begins only once the cells are activated.
  • Only ~1 in 1,000,000 monocytes are infected, but they carry virus throughout the body hidden from the immune system and seed other tissues.

Other lentivirus examples with faster disease: horses with infectious anemia, young goats with encephalitis.

Host factors influencing pathogenesis SHOULD KNOW: age (young at higher risk), stress (can trigger disease), genetics (susceptibility varies), concurrent infections (worsen progression).

HIV receptors MECHANISM

HIV needs 2 receptors
  • 1. CD4 on lymphocytes and macrophages
  • 2. Coreceptor (chemokine receptor) → activates gp41 → fusion with host membrane
    • CCR5 for macrophage-tropic strains. CCR5 mutations → natural resistance or delayed progression. This discovery led to the first entry inhibitor drug in 2003
    • CXCR4 for lymphocyte-tropic strains
  • These receptors are also found in tissues such as the colon, cervix, and neurons.
  • α4β7 integrin helps HIV infection in the gut.
  • DC-SIGN on dendritic cells does not mediate entry but transports HIV to lymphoid tissues → enhances T cell infection.

Pathogenesis: course of untreated HIVMUST KNOWMECHANISMPages 15–16
Core idea. Untreated HIV spans about 10 years. Viral load spikes early, is partly controlled, then surges again as CD4 cells become nearly depleted. The "latent" phase is clinically quiet but virologically intense.

Timeline MECHANISM

  1. Primary infection. Mucosal entry → initial viremia (4–11 days to reach bloodstream). Viremia detectable for 8–12 weeks. Dissemination to lymphoid organs.
  2. Acute phase (3–6 weeks post-infection). Mononucleosis-like illness in 50–75%. Sharp drop in CD4 T cells.
  3. Immune response (1 week to 3 months). Plasma viremia declines. CD4 count partially rebounds. Virus persists in lymph nodes because the immune system cannot eliminate it.
  4. Clinical latency (~10 years average). Patient may look healthy but has intense viral activity: ~10 billion particles produced and destroyed daily. CD4 T cells are the main producers, with an average half-life of 1.6 days once infected.
  5. Progression. Elevated viral expression → clinical disease.
  6. AIDS. Constitutional symptoms, opportunistic infections, neoplasms. Plasma viral load rises. Late strains are more virulent and cytopathic. Shift from M-tropic → T-tropic marks the transition. Death usually within 2 years of symptom onset if untreated.
Primary infection
Rapid replication, sharp viral RNA rise, acute HIV syndrome, wide dissemination, seeding of lymphoid organs, steep CD4 drop
Clinical latency
Immune system partly controls virus, viral load declines. CD4 stabilizes below normal. Lasts several years. HIV keeps replicating in lymphoid tissue
Constitutional symptoms
CD4 gradually falls. Weight loss, fevers, night sweats. Further decline → opportunistic infections
Late stage
Viral load surges, CD4 nearly depleted → AIDS, life-threatening opportunistic diseases → death without treatment

Memory cells and the latent reservoir

  • Some infected CD4 cells revert to a resting memory state → latent reservoir. They make no virus but persist for decades → HIV is nearly impossible to cure with standard therapy.
  • On ART: <1 per million CD4 T cells carry provirus, but the reservoir declines extremely slowly (up to 70 years to clear naturally).
  • Reactivation: antigen exposure or stopping ART → infectious virus release.
  • Other possible reservoirs: macrophages, hematopoietic stem cells, brain cells.
  • Berlin patient (2007): functional cure after a bone marrow transplant from a donor with a CCR5 mutation.

Lymphoid organs: "the heart of HIV infection"

  • Contain most lymphocytes. Only 2% of lymphocytes are in the blood.
  • In germinal centers, follicular dendritic cells trap antigens and activate lymphocytes. The same environment is perfect for HIV replication.
  • HIV actively replicates in lymphoid tissue even during latency. Cytokines activate CD4 T cells → ↑ susceptibility.
  • Over time, lymph node structure is destroyed → body can no longer mount effective immune responses.
Clinical findings, viral load, OIs, and cancersMUST KNOWPage 16
  • Acute HIV: nonspecific fatigue, rash, headache, nausea, night sweats. PDF practice Q13 answer: fever and malaise (mononucleosis-like or flu-like syndrome).
  • AIDS hallmark: severe immunosuppression → severe opportunistic infections and cancers like Kaposi sarcoma.
  • "Diarrhea and dwindling" prodrome before full-blown AIDS: fatigue, malaise, weight loss, fever, shortness of breath, chronic diarrhea, hairy leukoplakia, candidiasis, lymphadenopathy.
  • GI involvement is a major cause of debility.
  • Timeline: 8–10 years latency → AIDS → death in ~2 years without treatment.

Plasma viral load

  • Reflects the balance between viral replication and immune destruction. Each patient reaches a viral set point. High set point → faster progression, poorer treatment response.
  • Measured by widely available assays. Used to monitor antiretroviral therapy.

Pediatric AIDS SHOULD KNOW

  • Typically perinatal (during birth). Symptoms by age <2 years → death ~2 years later if untreated. Immature immune system.
  • Findings: interstitial pneumonitis, pneumonia, severe oral candidiasis, encephalopathy, stunting and wasting, generalized lymphadenopathy, sepsis, hepatosplenomegaly, diarrhea, growth retardation.
  • High plasma viral load predicts rapid progression, more aggressive than in adults.

Neurologic disease

  • Common: 40–90% of patients. Syndromes: subacute encephalitis, vacuolar myelopathy, aseptic meningitis, peripheral neuropathy.
  • Most common: AIDS-dementia complex. Late-stage, up to 65% of AIDS patients. Memory loss, poor concentration, apathy, behavioral changes. Average survival <6 months.
  • Children: seizures, developmental delay, encephalopathy, bacterial meningitis, psychiatric risk (anxiety disorders increasingly recognized). Children live longer with ART.

Opportunistic infections

  • Major cause of morbidity and mortality in late-stage HIV. Occur when CD4 <200 cells/μL. Spectrum changes as treatment improves.
  • Opportunistic infections result mainly from loss of cell-mediated immunity (PDF Q14).

HAART (1996)

  • Combination of drugs → HIV became a manageable chronic disease.
  • Suppresses replication to an undetectable plasma viral load, restores immune response, prolongs survival.
  • Does NOT cure HIV because the latent reservoir persists.
  • Monotherapy quickly leads to resistance. Combination delays resistance, but cross-resistance within a drug class can occur.

Challenges SHOULD KNOW

  • Drug resistance is growing, even in newly diagnosed patients. Perinatally infected infants: ~19% resistant strains.
  • Drugs are expensive, lifelong, with side effects (lipodystrophy). Limited access in many regions.
  • Breastfeeding transmission remains a challenge.

Prevention

  • Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) significantly reduces infection risk.
  • Zidovudine (AZT) cuts mother-to-child transmission by up to 70%. Shorter regimens (AZT or nevirapine) for resource-limited settings.
  • Best hope for global control = a safe, effective vaccine. All candidates so far ineffective or partially effective.
Why vaccines are hard
  • Rapid mutation, high env antigen variability
  • Virus not fully cleared by the immune system
  • Unknown correlates of protective immunity
  • Lack of good animal models: chimpanzees do not develop AIDS. Macaques are useful but imperfect
Approaches being tested
  • Recombinant env glycoproteins
  • Viral vectors
  • Gene therapy to make cells resistant to HIV

Patient education

  • Most important: adherence to ART to maintain viral suppression.
  • Regular viral load and CD4 monitoring. Safe sex, no needle sharing. Healthy lifestyle and mental health support. Vaccines and prophylaxis against opportunistic infections. Support systems (counseling, peer groups, stigma reduction).
EXAM TRAP
  • Fusion inhibitor = enfuvirtide. Entry inhibitor (CCR5 blocker) = maraviroc.
  • PDF practice Q14: "circulating neutralizing antibodies prove protection" is false. Antigenic variation lets HIV evade them. Zidovudine does inhibit RNA-dependent DNA polymerase.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY Part VII. HIV

  • HIV = Retroviridae, Lentivirus. Cylindrical, bar-shaped nucleoid is diagnostic on EM.
  • HIV-1 global, groups M N O, chimpanzee. HIV-2 West Africa, sooty mangabey.
  • gag and pol conserved. env highly variable → no vaccine.
  • gp120 binds CD4 + coreceptor, sets tropism, V3 neutralization. gp41 = fusion.
  • Tat transcription. Rev RNA export. Nef ↓ CD4 and MHC-I. Vpr G2 arrest. Vpu CD4 degradation. Vif blocks APOBEC3G.
  • CCR5 = macrophage-tropic, early. CXCR4 = T-tropic, late. DC-SIGN transports, does not mediate entry.
  • Occupational risk: HBV 6–30% > HCV ~1.8% > HIV <0.3%. MTCT 13–40% untreated.
  • Acute phase 3–6 weeks, mono-like in 50–75%. Latency ~10 years with ~10 billion virions daily.
  • Memory CD4 cells = latent reservoir. Berlin patient: CCR5-mutant donor.
  • Viral load predicts long-term outcome. CD4 predicts short-term OI risk. OIs at CD4 <200.
  • Most common neuro: AIDS-dementia complex. CMV retinitis = worst ocular complication.
  • ART cuts KS more than lymphomas. Breast, colon, prostate cancers not increased.
  • Screening ELISA. Western blot phased out. Infants: PCR. p24 reappearance = poor prognosis.
  • HAART (1996) suppresses but does not cure. Adherence is the top education point. AZT cuts MTCT up to 70%.

Encephalitic vs paralytic rabies

Encephalitic
  • 80%, agitation, seizures
  • Clue: hydrophobia, aerophobia
Paralytic
  • 20%, flaccid weakness
  • Clue: mistaken for GBS

Fast distinction: Fear of water = furious. Floppy limbs = paralytic.

Centripetal vs centrifugal spread

Centripetal
  • Toward CNS
  • Clue: retrograde axonal transport
Centrifugal
  • Away from CNS
  • Clue: reaches salivary glands

Fast distinction: In first (to brain), then out (to saliva).

Leader vs trailer RNA

Leader
  • First transcript from genome 3′ end
  • Clue: no cap, no poly-A
Trailer
  • From antigenome promoter
  • Clue: more efficient encapsidation signal

Fast distinction: Leader starts transcription. Trailer makes new (−) genomes.

PEP Category II vs III

Category II
  • Nibbling, scratches without bleeding
  • Clue: vaccine only
Category III
  • Transdermal bites, spontaneous bleeding, mucosa, bats, head and neck
  • Clue: vaccine + RIG

Fast distinction: Blood, mucosa, bats, or head and neck = add RIG.

SSPE vs PML

SSPE
  • Measles, child, 6–8 year latency
  • Clue: CSF anti-measles antibodies
PML
  • JC virus, immunosuppressed
  • Clue: white matter lesions, CSF JCV DNA

Fast distinction: SSPE = measles history. PML = immunosuppression.

PrPC vs PrPSc

PrPC
  • Normal cellular protein
  • Clue: GPI-anchored on cell surface
PrPSc
  • Misfolded scrapie isoform
  • Clue: forces PrPC to change shape

Fast distinction: C = cellular (normal). Sc = scrapie (disease).

Proto-oncogene vs tumor suppressor

Proto-oncogene
  • Promotes growth
  • Clue: overexpression or mutation activates
Tumor suppressor
  • Negative regulator (Rb, p53)
  • Clue: both alleles inactivated

Fast distinction: Gain of function vs loss of both copies.

Highly vs weakly oncogenic retroviruses

Highly (direct transforming)
  • Carries own oncogene from host
  • Clue: quick
Weakly (slowly transforming)
  • No oncogene, indirect
  • Clue: years, e.g. leukemia

Fast distinction: Has oncogene = fast. No oncogene = slow.

DNA vs RNA tumor virus transforming genes

DNA viruses
  • Essential for replication
  • Clue: no cellular homologs
RNA viruses
  • Not needed for replication
  • Clue: transduced cellular oncogenes

Fast distinction: DNA virus genes are viral originals. RNA virus oncogenes are borrowed from cells.

Exogenous vs endogenous retroviruses

Exogenous
  • Horizontal spread, only in infected cells
  • Clue: all pathogenic ones (HIV, HTLV)
Endogenous
  • Inherited, in all cells
  • Clue: usually not pathogenic, cannot transform

Fast distinction: Caught vs inherited.

Ecotropic vs amphotropic vs xenotropic

Eco
  • Own species only
Ampho
  • Broad, receptor widely distributed
Xeno
  • Foreign species, not natural host cultured cells

Fast distinction: Xeno = "foreign only."

HTLV-1 vs HIV

HTLV-1
  • Deltaretrovirus, ATL
  • Clue: tax, no oncogene
HIV
  • Lentivirus, AIDS
  • Clue: cytolytic, cancers indirect

Fast distinction: HTLV transforms T cells. HIV kills CD4 cells.

Polyomavirus vs papillomavirus

Polyoma
  • 45 nm, 5 kbp, 3 structural proteins
  • Clue: T antigens, BK, JC, SV40
Papilloma
  • 55 nm, 8 kbp, 2 structural proteins
  • Clue: E6, E7, keratinocytes

Fast distinction: Papilloma is larger but has fewer structural proteins.

Episomal vs integrated HPV DNA

Episomal
  • Separate from host DNA
  • Clue: benign or premalignant
Integrated
  • Inserted into host DNA
  • Clue: cervical cancer cells

Fast distinction: Integration = cancer.

gp120 vs gp41

gp120 (SU)
  • Binds CD4 + coreceptor
  • Clue: tropism, V3 neutralization
gp41 (TM)
  • Membrane anchor
  • Clue: fusion domain

Fast distinction: 120 attaches. 41 fuses.

CCR5 vs CXCR4

CCR5
  • Macrophage-tropic, early
  • Clue: maraviroc, Berlin patient
CXCR4
  • Lymphocyte-tropic, late
  • Clue: shift marks AIDS

Fast distinction: M-tropic → T-tropic as disease advances.

Viral load vs CD4 count

Viral load
  • Balance of replication and killing
  • Clue: long-term outcome, ART monitoring
CD4 count
  • Immune status
  • Clue: short-term OI risk (<200)

Fast distinction: Load = where the patient is heading. CD4 = danger now.

Genotypic vs phenotypic resistance testing

Genotypic
  • Sequences RT and protease genes
  • Clue: standard, most common
Phenotypic
  • Recombinant virus grown with drugs
  • Clue: complex mutation patterns

Fast distinction: Read the genes vs test the drugs.
