I. Systemic Mycoses — Overview
A. Overview — what makes a mycosis "systemic" p.1

Core idea

Systemic mycoses are fungal infections that affect internal organs and may spread throughout the body, going beyond the initial site of infection. They are caused by two very different groups of fungi.

MUST KNOW
  • Caused by endemic fungi (acquired through inhalation, geographically restricted) or opportunistic fungi (cause disease only in immunocompromised hosts)
  • Unlike superficial/cutaneous mycoses (limited to skin, hair, or nails), systemic mycoses typically begin in the lungs after inhalation of fungal spores → can disseminate to other organs, especially if the host is immunocompromised
  • >90% of cases are from inhalation of conidia. The lungs are the primary site of infection, where conidia convert to:
    • Yeast cells — H. capsulatum, B. dermatitidis, P. brasiliensis
    • Spherules — Coccidioides spp.
  • Despite very high infection rates in endemic areas, ~90% of infections occur in immunocompetent individuals and are asymptomatic or self-limited
  • Compromised cell-mediated (Th1) immunity → significantly greater risk of disseminated disease (lecturer-flagged 📌)
  • Incidence of disseminated disease for all four endemic mycoses is significantly higher in males (lecturer-flagged 📌)
SHOULD KNOW
  • Characterized by distinct geographic areas of distribution and dimorphic environmental molds
  • Serologic tests for serum antibodies may have both diagnostic and prognostic value
Recall cue: Two roads into systemic disease — geography (endemic fungi, inhaled) or immune status (opportunistic fungi). Both usually start in the lungs.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Systemic mycoses = endemic (geography-driven) or opportunistic (immune-status-driven)
  • Lungs are the primary portal of entry for >90% of cases
  • Th1/cell-mediated immunity is the key defense against dissemination
  • Males disseminate more often across all 4 endemic mycoses
II. Endemic Mycoses
Endemic mycoses at a glance + Dimorphism p.1–2

Core idea

Endemic mycoses are geographically restricted infections caused by pathogenic fungi that are consistently present in a particular area. There are 4:

  • Coccidioidomycosis — Coccidioides immitis / posadasii
  • Histoplasmosis — Histoplasma capsulatum
  • Blastomycosis — Blastomyces dermatitidis
  • Paracoccidioidomycosis — Paracoccidioides brasiliensis

Dimorphism — the shared trait of all 4

MUST KNOW
  • Dimorphism = ability of the fungus to change form in response to a stimulus from the host environment — essential for pathogenicity of each organism
  • Temperature-dependent switch:
    • Yeast / single-cell form — in the host, 36–37°C
    • Mycelium / filamentous mold form — in the environment, 25°C
MECHANISM — Dimorphic switch
Environment, 25°C → Mold / mycelium (hyphae + conidia)
⇅ temperature shift on inhalation
Host tissue, 36–37°C → Yeast (or spherule for Coccidioides)
Study aid (not from PDF): "Cold in the ground, hot in the body" — mold in soil, yeast at body temperature.
Recall cue: If a question mentions a fungus that looks different in culture at room temperature vs. in tissue/blood at 37°C — think dimorphic endemic fungus.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • 4 endemic mycoses: Cocci, Histo, Blasto, Para
  • All are thermally dimorphic: mold at 25°C (environment) → yeast/spherule at 37°C (host)
  • Dimorphism is required for virulence
A. Coccidioidomycosis — Coccidioides immitis / posadasii p.2–4

Core idea

Caused by C. immitis or C. posadasii — "sibling species" that are clinically and structurally almost indistinguishable. First described (wrongly) as a protozoan in 1892 in an Argentinian soldier, reclassified as a fungus in the early 1900s.

MUST KNOW
  • Endemic to the Southwestern US, Central America, South America (Lower Sonoran Life Zone — hot desert, high temp, low rainfall)
    • C. immitis → more endemic in California
    • C. posadasii → more frequent in Arizona, Texas, South America
  • Isolated from soil and indigenous rodents, but no evidence of spread by rodents and not communicable person-to-person
  • C. immitis is the most infectious systemic fungus — dimorphic: arthroconidia at 25–30°C, spherules at 37°C
  • The presence of spherules is diagnostic for this pathogen (lecturer-flagged 📌) — spherules up to ~80 μm, contain endospores ~2–5 μm each
  • Arthroconidia are highly resistant to adverse environmental conditions and remain viable for years — combined with high infectivity, this makes it suitable for use as a biological weapon (lecturer-flagged 📌)
  • Peak infections occur in summer and autumn (dry weather → dust with spores. Arthroconidia produced abundantly)
  • Infection is usually self-limited. Dissemination is rare but always serious and fatal when it occurs
SHOULD KNOW
  • Environmental conditions favoring spore growth: temperatures of 100°F, little rainfall, alkaline soil
  • C. immitis produces melanin in mold form — possible role in virulence (may protect from host immune response, may increase antifungal resistance). Role in pathogenesis still under investigation

01 | Mold form (environment, 25–30°C)

  • White to tan colony
  • Hyphae show alternating barrel-shaped arthroconidia separated by a disjunctor cell
  • Hyphae form chains of arthroconidia that fragment into individual, readily airborne arthroconidia that mix with dust and are inhaled

02 | Yeast form — spherule (host, 37°C)

  • Arthroconidia transform into a spherical, large, thick, doubly refractile-walled yeast cell called a spherule
  • Spherule ≈ up to 80 μm, contains endospores ≈ 2–5 μm each

03 | Life cycle (once inhaled)

MECHANISM — C. immitis life cycle
Arthroconidia inhaled → settle in terminal bronchioles → neutrophil/eosinophil inflammatory response (primary infection)
↓ ~Day 3
Arthroconidia develop into immature spherules
↓ Day 4
Spherules mature, divide internally → form hundreds–thousands of endospores
↓ Day 5
Spherules rupture → endospores released into host
↓
Each endospore can develop into a new mature spherule. Macrophages engulf some endospores → recruits lymphocytes/histiocytes → granuloma with giant cells if infection not cleared

04 | Clinical manifestations

  • 60% of cases asymptomatic. Remaining 40% self-limited
  • Self-limiting form = Valley fever / San Joaquin Valley Fever / Desert Rheumatism — influenza-like illness (fever, malaise, cough, arthralgia, headache)
  • Radiographs: hilar adenopathy with pulmonary infiltrates. In 5% — solitary nodules or thin-walled cavity
  • Hypersensitivity reaction in ~15% post-infection: maculopapular rash, erythema nodosum, or erythema multiforme
  • Disseminated / Secondary Coccidioidomycosis (<1% of cases, but often debilitating/life-threatening):
    • If chronic progressive pulmonary disease develops, usually within 1 year of primary infection
    • Spherules/endospores spread hematogenously or by direct extension — most frequent sites: skin, bones/joints, meninges
MUST KNOW — Risk factors for dissemination
  • Heredity
  • Sex — Males > Females (except during pregnancy, since C. immitis has estrogen-binding proteins. Elevated estradiol/progesterone in pregnancy stimulates fungal growth)
  • Age (young and older individuals)
  • Compromised cell-mediated immunity
  • Race (decreasing order): Filipinos > African American > Native American > Hispanics > Asians — affects Filipinos the most. A genetic component is being studied (lecturer-flagged 📌)

05 | Diagnostics

  • Antigen tests — 2 clinically useful preparations, both can elicit positive delayed-type skin reactions:
    • Coccidioidin — extracted from filtrate of a liquid mycelial culture
    • Spherulin — produced from a filtrate of a broth culture of spherules
  • Skin test (Coccidioidin Ag) — for symptomatic and asymptomatic patients. Used for epidemiologic/exposure purposes. Positive = >5 mm induration at 24–48 hr
  • Microscopy — KOH or Calcofluor stain to find spherules/endospores. H&E, GMS, PAS also usable
  • Culture media — Inhibitory mold agar. Brain Heart Infusion blood agar slant (± antibiotics/chlorheximide)
  • Serology (ELISA) — IgM = active/early infection, IgG = past infection
EXAM TRAP Arthroconidia are HIGHLY INFECTIOUS and remain viable for years — a biosafety cabinet MUST be used when manipulating samples/cultures in the lab. Question-writers love to test this lab-safety point specifically for Coccidioides.

Coccidioidomycosis — treatment by severity

Symptomatic 1° infectionSupportive therapy (self-limiting). Itraconazole can reduce symptoms

Recall cue: Spherule = diagnostic. Meningitis = fluconazole (CNS penetration). Severe/disseminated = Amphotericin B. Filipino ethnicity + California/Arizona travel + flu-like illness = think Valley Fever.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Coccidioides immitis/posadasii. Endemic SW USA, Mexico, South America
  • Morphology: arthroconidia → spherules with endospores (spherule = diagnostic)
  • Clinical: Valley fever (flu-like, erythema nodosum). Disseminated disease → skin, bones, meninges
  • Risk groups: Filipinos, African Americans, pregnant women, immunocompromised, males
  • Dx: microscopy (spherules), culture (hazardous — biosafety cabinet), serology
  • Tx: Itraconazole (mild) · Amphotericin B (severe) · Fluconazole (CNS/meningitis)
B. Histoplasmosis — Histoplasma capsulatum p.4–5

Core idea

Caused by Histoplasma capsulatum, a dimorphic soil saprophyte. Also known as Darling's Disease (1905, Dr. Samuel Darling originally described it as a "protozoan"). The most prevalent pulmonary fungal infection in both animals and humans.

MUST KNOW
  • Natural habitat: soil with excreta of bats, chickens, and starlings — high nitrogen content favors growth (lecturer-flagged 📌)
  • Optimal growth temperature: 22–29°C
  • Highest incidence in Central and Eastern US — endemic in Ohio River Valley and parts of the Mississippi River Valley
  • Mode of transmission: inhalation of macroconidia (prolonged exposure to dust/soil, disturbance of bird/bat guano) or traumatic implantation of spores (prick from infected plants/thorns) — at risk: spelunkers, agriculturists, outdoor construction workers
  • Facultative intracellular parasite — grows inside macrophages and reticuloendothelial cells
  • 95% of cases: subclinical, self-limited flu-like symptoms

01 | Mold form (environment, <37°C, esp. 25–33°C)

  • Brown colony on Sabouraud's dextrose agar after 4 weeks. Slow-growing, needs ~4–12 weeks total
  • Large tuberculate macroconidia: 8–16 μm with peripheral spiny projections
  • Smaller, infectious microconidia: 2–5 μm
  • Exists as mold with aerial hyphae producing tuberculate macroconidia + microconidia spores (aerosolized/dispersed for inhalation)

02 | Yeast form (tissue or 37°C culture)

  • Measures 2–4 μm. Single bud separated by a narrow base from mother cell

03 | Pathogenesis

MECHANISM — H. capsulatum pathogenesis
Inhalation of macroconidia OR traumatic implantation of spores
↓
Body temperature signals transformation to oval, budding yeast form
↓
Yeast phagocytosed by alveolar macrophages → transported via bloodstream to regional lymph nodes and other organs
↓
Inside macrophages: yeast produces HCO₃⁻ and NH₃ → raises phagosomal pH (alkaline)
↓
Prevents phagolysosomal acidification and fusion → inactivates lysosomal enzymes → organism multiplies
↓
Can disseminate to liver, spleen, lymph node, bone marrow

04 | Clinical manifestations

05 | Diagnostics

  • Microscopy — GMS, PAS, Giemsa stains. Small ovoid cells within macrophages, usually positive in disseminated disease
  • Culture — Glucose-cysteine-blood agar at 37°C, or SDA/IMA at 25–30°C (requires 4–12 weeks)
  • Complement Fixation (CF) — detects Ab to histoplasmin (crude standardized mycelial broth filtrate antigen) or yeast cell. Positive within 2–5 weeks post-infection. Titers rise with progressive disease
  • Immunodiffusion (ID) — detects precipitins to 2 specific antigens:
    • H (Hefe/yeast) Ag → active histoplasmosis
    • M (Mycelium) Ag → may reflect repeated skin testing or past exposure
  • ELISA — one of the most sensitive tests. Detects circulating polysaccharide antigen

  • Acute pulmonary: supportive therapy + rest. Itraconazole for mild–moderate infection
  • Disseminated disease: Amphotericin B = systemic treatment of choice, prolonged duration, monitor for relapse. AIDS patients often relapse → maintenance therapy with itraconazole
  • Control: not communicable person-to-person (conidia spread by wind/dust) — high-risk individuals should wear face masks while hiking/caving. Poultry/bird workers should wear protective gear. Spraying formaldehyde on infected soil may destroy H. capsulatum

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Histoplasma capsulatum. Reservoir = soil with bird/bat droppings
  • Pathogenesis: intracellular survival in macrophages via phagosome alkalinization
  • Clinical: subclinical/mild (most). Chronic pulmonary (cavitary). Disseminated (mucocutaneous ulcers, hepatosplenomegaly, bone marrow)
  • Dx: microscopy (GMS, PAS), culture (slow), serology (CF, ID, ELISA)
  • Tx: Itraconazole (mild) · Amphotericin B (severe/disseminated)
C. Blastomycosis — Blastomyces dermatitidis p.5–6

Core idea

Blastomyces dermatitidis causes "North American Blastomycosis." Endemic to North and Central America, Canada, North River Valley, plus Africa, South America, and Asia.

MUST KNOW
  • Thermally dimorphic fungus
  • Inhalation is the primary mode of entry. Lungs = primary infection site
  • Often presents as mild, self-limited pulmonary infiltrates — frequently undiagnosed
  • Yeast form: large, single budding cell, thick-walled, multinucleated, spherical (8–15 μm), with a broad-based bud between daughter and parent yeast (bud can enlarge to same size as parent before detaching)
  • Distinct pyogranulomatous reaction with neutrophils + non-caseating granuloma on histology

01 | Mold form (25°C in culture)

  • Produces hyaline, branching, septate hyphae with "lollipop-shaped" conidia
  • On SDA at room temperature: white or brownish colony (or white on top, brown/tan on bottom)
  • Branching hyphae with spherical/ovoid/conical conidia (3–5 μm). Slender terminal or lateral conidiospores. Large chlamydospores (7–8 μm)

02 | Yeast form (37°C / in host)

  • Yeast colonies described as wrinkled, waxy, and soft

03 | Pathogenesis

MECHANISM — B. dermatitidis pathogenesis
Environment: mold with septate hyphae producing spores
↓ inhaled or inoculated into skin
Warmer host temperature → transformation into broad-based budding yeast
↓
Yeast colonizes lungs OR disseminates via bloodstream (skin, bone, joints, organs, CNS) — just like histoplasmosis

Dissemination usually occurs among immunosuppressed individuals, causing skin lesions on exposed surfaces: ulcerated, verrucous granuloma with advancing border, central scarring, microabscesses along sharp sloping edges, plus lesions of bone, genitalia, CNS.

04 | Diagnostics

  • Microscopy — single, broadly attached buds on thick-walled yeast cells
  • Culture — colonies develop within 2 weeks on Sabouraud's or enriched blood agar at 30°C
  • Identification — confirmed by conversion to yeast form after cultivation on rich medium at 37°C
  • Antigen/antibody detection — extraction/detection of B. dermatitidis-specific antigen A. CF and ID measure antibodies
  • Blastomycin skin test — lacks specificity and sensitivity. False-positive cross-reactions occur with exposure to other fungi

  • Dx: microscopy, culture (37°C conversion), antigen/antibody tests
  • Tx: Itraconazole (localized) · Amphotericin B (severe)
D. Paracoccidioidomycosis — Paracoccidioides brasiliensis p.6–7

Core idea

Also known as South American blastomycosis. Confined to endemic regions of Central and South America and rural Latin America — common among farmers. No known natural habitat (rarely isolated in nature, like Blastomyces), theorized to be soil-associated.

MUST KNOW
  • Males > Females predilection — 90% of active-disease patients are males, aged 30–60 years
  • Non-contagious, thermally dimorphic (like the other 3 endemic mycoses)
  • Yeast form (36°C): large, multiple-budding yeast cells (up to 30 μm), thinner walls than B. dermatitidis, buds attached by a narrow connection resembling spokes of a ship's wheel / pilot's wheel

01 | Mold form (25°C)

  • Grows very slowly. Fine, septate, clear hyphae. Produces chlamydospores or conidia

02 | Yeast form (36°C)

  • Culture: white or brownish colony

03 | Pathogenesis

MECHANISM — P. brasiliensis pathogenesis
Inhalation of conidia (airborne spores) — infects mainly rural workers
↓
Initial lesion lodges in pulmonary alveoli (37°C)
↓
Conidia/hyphae fragments recognized by macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells → response determines outcome
Most common: pulmonary granuloma forms → may be dormant for decades → chronic progressive pulmonary disease or dissemination
Chronic (90% of cases, males 30–60y): yeast spreads from lungs to skin/mucocutaneous tissue → granulomatous, suppurative lesions of lymph nodes, spleen, adrenal glands, liver. Painful oral mucosal sores
Acute/subacute (<10%, <30 years old): shorter incubation, more rapidly progressive

Histopathology: granuloma with central caseation or microabscesses. Yeasts seen in giant cells or directly in exudates.

04 | Diagnostics

  • Microscopy — KOH or Calcofluor white for direct exam
  • Culture — Sabouraud's or yeast extract agar at room temperature. Conversion to yeast form in vitro at 36°C is confirmatory
  • Serologic testing (most useful) — antibody to paracoccidioidin detected by CF and ID

05 | Treatment

  • Itraconazole — most effective
  • Ketoconazole and TMP-SMX — alternatives
  • Amphotericin B — for severe cases
Recall cue: "Pilot's wheel / ship's wheel / mariner's wheel" yeast = Paracoccidioides. Latin American farmer, painful oral ulcer, multiple budding yeast.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Paracoccidioides brasiliensis. Endemic to rural Latin America (farmers, men >> women)
  • Morphology: multiple-budding yeast ("ship's wheel")
  • Clinical: chronic — pulmonary + mucocutaneous lesions. Acute/subacute — lymph nodes, liver, spleen, skin
  • Dx: microscopy, culture, serology (CF, ID)
  • Tx: Itraconazole (preferred) · Amphotericin B (severe) · alternatives: ketoconazole, TMP-SMX
III. Opportunistic Mycoses

This condition usually occurs in patients with compromised immune systems, making them susceptible to ubiquitous organisms that immunocompetent individuals normally resist. p.7

A. Candidiasis — Candida species p.7–10

Core idea

Caused by several Candida species that are part of the normal flora of skin, mucous membranes, and GI tract — colonizes mucosal surfaces soon after birth (endogenous opportunist). The most prevalent systemic mycosis.

MUST KNOW
  • Most common etiologic agents: C. albicans, C. parapsilosis, C. glabrata, C. tropicalis, C. guilliermondii, C. dubliniensis
  • C. albicans = most common pathogen, dimorphic (yeast + pseudohyphae + true hyphae)
  • Widespread fluconazole use has driven azole-resistant species: C. glabrata, C. krusei, C. lusitaniae
  • Multi-drug resistant species: C. auris
  • C. glabrata produces only yeast cells, no pseudohyphae — a classic distinguishing point
  • 2 morphologic tests distinguish C. albicans from other Candida:
    • Incubation in serum at 37°C for 60–90 min → forms true hyphae / germ tubes (thinner, more uniform than pseudohyphae)
    • Nutritionally deficient media → produces large, spherical chlamydospores

01 | Morphology and identification

  • In culture/tissue: oval, budding yeast cells (3–6 μm). Form pseudohyphae when buds continue to grow but fail to detach, forming chains of elongated cells pinched/constricted at the septations
  • Agar media (37°C or room temp): soft, cream-colored colonies with a yeasty odor. Pseudohyphae appear as submerged growth below the agar surface
  • 2 serotypes: Serotype A (includes C. tropicalis) and Serotype B

02 | Pathogenesis — virulence factors

The candida cell wall (mannan, glucans, polysaccharides, proteins, glycoproteins) stimulates host defenses and facilitates attachment/invasion.

MECHANISM — Candida virulence factors
Morphogenesis — reversible yeast ⇌ hyphae/pseudohyphae switching. Hard for host defenses to mobilize a response. Pseudohyphae resist phagocytosis. Hyphae/germ tubes express more surface receptors and bind opsonins, aiding invasion
Biofilm formation — yeast + pseudohyphae accumulate on biological/prosthetic surfaces, protected by an extracellular matrix that resists host immunity and antifungal drugs
Host recognition molecules — Agglutinin-like sequence (ALS) surface glycoprotein (e.g. adhesins) binds host receptors, mediating attachment to epithelial/endothelial cells
Invasion enzymes — 10 secreted aspartyl proteinases (SAP) degrade host membranes and destroy IgA. Phospholipase B1 (from yeast/pseudohyphae) degrades host membrane phospholipids → increases permeability

03 | Clinical manifestations

Cutaneous and mucosal candidiasis

Occurs when Candida population increases and the skin/epithelium is damaged, allowing local invasion of yeast/pseudohyphae.

SHOULD KNOW — Risk factors

AIDS, pregnancy, diabetes, extreme age groups, birth control pills, trauma, corticosteroid/antibiotic treatment, high glucose, cellular immunodeficiency

  • Thrush / oral candidiasis — patchy-to-confluent whitish pseudomembranous lesions (epithelial cells, yeast, pseudohyphae) on tongue/lips/gums/palate. Can form intractable biofilm. Can pass mother→infant or infant→infant via caregivers in a nursery. Develops in most AIDS patients
  • Vulvovaginal candidiasis — vulvovaginitis with irritation, pruritus, discharge. Risk factors: diabetes, pregnancy, antibacterial drugs (alter microbiota), local acidity/secretions
  • Other forms:
    • Cutaneous invasion — skin weakened by trauma, burns, laceration
    • Intertriginous infection — moist, warm areas (axilla, groin, intragluteal/intramammary folds)
    • Diaper rash — common in newborns. Moist red lesion, may vesiculate
    • Interdigital involvement — prolonged water immersion. Homemakers, bartenders, cooks, vegetable/fish handlers
    • Onychomycosis — nail/nail plate invasion. Painful erythematous swelling resembling paronychia, can destroy the nail
  • Candidal endocarditis — deposition/growth of yeast + pseudohyphae on prosthetic heart valves/vegetations. Recalcitrant biofilm formation. Associated with chronic corticosteroids/immunosuppressants, hematologic disease (leukemia, lymphoma, aplastic anemia), chronic granulomatous disease
  • Chronic Mucocutaneous Candidiasis (CMC) — rare. Granulomatous candidal lesions on cutaneous/mucosal surfaces. Classified by age of onset, endocrinopathy, genetic predisposition, immune status. Most common form starts in early childhood, associated with autoimmunity and hypoparathyroidism. Chronic raised crusty disfiguring keratotic lesions on skin, oral mucosa, scalp
Systemic candidiasis
  • Caused by broad-spectrum antibiotics that allow overgrowth, or when Candida enters the bloodstream and host defenses can't contain it (e.g. via intestinal mucosa)
  • Can infect kidney, attach to prosthetic heart valves, cause arthritis, meningitis, endophthalmitis
  • Critical host defense: adequate number of functional neutrophils to ingest/kill yeast
  • Candidemia — caused by indwelling catheters, surgery, IV drug abuse, aspiration, or skin/GIT damage. Transient if innate immunity/neutrophils are normal. If phagocytic defenses are compromised → occult lesions in kidney, skin (maculonodular), eyes, heart, meninges

04 | Diagnosis

  • Microscopy — Gram-stained smears or histopathologic slides show pseudohyphae and budding cells. Specimens from tissue biopsies, CSF, others
  • KOH and Calcofluor white — for skin/nail scrapings
  • Culture — room temp or 37°C on fungal/bacteriological media. Look for pseudohyphae. C. albicans identified by germ tube or chlamydospore production
  • CHROMagar — commercial medium, rapid identification (1–4 days) via fungal enzymatic action on chromogenic substrates

05 | Treatment

  • Thrush / oral mucocutaneous: Nystatin (topical). Oral ketoconazole or fluconazole. Eliminate contributing factors (excess moisture, antibacterial use) — hastens clearing of cutaneous lesions
  • Systemic candidiasis: Amphotericin B = treatment of choice. Can combine with oral flucytosine, fluconazole, or caspofungin
  • Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis: oral ketoconazole and other azoles. If genetic cellular immune defect → lifelong treatment required

06 | Prevention and control

Candidiasis is not considered communicable — all persons normally harbor the organism. The most important preventive measure is avoiding disruption of the normal microbiota and intact host defenses.

EXAM TRAP Not every Candida in a specimen means disease — Candida commonly colonizes the oropharynx/upper respiratory tract, so its presence in sputum generally has no diagnostic significance for pulmonary candidiasis unless correlated with invasive disease.
Recall cue: C. albicans = germ tube (+), chlamydospores, green on CHROMagar. C. glabrata = no pseudohyphae, dark purple, often fluconazole-resistant.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Candida species. Reservoir = normal flora of mouth, gut, skin (endogenous)
  • Virulence: yeast ⇌ hyphae switching, adhesins, biofilms, hydrolytic enzymes (SAPs, phospholipase B1)
  • Clinical: thrush, vulvovaginitis, diaper rash, onychomycosis, systemic candidemia (catheters/surgery/neutropenia), endocarditis (prosthetic valves), meningitis
  • Dx: microscopy (pseudohyphae, budding yeast), germ tube test (C. albicans), CHROMagar (species color)
  • Tx: superficial → Nystatin, azoles · systemic → Amphotericin B, fluconazole, echinocandins
B. Cryptococcosis — Cryptococcus species p.10–11

Core idea

Caused by C. neoformans or C. gattii — environmental, basidiomycetous yeasts with large polysaccharide capsules.

MUST KNOW
  • C. neoformans: more common (~90% of infections), worldwide. Habitat = dry pigeon droppings (optimal growth, serves as reservoir — birds themselves are not infected), also trees/soil. Hits immunocompromised hosts (HIV/AIDS, hematologic malignancy, immunosuppression)
  • C. gattii: less common, mainly found in trees in tropical areas, mainly affects normal (immunocompetent) hosts
  • Transmission: inhalation of desiccated yeast cells or the smaller basidiospore. Lungs = primary organ. Neurotropic yeasts migrate to the CNS (meningoencephalitis)
  • Growth at 37°C, production of laccase (phenol oxidase) → makes melanin from phenolic substrates, and reduces ferric→ferrous iron (aids iron acquisition)

01 | Species overview

  • All Cryptococcus are encapsulated and produce urease
  • Differentiated by capsular serotypes, genotypes, biochemical reactions, and clinical manifestations

02 | Morphology and identification

  • Culture: whitish, mucoid colonies within 2–3 days
  • Microscopy: spherical, budding yeast cells (5–10 μm) surrounded by a thick, non-staining capsule
  • Selective/differential media: avoid cyclohexamide-containing media (inhibits Cryptococcus growth). Bird seed agar (contains caffeic acid) — C. neoformans and C. gattii produce melanin on their cell wall when laccase acts on the diphenolic substrate

Virulence factors

  • Laccase — melanin/iron acquisition (above)
  • Capsular polysaccharide antigen — 5 capsular serotypes (A–D, AD). Polysaccharides are structurally similar regardless of serotype. The major capsular antigen is Glucuronoxylomannan (GXM), released during infection and detected by enzyme immunoassay or latex particle agglutination (diagnostic of cryptococcal infection)

03 | Pathogenesis

MECHANISM — Cryptococcus pathogenesis
Entry: inhalation of dry, minimally encapsulated, easily aerosolized yeast cells
↓
Primary pulmonary infection — usually asymptomatic, mimics influenza-like illness, resolves spontaneously
↓ (in immunocompromised patients)
Yeast multiplies and disseminates — preference for the CNS

04 | Clinical manifestations

  • Chronic meningitis = major clinical manifestation — can resemble a brain tumor, brain abscess, degenerative CNS disease, or other mycobacterial/fungal meningitis
  • CSF findings: high pressure, high protein, high cell count. Glucose normal to low
  • Symptoms: headache, neck stiffness, disorientation
  • Other lesions: skin, lungs, other organs
  • Untreated cases are fatal. Course fluctuates with treatment
  • Not transmitted person-to-person

05 | Diagnosis

  • Microscopy — wet mount with India ink: delineates the capsule (halo effect), spherical budding yeast surrounded by thick capsule
  • Serology — test for GXM via latex slide agglutination or EIA/ELISA. ~90% sensitive, especially with meningitis
  • Culture — grows within a few days at room temp or 37°C. Whitish/mucoid colonies, can produce brown/dark pigment (melanin)

06 | Treatment

MUST KNOW

Combination therapy of Amphotericin B + Flucytosine is the standard treatment for cryptococcal meningitis (lecturer-flagged 📌).

  • Amphotericin B — curative in most non-AIDS patients, with or without flucytosine
  • AIDS patients: inadequate treatment → relapse when amphotericin B withdrawn → suppressive therapy with fluconazole (excellent CNS penetration, prevents relapse)
  • HAART lowers the incidence of cryptococcosis
Recall cue: Pigeon droppings + HIV/AIDS + chronic meningitis + India ink halo = C. neoformans. Normal host + tropical tree exposure = think C. gattii.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Cryptococcus species. Reservoir = pigeon droppings (neoformans), tropical trees (gattii)
  • Virulence: polysaccharide capsule (GXM), melanin (laccase), urease
  • Clinical: pulmonary infection → cryptococcal meningitis (headache, ↑CSF pressure)
  • Dx: India ink (capsule), antigen test (latex agglutination, EIA), culture (urease +)
  • Tx: Amphotericin B + Flucytosine → fluconazole maintenance (esp. AIDS)
C. Aspergillosis — Aspergillus species p.11–12

Core idea

Caused by Aspergillus species — ubiquitous saprophytes in nature, occurring worldwide.

MUST KNOW
  • A. fumigatus = most common human pathogen. A. flavus, A. niger, A. terreus, A. lentulus may also cause disease
  • Grows rapidly, producing aerial hyphae bearing conidial structures: long conidiospores with terminal vesicles. Vesicles bear phialides that produce basipetal chains of conidia
  • Microscopy: hyaline, septate uniform hyphae (~4 μm), branching dichotomously at acute angles (45°) — classic identifying feature

02 | Pathogenesis

  • Produces abundant small conidia, easily aerosolized — atopic individuals develop severe allergic reactions to conidial antigens
  • Normal host: hyphae engulfed by neutrophils → infection contained
  • Susceptible host: conidia may swell and germinate into hyphae → invade lungs/tissues. Macrophages have diminished capacity to contain the inoculum and destroy conidia. Can form an aspergilloma (fungus ball) in pre-existing cavities. Can invade blood vessels

03 | Clinical findings

Allergic forms
  • Immediate asthmatic reactions — IgE Ab to Aspergillus conidia surface antigen
  • Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) — conidia germinate, hyphae colonize the bronchial tree without invading lung parenchyma. Asthma, recurrent chest infiltrates, eosinophilia, Type I and Type III (Arthus) hypersensitivity to Aspergillus antigen, difficulty breathing, sputum + for Aspergillus, serum precipitins, permanent lung scarring
  • Extrinsic allergic alveolitis — develops in normal hosts exposed to a massive dose of conidia
Aspergilloma & extrapulmonary colonization
  • Aspergilloma (fungus ball) — inhaled conidia enter an existing cavity, germinate, produce abundant hyphae in the abnormal pulmonary space. Previous cavitary disease = risk factor. May be asymptomatic or cause cough, dyspnea, weight loss, fatigue, hemoptysis. Rarely becomes invasive
  • Localized, non-invasive colonization can involve nasal sinuses, ear canal, cornea, and nails
Invasive aspergillosis
MECHANISM — Invasive aspergillosis
Inhalation and germination of conidia → invasive disease (± dissemination)
↓
Hyphae invade the lumens and walls of blood vessels → thrombosis, infarction, necrosis
↓
May spread from lungs to GIT, kidney, liver, brain, other organs → abscess/necrotic lesions
SHOULD KNOW — Risk factors for invasive disease

Lymphocytic/myelogenous leukemia and lymphoma, stem cell transplant recipients, long-term steroid therapy, AIDS with CD4 <50/μL. Symptoms: fever, cough, dyspnea, hemoptysis.

04 | Diagnosis

  • Microscopy — KOH/calcofluor white or histologic sections: hyaline, septate uniform hyphae (~4 μm), dichotomous branching at acute angles
  • Culture — grows within a few days on most media at room temperature. Species identified by conidial structure shape
  • A. fumigatus — culture: gray-green colony on potato flakes agar (PFA). Microscopy: uniseriate conidiophore with columnar conidia

  • Tx: Voriconazole (preferred for invasive) · Amphotericin B · surgery (aspergilloma) · steroids (allergic)
D. Mucormycosis (Zygomycosis) p.12–13

Core idea

Also known as Zygomycosis — ubiquitous, thermotolerant saprophytes of the order Mucorales (Phylum Glomerulomycota, Subphylum Mucormycotina).

MUST KNOW
  • Leading pathogens: Rhizopus, Rhizomucor, Lichtheimia, Cunninghamella, Absidia, Mucor — Rhizopus oryzae is the most prevalent agent, with abundant cottony colonies
  • Angioinvasion is the most characteristic pathogenesis of mucormycosis (lecturer-flagged 📌) → thrombosis of involved vessel → ischemia and necrosis. Lack of blood supply also protects the fungus from antifungal drugs and host inflammatory cells
  • Also called "Black Fungus" — blackened/discolored tissue (especially face, nose, eyes) as the fungus damages blood vessels

01 | Pathogenesis

MECHANISM — Mucormycosis pathogenesis
Normal host: hyphae engulfed by neutrophils → infection contained
Susceptible host: fungal spores bind extracellular matrix proteins in the basement membrane, secreting lipolytic/glycosidic enzymes and proteases that degrade the underlying stroma → invasion
Angioinvasion → thrombosis → ischemia and necrosis
SHOULD KNOW — Role of iron
  • Iron is essential for fungal cell growth/development. Higher free iron availability is linked to severity of infection
  • Conditions that impair transferrin's iron-binding capacity (e.g. acidosis) shift iron from ferric→ferrous form, favoring fungal growth
  • Repeated blood transfusions → iron overload = predisposition to mucormycosis

02 | Clinical manifestations

  • Rhinocerebral mucormycosis (major clinical form) — germination of sporangiospores in nasal passages → hyphae invade blood vessels → thrombosis, infarction, necrosis → progresses rapidly, invading sinuses, eyes, cranial bones, brain. Damages blood vessels and nerves. Edema of the face, bloody nasal exudate, orbital cellulitis. Survival is high with proper treatment, but residual effects can include partial facial paralysis or loss of an eye
  • Thoracic mucormycosis — inhalation of sporangiospores, invasion of lung parenchyma and vasculature ("Black Fungus" — blackening of infected tissue)
MUST KNOW — Classic risk factors

Uncontrolled diabetes/ketoacidosis and iron overload are the classic predisposing conditions for mucormycosis.

03 | Diagnosis

  • Microscopy — broad hyphae (10–15 μm) with uneven thickness, irregular branching, and sparse septations (essentially non-septate/pauci-septate — the classic contrast with Aspergillus)
  • Culture — rapid growth. Identification based on sporangial structures

04 | Treatment

  • Surgical debridement (urgent) + Amphotericin B
  • Control of underlying disease (e.g. glycemic control)
EXAM TRAP Broad, ribbon-like, non-septate hyphae branching at wide angles (Mucor) vs. thin, septate hyphae branching at acute (45°) angles (Aspergillus) — a classic vignette differentiator.
Recall cue: Diabetic ketoacidosis + facial swelling/black necrotic eschar + rhinocerebral disease = Mucor. Treatment is urgent — surgery and Amphotericin B, not one or the other.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia. Reservoir = soil, decaying organic matter
  • Pathogenesis: angioinvasion → thrombosis, necrosis ("black fungus"). Risk ↑ with diabetes/ketoacidosis, iron overload
  • Clinical: rhinocerebral (facial swelling, orbital cellulitis), pulmonary, disseminated
  • Dx: broad, ribbon-like, non-septate hyphae. Culture (cottony colonies)
  • Tx: urgent surgical debridement + Amphotericin B
E. Pneumocystis Pneumonia — Pneumocystis jirovecii p.13

Core idea

Major cause of death in AIDS patients. Caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii — historically thought to be a protozoan. A human-specific species also found in the lungs of many animals (rats, mice, dogs, cats, ferrets, rabbits). Rarely causes disease except in immunocompromised individuals. An extracellular pathogen.

MUST KNOW
  • P. carinii is found only in rats — the human pathogen is specifically P. jirovecii (classic naming trap)
  • Morphology: thin-walled trophozoites. Cysts are thick-walled, spherical to elliptical (4–6 μm), containing 4–8 nuclei — stained with silver stain, toluidine blue, calcofluor white. Trophozoites and cysts present in tight masses
  • Growth is limited to the surfactant layer above the alveolar epithelium

01 | Pathogenesis

  • Interstitial plasma pneumonitis — in non-AIDS patients: infiltration of alveolar spaces with plasma cells

02 | Diagnosis

  • Specimens stained and examined for trophozoites and cysts
  • Cannot be cultured — a key limitation
  • P. jirovecii contains a surface glycoprotein detectable in sera of acutely ill or normal individuals — used to establish prevalence, though not clinically useful for diagnosis

03 | Treatment

  • Acute cases: Trimethoprim–Sulfamethoxazole (TMP-SMX) or Pentamidine isethionate
  • Pentamidine may also be used as prophylaxis for the immunocompromised
Recall cue: AIDS patient, CD4 <200/μL, diffuse bilateral infiltrates, cannot be cultured → think PCP. Treat and prevent with the same drug class: TMP-SMX.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Cause: Pneumocystis jirovecii. Host = AIDS (CD4 <200/μL)
  • Morphology: cysts with 4–8 nuclei (silver stain, toluidine blue)
  • Pathogenesis: interstitial pneumonia, foamy alveolar exudate
  • Dx: microscopy of BAL → cysts/trophozoites. Cannot be cultured
  • Tx: TMP-SMX (treatment + prophylaxis) · Pentamidine (alternative)
IV. Antifungals

Fungi are eukaryotes, like humans, sharing many molecular/cellular processes and some homologous genes/proteins — this is why some antifungals are toxic to humans (they hit shared targets), and also why antifungal drug development has historically lagged behind antibacterial development.

SHOULD KNOW — Limitations of current antifungals

  • Poor penetration of certain tissues
  • Development of resistance
  • Significant side effects — the reason some antifungals can only be given topically, not systemically

C. Systemic agents

01 | Amphotericin B

MUST KNOW
  • Major antifungal agent of the polyene class. A metabolite of Streptomyces
  • Most effective drug for severe systemic mycoses (lecturer-flagged 📌) — broad spectrum, resistance is rare, fungicidal. At low levels has an immunostimulatory effect
  • MOA: forms complexes with ergosterol → alters membrane fluidity → pores form → leakage of potassium and other small molecules → cell death. Has a stronger affinity for ergosterol than cholesterol. Mammalian cells (which use cholesterol, not ergosterol) are relatively — but not completely — resistant to its effects, since weak binding to cholesterol can still sometimes cause toxicity
  • Given IV. Widely distributed in most tissues but poor CNS penetration (poor penetration of joints and CNS too — intrathecal/intraarticular administration may be recommended for some infections)
  • Response depends on: dose/rate of administration, site of infection, immune status, inherent susceptibility of the organism
SHOULD KNOW — Side effects
  • Acute reactions: fever, chills, dyspnea, hypotension
  • Chronic reactions: mainly nephrotoxicity — may be reversible. Azotemia is the most common manifestation. Permanent reduction of glomerular/renal tubular function can occur. Damage correlates with dose
  • Also: hypokalemia, anemia, renal tubular acidosis, headache, nausea, vomiting
  • Book note: adverse reactions are greatly diminished with newer lipid preparations of Amphotericin B

02 | Flucytosine

  • Fluorinated derivative of cytosine (5-fluorocytosine). Oral antifungal. Used for cryptococcosis or candidiasis in conjunction with Amphotericin B
  • Effective against many dematiaceous fungal infections (dark pigmentation/melanin in cell wall — e.g. chromoblastomycosis, mycetoma, fungal sinusitis)
  • Penetrates well into tissues including CSF
MECHANISM — Flucytosine
Actively transported into the fungal cell by a permease (synergy with Amphotericin B, which increases membrane permeability → more flucytosine gets in)
↓ cytosine deaminase (absent in mammalian cells → selective toxicity)
Converted to 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)
↓
5-fluorodeoxyuridylic acid monophosphate → inhibits thymidylate synthetase → blocks DNA synthesis (deprives the organism of thymidylic acid)
Because mammalian cells lack cytosine deaminase, they cannot convert flucytosine to the toxic 5-FU directly — this is the basis of its selective toxicity. Limited use due to rapid emergence of resistant mutant strains.
  • Indications: combined with Amphotericin B for cryptococcosis/candidiasis (true synergy). Cryptococcal meningitis — good CSF penetration of both drugs together. Delays/limits emergence of flucytosine-resistant strains
  • Side effects: little toxicity, well tolerated. When converted to fluorouracil (highly toxic) may cause bone marrow suppression, hair loss, abnormal liver function, colitis
CLINICAL PEARL In clinical practice, fluorouracil (5-FU) itself is used directly as a chemotherapy agent (not derived from flucytosine conversion) — and carries its own immunosuppressive side effects. Don't confuse "5-FU as flucytosine's active metabolite" with "5-FU as a standalone chemo drug."

03 | Azoles (general)

  • Signature 5-membered imidazole ring (2 non-adjacent nitrogen atoms, e.g. ketoconazole) vs. triazole ring (3 nitrogen atoms — fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole)
  • Broad spectrum. Used to treat less severe mycoses in place of Amphotericin B — oral administration, less toxic, fungistatic (not fungicidal)
  • MOA: interferes with ergosterol synthesis by blocking the cytochrome P450-dependent 14α-demethylation of lanosterol (lanosterol is the shared precursor of ergosterol in fungi and cholesterol in mammals)
  • Fungal cytochrome P450 is 100–1000× more sensitive than the mammalian enzyme, giving azoles a much greater affinity for the fungal target
  • General side effect theme: some ability to inhibit mammalian cytochrome P450 too — degree and consequences vary by specific drug (see below)

05 | Griseofulvin

  • Derived from a species of Penicillium
  • Treats dermatophytoses only — no effect on other fungi. Treatment must be given for long periods
  • Poorly absorbed. Concentrates in the stratum corneum → inhibits hyphal growth. After oral administration is distributed throughout the body and accumulates in keratinized tissue
  • MOA: within the fungus, interacts with microtubules and disrupts mitotic spindle function by binding tubulin → inhibits growth. Only actively growing hyphae are affected (it targets active mitosis)
  • Long-term therapy required (weeks to months) for skin, hair, and nail dermatophyte infections
  • Side effects: headache (most common, resolves without stopping the drug), GI disturbance, drowsiness, hepatotoxicity

06 | Terbinafine

  • An allylamine. Blocks ergosterol synthesis by inhibiting squalene epoxidase (an earlier step than the azoles' target)
  • Oral route. Treats dermatophyte infections, effective for nail infections (e.g. long-term treatment of tinea unguium, sometimes as an intermittent "pulse" protocol)
  • Side effects: uncommon — GI distress, headaches, skin reactions, loss of taste sensation

D. Topical agents

01 | Nystatin

  • Polyene antibiotic, structurally related to Amphotericin B with a similar MOA — complexes with ergosterol, creates pores, disrupts the membrane, causes leakage of ions/small molecules, and cell death
  • Used for local candidal infections of the mouth and vagina. May suppress subclinical esophageal candidiasis and GI Candida overgrowth
  • No systemic absorption. Essentially no side effects. Given topically because it is too toxic for parenteral administration

02 | Clotrimazole, Miconazole, and other topical azoles

  • Too toxic for systemic use, but comparable topical efficacy: Clotrimazole, Miconazole, Econazole, Butoconazole, Tioconazole, Terconazole, Luliconazole
  • Broad spectrum — tinea pedis, tinea corporis, tinea versicolor, tinea cruris, cutaneous candidiasis (creams/powders). Vulvovaginal candidiasis (vaginal suppositories/creams). Oral/esophageal thrush (clotrimazole oral troche)
  • Nail infections — often intractable. Luliconazole has shown dramatic effectiveness
EXAM TRAP Ketoconazole is systemic-capable but is the most hormonally toxic azole — if a vignette describes gynecomastia/adrenal insufficiency on an antifungal, think ketoconazole, not fluconazole/itraconazole (which spare steroidogenesis).
Recall cue: "-conazole" almost always = ergosterol synthesis blocker (azole). "-fungin" = echinocandin (cell wall). Only two drugs bind ergosterol directly rather than blocking its synthesis: Amphotericin B and Nystatin.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY
  • Amphotericin B — polyene, binds ergosterol, most effective for severe systemic mycoses, nephrotoxic
  • Flucytosine — synergy with Amphotericin B, → 5-FU, blocks DNA synthesis, bone marrow suppression
  • Azoles — block 14α-demethylase. Ketoconazole = most hormone side effects. Fluconazole = best CNS penetration. Itraconazole = 1st choice for histo/blasto. Voriconazole = aspergillosis + visual SEs. Posaconazole = covers mucormycosis
  • Echinocandins — block β-glucan synthase (cell wall). Caspofungin = 1st licensed, refractory aspergillosis
  • Griseofulvin — disrupts mitotic spindle, dermatophytes only, long courses
  • Terbinafine — blocks squalene epoxidase, great for nail dermatophyte infections
  • Nystatin — topical polyene, no systemic absorption, safe

Commonly Confused Concepts
Pairs students mix up on this topic cross-lesson
Do not confuse: Aspergillus vs. Mucor (Zygomycetes) on microscopy
Aspergillus
  • Septate hyphae
  • Branches at acute (45°) angles
  • Uniform, ~4 μm width
  • Invasive form → CD4 <50, leukemia, transplant
Mucor / Rhizopus
  • Non-septate (or sparsely septate) hyphae
  • Branches at wide (~90°) angles
  • Broad, ribbon-like, uneven thickness (10–15 μm)
  • Classic setting → diabetic ketoacidosis, iron overload
Fast distinction: septate + acute angle = Aspergillus. Non-septate + wide angle = Mucor. Both angioinvade, but the risk-factor vignette (DKA vs. leukemia/transplant) is usually the faster clue.
Do not confuse: Blastomyces vs. Paracoccidioides yeast morphology
Blastomyces dermatitidis
  • Single, broad-based bud
  • Bud can grow to same size as parent before detaching
  • North America — river valleys
Paracoccidioides brasiliensis
  • Multiple buds around one cell — "ship's/pilot's wheel"
  • Thinner walls than Blastomyces
  • Latin America — rural farmers
Fast distinction: one big bud = Blastomyces. A ring of many small buds = Paracoccidioides.
Do not confuse: Histoplasma vs. Cryptococcus at the level of the macrophage
Histoplasma capsulatum
  • Small (2–4 μm), obligate intracellular yeast — actually lives and multiplies inside macrophages
  • Survives by alkalinizing the phagosome
  • Stains: GMS, PAS, Giemsa
Cryptococcus neoformans
  • Larger (5–10 μm), encapsulated yeast — the thick polysaccharide capsule is the defining feature, not intracellular survival
  • Identified with India ink (halo effect) or capsular antigen tests
Fast distinction: if the vignette says "yeast found INSIDE macrophages," think Histoplasma. If it says "halo" or "capsule," think Cryptococcus.
Do not confuse: Candida albicans vs. Candida glabrata
C. albicans
  • Most common Candida pathogen
  • Forms germ tubes (37°C serum) and chlamydospores
  • Yeast + pseudohyphae + true hyphae
  • CHROMagar: green
C. glabrata
  • Produces only yeast cells — no pseudohyphae
  • Frequently fluconazole-resistant
  • CHROMagar: dark purple
Fast distinction: "no pseudohyphae seen" + azole resistance on a Candida vignette → think C. glabrata, not C. albicans.
Do not confuse: Nystatin vs. Amphotericin B
Nystatin
  • Same class (polyene), same MOA as Amphotericin B
  • Topical only — too toxic to give parenterally
  • No systemic absorption, essentially no side effects
Amphotericin B
  • Same class, same MOA
  • IV/systemic — the go-to for severe systemic mycoses
  • Significant toxicity: nephrotoxicity, infusion reactions
Fast distinction: identical mechanism ≠ identical use. The only real difference the lecture emphasizes is route and tolerability, not spectrum.
Do not confuse: Pneumocystis jirovecii vs. Pneumocystis carinii
P. jirovecii
  • The species that infects humans
  • Cause of PCP (Pneumocystis pneumonia) in AIDS
P. carinii
  • Found only in rats
  • Historical/older name still seen in the abbreviation "PCP," but is not the human pathogen
Fast distinction: if the question is about a human patient, the organism is P. jirovecii even though the abbreviation "PCP" persists from the old name.
Exam Traps
Likely test traps across the whole lesson cross-lesson
EXAM TRAP C. immitis arthroconidia remain viable for years and are extremely infectious — lab specimens require a biosafety cabinet. A question describing routine bench handling of a suspected Coccidioides culture is testing whether you catch this safety violation.
EXAM TRAP A spherule containing endospores is diagnostic of Coccidioides — don't confuse it with the thick capsule of Cryptococcus (no internal endospores) or the intracellular yeast of Histoplasma (no spherule stage).
EXAM TRAP Candida cultured from sputum is usually a colonizer, not a pathogen — it has no diagnostic significance for pulmonary candidiasis on its own.
EXAM TRAP C. neoformans classically hits immunocompromised hosts. C. gattii classically hits immunocompetent/normal hosts in tropical areas. Exam writers like to swap these.
EXAM TRAP Among the azoles, ketoconazole is the one with major hormonal side effects (gynecomastia, adrenal insufficiency) — fluconazole and itraconazole specifically do not significantly impair mammalian steroidogenesis.
EXAM TRAP Hyphae morphology on microscopy: Aspergillus = septate, acute-angle branching. Mucor/Rhizopus = non-septate (or sparse septa), wide-angle branching. Swapping these is one of the most common distractor patterns in mycology questions.
EXAM TRAP Amphotericin B and Nystatin are the same drug class with the same mechanism — the only reason Nystatin is topical-only is toxicity if given parenterally, not a difference in spectrum or mechanism.
EXAM TRAP "PCP" pneumonia in a human patient is caused by Pneumocystis jirovecii, not P. carinii — P. carinii is found only in rats.
EXAM TRAP Mucormycosis's classic risk factors are diabetic ketoacidosis and iron overload (from repeated transfusions) — the "black fungus" nickname and angioinvasive necrosis both point here, not to Aspergillus.
EXAM TRAP Amphotericin B + Flucytosine combination therapy is the standard specifically for cryptococcal meningitis — know this pairing precisely, since single-agent flucytosine resistance emerges rapidly.
EXAM TRAP Griseofulvin (disrupts the mitotic spindle) and Terbinafine (inhibits squalene epoxidase) both treat dermatophytes but by entirely different mechanisms — don't merge their MOAs on a mechanism-matching question.
EXAM TRAP Only ~1% of Coccidioidomycosis cases disseminate, but when they do it is often fatal — don't read "rare" as "not dangerous."

🧠 Cram Sheet — 5 to 10 Minute Full Run-Through

Everything below is already covered in detail above. This is the condensed, stand-alone version — read it top to bottom right before your exam.

1 Systemic mycoses = endemic (geography/inhalation-driven) or opportunistic (immune-status-driven)

>90% enter via the lungs. Intact Th1/cell-mediated immunity prevents dissemination. Males disseminate more than females for all 4 endemic mycoses.

2 All 4 endemic mycoses are thermally dimorphic

Mold/mycelium at 25°C (environment) ⇌ yeast or spherule at 36–37°C (host). Dimorphism is required for virulence.

3 Coccidioides — spherule is diagnostic

SW USA/Latin America. Arthroconidia inhaled → spherules with endospores in tissue. Highly infectious — needs a biosafety cabinet. Highest disseminated-disease risk in Filipinos. Tx: itraconazole (mild) → Amphotericin B (severe) → fluconazole (meningitis, best CNS penetration).

4 Histoplasma — obligate intracellular yeast

Soil with bird/bat droppings. Ohio/Mississippi river valleys. Survives inside macrophages by alkalinizing the phagosome. Tx: itraconazole (mild) → Amphotericin B (disseminated).

5 Blastomyces — single, broad-based budding yeast

North America river valleys. Tx: itraconazole (localized, ~6 months) → Amphotericin B (severe).

6 Paracoccidioides — multiple-budding "ship's wheel" yeast

Latin American farmers, 90% male. Tx: itraconazole = most effective.

7 Candida — endogenous opportunist

C. albicans: germ tube (+), chlamydospores, green on CHROMagar. C. glabrata: no pseudohyphae, dark purple, often fluconazole-resistant. Systemic disease → Amphotericin B ± flucytosine/fluconazole/caspofungin.

8 Cryptococcus — encapsulated yeast, India ink halo

C. neoformans: pigeon droppings, worldwide, immunocompromised hosts. C. gattii: tropical trees, immunocompetent hosts. Cryptococcal meningitis → Amphotericin B + Flucytosine, then fluconazole maintenance (esp. AIDS).

9 Aspergillus — septate hyphae, acute-angle branching

A. fumigatus = most common. Forms: allergic (ABPA), aspergilloma (fungus ball in old cavities), invasive (angioinvasive, CD4<50/leukemia/transplant). Tx: voriconazole preferred for invasive disease.

10 Mucormycosis — non-septate, wide-angle hyphae

Angioinvasion is the hallmark → thrombosis/necrosis ("black fungus"). Classic risk factors: diabetic ketoacidosis, iron overload. Rhinocerebral disease → urgent surgical debridement + Amphotericin B.

11 Pneumocystis jirovecii — AIDS-defining pneumonia

CD4 <200/μL. Cysts (4–8 nuclei) on silver stain/toluidine blue. Cannot be cultured. TMP-SMX for both treatment and prophylaxis.

12 Antifungal target map

Polyenes (Amphotericin B, Nystatin) bind ergosterol directly → pores. Azoles and Terbinafine block ergosterol synthesis (14α-demethylase / squalene epoxidase). Echinocandins block cell-wall β-1,3-glucan synthase. Flucytosine → 5-FU → blocks DNA/RNA synthesis. Griseofulvin disrupts the mitotic spindle.

13 Amphotericin B = most effective drug for severe systemic mycoses

Nephrotoxic (azotemia most common). Poor CNS/joint penetration. Newer lipid formulations reduce toxicity.

14 Fluconazole = best CNS penetration among the azoles

Drug of choice for cryptococcal and coccidioidal meningitis maintenance. Does not impair steroidogenesis.

15 Itraconazole = first choice for histoplasmosis and blastomycosis

Also covers coccidioidomycosis, paracoccidioidomycosis, aspergillosis, and dermatophyte/mold onychomycosis.

16 Voriconazole = preferred for invasive aspergillosis

Side effect: reversible visual disturbances in ~30% of patients.

17 Ketoconazole = most hormonally toxic azole

Inhibits testosterone/cortisol synthesis even at therapeutic doses → gynecomastia, impotence, adrenal insufficiency.

18 Nystatin = topical-only cousin of Amphotericin B

Identical polyene mechanism, but too toxic to give systemically — used only for local mouth/vaginal candidiasis, essentially no side effects.

Answer in a few words before you tap to reveal. Covers the entire lesson, front to back.

Two broad causes of systemic mycoses?
Endemic fungi (inhaled, geographic) and opportunistic fungi (immunocompromised hosts).
Shared temperature-dependent property of the 4 endemic mycoses?
Thermal dimorphism — mold at 25°C, yeast/spherule at 37°C.
Tissue form that is diagnostic of Coccidioides?
The spherule containing endospores.
Why must Coccidioides cultures be handled in a biosafety cabinet?
Arthroconidia are highly infectious and remain viable for years.
Ethnicity with the highest risk of disseminated coccidioidomycosis?
Filipinos (then African American > Native American > Hispanic > Asian).
Drug of choice for coccidioidal meningitis?
Fluconazole (oral, good CNS penetration).
Natural habitat of Histoplasma capsulatum?
Soil with bird/bat droppings (high nitrogen content).
How does Histoplasma survive inside macrophages?
Raises the phagosomal pH (alkalinizes it), blocking phagolysosome fusion/acidification.
First-choice drug for histoplasmosis and blastomycosis?
Itraconazole.
Yeast form of Blastomyces dermatitidis?
Large, thick-walled, single cell with a broad-based bud.
Classic yeast morphology of Paracoccidioides brasiliensis?
Multiple budding cells — "ship's / pilot's wheel."
Two morphologic tests that identify C. albicans?
Germ tube test (serum, 37°C, 60–90 min) and chlamydospore formation on nutrient-deficient media.
Which Candida species produces no pseudohyphae?
C. glabrata.
Color of C. albicans on CHROMagar?
Green.
What stains the Cryptococcus capsule for microscopy?
India ink (halo effect).
Which Cryptococcus species typically affects immunocompetent hosts?
C. gattii.
Major capsular antigen of Cryptococcus detected by latex agglutination?
Glucuronoxylomannan (GXM).
Standard treatment for cryptococcal meningitis?
Amphotericin B + Flucytosine, then fluconazole maintenance.
Most common pathogenic Aspergillus species?
A. fumigatus.
Describe Aspergillus hyphae on microscopy.
Hyaline, septate, ~4 μm, branching dichotomously at acute (45°) angles.
What is an aspergilloma?
A fungus ball forming in a pre-existing lung cavity.
Preferred treatment for invasive aspergillosis?
Voriconazole (or Amphotericin B).
Most characteristic pathogenic mechanism of mucormycosis?
Angioinvasion → thrombosis and necrosis.
Two classic risk factors for mucormycosis?
Diabetic ketoacidosis and iron overload.
Describe Mucor hyphae on microscopy.
Broad, ribbon-like, non-septate, irregular wide-angle branching.
Treatment approach for rhinocerebral mucormycosis?
Urgent surgical debridement + Amphotericin B.
Which Pneumocystis species actually infects humans?
P. jirovecii (P. carinii is found only in rats).
Can Pneumocystis be cultured?
No — diagnosis relies on staining cysts/trophozoites (silver stain, toluidine blue, calcofluor white).
Drug of choice for PCP treatment and prophylaxis?
TMP-SMX (Pentamidine is the alternative).
Mechanism of action of Amphotericin B?
Binds ergosterol in the fungal membrane → pores → ion leakage → cell death.
Mechanism of action of azoles?
Inhibit fungal cytochrome P450 14α-demethylase, blocking lanosterol → ergosterol conversion.
Most hormonally toxic azole?
Ketoconazole (gynecomastia, adrenal insufficiency).
Which azole has the best CNS penetration?
Fluconazole.
Mechanism of action of echinocandins?
Inhibit β-1,3-glucan synthase, disrupting the fungal cell wall.
Which echinocandin was the first licensed?
Caspofungin.
Mechanism of action of flucytosine?
Converted to 5-fluorouracil intracellularly → inhibits thymidylate synthetase → blocks DNA synthesis.
Mechanism of action of griseofulvin?
Binds tubulin, disrupting the mitotic spindle — inhibits fungal mitosis.
Mechanism of action of terbinafine?
Inhibits squalene epoxidase, an early step of ergosterol synthesis.
Why is Nystatin used only topically?
Same mechanism as Amphotericin B, but too toxic for parenteral use.
