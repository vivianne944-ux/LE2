A. Parvoviridae — Parvovirus B19 pp. 1–2

★ Must Know

Genome: single-stranded linear DNA. Capsid: icosahedral. Envelope: none. Tropism: erythroid progenitor cells.

Recall cue: B19 = blood-lineage target.

Clinical Pearl

B19 binds the blood group P antigen, found on mature erythrocytes, erythroid progenitors, megakaryocytes, endothelial cells, placenta, and fetal liver and heart.

Core mechanism: why B19 loves rapidly dividing erythroid cells

Parvovirus replication
Endocytosis
Uncoating in nucleus
Wait for natural host S phase
Host synthesizes viral DNA
Capsid proteins accumulate in nucleus
Genome packaging
Cell lysis
⚠ Exam Trap

Parvovirus cannot drive the host into S phase. It waits for the host cell to enter S phase naturally. This is a clean contrast with adenovirus.

Stability and culture

  • No lipid envelope means marked environmental resistance.
  • Lecture values: stable from pH 3–9 and can withstand 56°C for 60 minutes.
  • Can be inactivated by formalin, beta-propiolactone, and strong oxidizing agents including hydrogen peroxide and chlorine.
  • B19 is difficult to culture because only primary erythroid progenitors are permissive.

Pathogenesis and fifth disease

Respiratory entry or blood / vertical transmission
Viremia around days 7–10 after exposure → highly infectious
Direct killing of erythroid precursors → temporary interruption of erythropoiesis
Antibody response begins
“Slapped cheek” rash → fine lacy rash on trunk and limbs
Once rash appears, immunocompetent patient is no longer infectious
High Risk
Hematologic disease

Sickle cell disease or thalassemia → abrupt transient interruption of erythropoiesis → aplastic crisis.

High Risk
Pregnancy

Fetal infection → severe anemia → non-immune hydrops and fetal loss.

High Risk
Immunocompromised

Persistent infection → pure red cell aplasia and chronic anemia. Infectiousness can persist for months or years.

Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

★ Must Know
  • PCR detects viral DNA in serum, blood cells, tissues, and respiratory secretions.
  • IgM turns positive around 7–10 days and suggests recent infection.
  • IgG appears around 15+ days and suggests past exposure and immunity.
  • Chronically infected immunodeficient patients may be antibody-negative.
Management
  • Usually supportive.
  • Severe anemia may require transfusion.
  • Persistent infection in immunocompromised patients may be treated with commercial immunoglobulin preparations containing neutralizing antibodies.
  • Lecture states no vaccine and no antiviral drug are available.
Active recall: When is a child with fifth disease most infectious?
During the viremic prodromal period before the rash. Once the rash appears, antibody production has begun and an immunocompetent patient is no longer infectious.
Active recall: Why can B19 trigger aplastic crisis?
It directly infects and kills erythroid precursors, abruptly interrupting red cell production.

B. Adenoviridae pp. 2–3

★ Must Know

ds linear DNA · icosahedral · non-enveloped. Fibers project from the 12 vertices and mediate attachment and hemagglutinating activity.

★ Must Know

The penton base has toxin-like activity that promotes rapid cytopathic effects and cell detachment. Adenovirus can force a cell into S phase.

Replication

Adenovirus replication sequence
Fiber attachment
Endosome entry
Uncoating begins in cytoplasm, completes in nucleus
Early genes force S phase and block defenses
DNA synthesis in nucleus
Late structural proteins made in cytoplasm
Capsid assembly and DNA packaging in nucleus
Cell lysis

Lecture note: a cell can produce roughly 100,000 virus particles.

⚠ Exam Trap

Parvovirus waits for S phase. Adenovirus pushes the host into S phase.

Immune evasion

VA RNA

Non-coding viral-associated RNA that protects adenovirus from interferon.

E3 proteins

Inhibit cytolysis of infected cells.

E3 gp19 kDa

Blocks MHC I movement to the surface, helping infected cells evade cytotoxic T lymphocytes.

Types 40 and 41.

C. Papillomavirus and Polyomavirus Topics pp. 3–5

Lecture framing

The lecture presents papillomaviruses and polyomaviruses under the older Papovaviridae framework while also noting that the family name is obsolete.

Human Papillomavirus

★ Must Know

Supercoiled double-stranded circular DNA · icosahedral · non-enveloped. HPV is highly specific for surface epithelia of skin and mucous membranes.

★ Must Know

Low-risk 6 and 11 → genital warts and laryngeal papillomas.
High-risk 16 and 18 → strong cervical and other anogenital cancer association.

Replication follows keratinocyte maturation

HPV epithelial life cycle
Infect basal stem cells
Host keratinocyte divides and moves upward
Early viral genes expressed
Viral genome multiplies
Capsid proteins appear in differentiated upper cells
Virions assemble and are shed at superficial layers

Why high-risk HPV can cause malignancy

Persistent high-risk HPV infection
E6 and E7 expression
Binding / inactivation of p53 and pRb
Loss of cell-cycle control + blocked apoptosis + chromosomal abnormalities
Slow progression toward malignancy over years to decades
⚠ Exam Trap

Removing a visible wart does not guarantee eradication because HPV can remain in basal cells of the surrounding tissue. Recurrence is common.

Diagnosis
  • Usually visual inspection.
  • Biopsy if uncertain, immunocompromised, or lesion is pigmented or ulcerated.
  • No tissue culture system for HPV recovery.
  • Typing by DNA amplification such as PCR.
  • Pap smear screens for HPV-associated dysplasia and cancer.
Treatment and prevention
  • Patient-applied: imiquimod, podofilox, sinecatechins.
  • Provider options: cryotherapy, surgical removal, laser, TCA or BCA.
  • Lecture lists quadrivalent Gardasil types 6, 11, 16, 18 and recommends vaccination for young males and females.

Herpesvirus Core Framework pp. 5–6

★ Must Know

Single molecule of linear dsDNA, icosahedral capsid, lipid envelope derived from the host nuclear membrane, plus a protein-rich tegument between capsid and envelope.

★ Must Know

Defining infection pattern: acute infection → lifelong latency → periodic reactivation.

HSV-1 and HSV-2 pp. 6–7

FeatureHSV-1HSV-2
Classic primary siteOropharyngeal regionGenital tract
Transmission emphasizedSaliva, kissing, contaminated fingersSexual intercourse, passage through infected birth canal
Classic latencyTrigeminal gangliaSacral ganglia
★ Must Know

Reactivation triggers listed in the lecture include axonal injury, fever, physical or emotional stress, and UV light. Reactivated virus travels back down axons to skin or mucosa.

Clinical spectrum
  • Recurrent mucocutaneous lesions can progress through pustular and crusting stages and heal without scarring.
  • Eye infection can cause dendritic keratitis, corneal ulcers, and recurrent stromal opacity with risk of blindness.
  • Genital herpes is more often HSV-2 but HSV-1 can also cause it. Painful vesicoulcerative lesions may accompany fever, malaise, dysuria, and inguinal lymphadenopathy.
  • Skin infections: herpetic whitlow in dentists and hospital personnel, herpes gladiatorum in wrestlers, severe eczema herpeticum in chronic eczema.
  • HSV-1 is described as the most common cause of sporadic fatal encephalitis in the US.
Neonatal herpes
  • Can be acquired in utero, during birth, or after birth.
  • Most common route is exposure to herpetic lesions during passage through the birth canal.
  • Most neonatal infections in the lecture are attributed to HSV-2.
  • Patterns: skin-eye-mouth disease, encephalitis, or disseminated multi-organ disease.
  • Disseminated disease has the worst prognosis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis
  • PCR / NAAT is the gold standard in the lecture.
  • PCR can be performed on vesicle swabs, blood, and CSF.
  • CSF PCR is the test of choice for herpes meningitis and encephalitis.
  • Giemsa staining from vesicle base can show multinucleated giant cells but is not type-specific among HSV-1, HSV-2, and VZV.
Treatment

Acyclovir, valacyclovir, and vidarabine are listed as inhibitors of viral DNA synthesis. They suppress manifestations, shorten healing, and reduce recurrences of genital herpes. Antibodies do not prevent reactivation.

Active recall: HSV-1 vs HSV-2 latency?
HSV-1 → trigeminal ganglia. HSV-2 → sacral ganglia.

Varicella-Zoster Virus — HHV-3 pp. 7–9

★ Must Know

Primary infection = varicella / chickenpox. Reactivation of latent virus in sensory ganglia = herpes zoster / shingles.

⚠ Exam Trap

Chickenpox shows lesions in successive crops, so macules, papules, vesicles, and crusts can coexist. This becomes useful later when contrasting smallpox.

Varicella pathogenesis
Upper respiratory mucosa or conjunctiva
Regional lymph nodes
Primary viremia
Liver and spleen replication
Secondary viremia
Infected mononuclear cells carry virus to skin
Generalized vesicular rash
Varicella
  • Incubation 10–21 days.
  • Malaise and fever precede rash.
  • Rash starts on trunk, then face, limbs, and oral mucosa according to the lecture.
  • Potential severe complications in neonates, adults, immunocompromised patients, and pediatric leukemia patients include pneumonia and disseminated disease.
  • Rare encephalitis may be life-threatening.
Zoster

Painful vesicular eruption limited to the skin innervated by a sensory ganglion, often dermatomal. The lecture emphasizes older and immunocompromised patients.

Treatment and prevention

Acyclovir, valacyclovir, famciclovir, and foscarnet are listed. A live attenuated varicella vaccine is described as about 95% effective in preventing severe disease. The lecture also describes a zoster vaccine for older adults and those with chronic medical conditions.

Active recall: Same virus, two diseases?
VZV causes chickenpox on primary infection and shingles after reactivation from sensory ganglia.

Epstein-Barr Virus — HHV-4 pp. 9–10

★ Must Know

EBV spreads mainly through saliva. Initial replication occurs in oropharyngeal epithelium and virus then infects B lymphocytes through the complement C3d receptor.

★ Must Know

EBV early gene expression can drive B-cell proliferation and immortalization rather than killing the cell. A small number of infected lymphocytes persist for life.

Diagnosis
  • EBV-specific serology is the lecture method of choice for most cases.
  • Heterophile antibody detection uses RBCs or latex agglutination.
  • Serologic markers listed: anti-VCA IgG/IgM, anti-EA IgG, anti-EBNA IgG.
  • Tissue gold standard in the lecture: in situ hybridization for EBER.
  • Quantitative NAAT is useful for viral load and post-transplant lymphoproliferative risk.
Treatment note

The lecture describes acyclovir plus prednisolone but explicitly notes no significant benefit on duration of general illness and no proven benefit for EBV-associated lymphomas. Acyclovir can reduce oropharyngeal shedding while the drug is being taken.

Active recall: What receptor does EBV use on B cells?
The complement component C3d receptor.

Cytomegalovirus — HHV-5 pp. 10–11

★ Must Know

CMV is a betaherpesvirus with the largest genetic content among the human herpesviruses in the lecture. Histopathology can show large cells with “owl’s eye” nuclear inclusions.

⚠ Exam Trap

CMV can cause an EBV-like mononucleosis syndrome, but the lecture’s major distinction is absence of heterophile antibodies in CMV.

EBV mononucleosis

Heterophile antibodies are part of the diagnostic framework described in the lecture.

CMV mononucleosis-like syndrome

Persistent fever, myalgia, lymphadenopathy, blood count and liver test abnormalities with heterophile antibodies absent.

Congenital CMV

Congenital disease

CMV can cross the placenta during primary or reactivated maternal infection. Severity is emphasized when infection occurs in the first trimester. Lecture manifestations include jaundice, petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly, CNS defects, deafness, myocarditis, pneumonitis, chorioretinitis, microcephaly, mental and motor retardation, and periventricular calcifications.

Immunocompromised host

Opportunistic disease

High-risk groups include HIV patients, solid-organ and bone-marrow transplant patients, and patients receiving chemotherapy. Listed diseases include retinitis with “pizza pie” appearance, esophagitis, colitis, pneumonitis, hepatitis, polyradiculopathy, transverse myelitis, and subacute encephalitis.

Diagnosis and treatment
  • NAAT / PCR is the standard test in the lecture and can provide quantitative viral load.
  • Higher viral loads favor systemic disease.
  • Serology may use IgM or a fourfold IgG rise.
  • Ganciclovir is listed for life-threatening CMV in immunosuppressed patients.
  • Cidofovir and foscarnet are listed for retinitis and ganciclovir-resistant strains.
Active recall: EBV-like mono but heterophile-negative?
CMV.

HHV-6B. HHV-7 can also cause roseola but is often asymptomatic.
II-B. PoxviridaeCytoplasmic replication · smallpox · mpox · cowpox · molluscum

Poxvirus Core Features pp. 12–13

★ Must Know

Oval or brick-shaped, complex virions with a core and lateral bodies. Genome is a single linear dsDNA molecule.

★ Must Know

Entire multiplication cycle occurs in the cytoplasm. Poxvirus supplies its own enzymes, including viral DNA-dependent RNA polymerase. DNA polymerase and thymidine kinase are also needed.

Poxvirus replication
Attach to cell surface
Fusion → viral cores released into cytoplasm
Uncoating liberates viral DNA
Virus-encoded machinery drives DNA replication and gene expression
Late mRNA → structural proteins and enzymes
Maturation + lipoprotein envelope during budding
Rapid host macromolecular shutoff → cell death
⚠ Exam Trap

Poxvirus is the standout DNA-virus replication exception here because it does not depend on nuclear replication machinery in the way the other DNA viruses in this lecture do.

Immune evasion

The lecture describes poxvirus proteins resembling host defense regulators and countering TNF, interferon gamma, IL-1, and complement networks.

Smallpox — Variola Virus pp. 13–14

★ Must Know

First viral disease eradicated worldwide. The lecture gives 1980 as the eradication year.

Transmission

Most efficient by inhalation. Direct contact with scars or pustular material and fomites are less efficient. Patients are highly infectious in the first week after rash appears following fever.

Lesion progression described in the lecture
1–5 days fever + malaise→Macules→Papules→Vesicles→Pustules

Incubation: approximately 10–14 days.

Mpox, Cowpox, Molluscum Contagiosum pp. 14–15

Mpox

Zoonotic orthopoxvirus. Similar to smallpox but usually less severe. The lecture’s distinguishing clue is pronounced cervical and submandibular lymphadenopathy.

Person-to-person routes listed: skin-to-skin, mouth-to-skin, and close breathing. Diagnosis: PCR.

Cowpox

Natural reservoir: rodents. Cattle and humans are accidental hosts. Milking exposure can cause hand lesions. Lecture clue: deep red hemorrhagic lesions distinguish cowpox from vaccinia.

Molluscum

Benign epidermal tumor with rounded dome-shaped pink waxy umbilicated papules containing caseous plugs. Spread by direct contact, fomites, swimming pools, and sexual contact. Can be seen in AIDS patients.

Molluscum details

Lesions are uncommon on palms, soles, or mucous membranes. Scratching can cause autoinoculation. Lesions may persist up to two years but can regress spontaneously. Diagnosis is usually clinical, with PCR or electron microscopy available. The lecture lists imiquimod cream as treatment.

Active recall: Which poxvirus clue separates mpox from smallpox and chickenpox in the lecture?
Pronounced cervical and submandibular lymphadenopathy.
Active recall: Which DNA virus family in this lecture replicates entirely in cytoplasm?
Poxviridae.
II-C. Hepatitis B Virus — HepadnaviridaeGenome · reverse transcription · serology · treatment · prevention

Virion and Genome pp. 14–15

★ Must Know

Enveloped virion with an icosahedral nucleocapsid. Envelope carries HBsAg. Core carries HBcAg. HBeAg is associated with the nucleocapsid and indicates active replication and high infectivity.

★ Must Know

HBV DNA is circular and partially double-stranded. One DNA strand is incomplete and is associated with a viral DNA polymerase.

HBV replication — the key reason Group 7 “goes back” through RNA
Attach + uncoat
rcDNA enters nucleus
rcDNA → cccDNA
cccDNA transcribed → viral RNAs including pregenomic RNA
Pregenomic RNA encapsidated with core antigen
Reverse transcriptase makes negative DNA strand
Positive DNA strand begins but remains incomplete
Core acquires HBsAg envelope at pre-Golgi membranes → release or nuclear recycling
⚠ Exam Trap

HBV is a DNA virus, but replication includes an RNA intermediate and reverse transcription. Do not confuse this with a classic dsDNA-only replication pathway.

Stability

  • Lecture values: stable at 37°C for 60 minutes and remains viable after drying at 25°C for at least one week.
  • Higher temperature conditions listed as inactivating include 100°C for one minute or 60°C for 10 hours.
  • 0.5% sodium hypochlorite is described as destroying HBV antigenicity within three minutes.

Transmission, Pathology, and Chronic Disease pp. 15–16

Transmission

Infectious HBV can be present in body fluids. The lecture emphasizes blood, semen, saliva, and breast milk, plus perinatal transmission, contaminated needles and instruments, tattooing, ear piercing, and sexual transmission.

Parenteral drug usersHealthcare personnelHemodialysis patients and staffMultiple transfusionsOrgan transplantsInfants of HBV-positive mothersHigh sexual exposureInstitutionalized persons
Acute tissue injury

Lecture microscopy includes spotty hepatocyte degeneration and necrosis, diffuse lobular inflammation, Kupffer / reticuloendothelial hyperplasia, periportal mononuclear infiltration, and local necrosis. The preserved reticulum framework can allow hepatic regeneration over 8–12 weeks.

Chronic and fulminant

HBsAg persisting beyond 6 months with absent anti-HBs indicates chronic infection. Severe chronic disease can progress to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. Fulminant hepatitis can produce extensive necrosis with high fever, abdominal pain, renal dysfunction, coma, and seizures.

HCC clue

Ongoing viral replication, especially failure to clear HBeAg, is associated in the lecture with greater risk of cirrhosis. HCC can appear years after primary infection and may present with weight loss, right upper quadrant pain, fever, and intestinal bleeding.

HBV Serology — Memorize the Patterns pp. 16–17

HBsAg

First serum marker detected after infection. Persistent beyond 6 months supports chronic infection.

Anti-HBs

Indicates immunity. Lecture states it can remain for life. Vaccination produces anti-HBs only.

Anti-HBc

IgM = recent infection within about 6 months. IgG / total anti-HBc persists in resolved and chronic natural infection.

HBeAg / anti-HBe

HBeAg = active replication and high infectivity. Anti-HBe points toward lower replicative activity in the lecture.

Laboratory Testing, Treatment, and Prevention pp. 17–18

Testing
  • Elevated ALT, AST, and bilirubin support the clinical and serologic picture.
  • Most useful detection methods listed: ELISA for HBV antigens / antibodies and PCR for viral DNA.
  • High HBV DNA viral load is associated with active chronic infection and increased cirrhosis / HCC risk.
Chronic treatment
  • Lecture first-line: pegylated interferon alfa-2a, entecavir, tenofovir.
  • Entecavir inhibits HBV polymerase.
  • Tenofovir is a nucleotide analog inhibitor of HBV reverse transcriptase and polymerase.
  • Telbivudine is listed second-line.
  • Lamivudine, also called 3TC, and adefovir are listed third-line polymerase inhibitors.
Postexposure prevention in the lecture
Potential exposure such as needlestick or mucosal contamination
Hepatitis B immune globulin gives immediate passive protection
Hepatitis B vaccine given simultaneously at a different site
Active immunity develops
★ Must Know

Recombinant hepatitis B vaccination is described as the most effective measure to prevent HBV and its consequences. Universal precautions are emphasized for blood and body fluids.

Warning box

Exam Traps

B19 rash = late

The rash is an antibody-phase finding. High infectivity was earlier during viremia.

Adenovirus is not just respiratory

Eye, GI, urinary tract, and severe transplant disease are all in the lecture.

HPV treatment is not eradication

Wart removal does not guarantee viral clearance from basal cells.

Herpes envelope source

The lecture’s key exception is budding through the nuclear membrane.

HSV culture vs CSF PCR

PCR is the gold standard and CSF PCR is the test of choice for CNS HSV disease.

Chickenpox crops

Mixed lesion stages are a high-yield VZV pattern and help distinguish smallpox.

CMV can look like EBV
