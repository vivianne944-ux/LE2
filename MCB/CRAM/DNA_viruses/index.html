1. Orientation: The DNA Virus Family Map

Page 1

MUST KNOW The single split that organizes this whole lecture

Every virus in this trans is a DNA virus, and the lecture divides them by presence of a lipid envelope. This one split predicts environmental resistance, which matters clinically for disinfection and transmission.

Non-enveloped (Naked) DNA viruses
  • Parvoviridae - smallest DNA animal viruses, ssDNA
  • Adenoviridae - dsDNA, fibers at vertices
  • Papovaviridae - Papillomaviridae (HPV) + Polyomavirinae (JCV, BKV, MCV)

No lipid envelope means extreme environmental resistance (parvovirus tolerates pH 3-9 and 56°C for 60 minutes), so fomites and surfaces matter.

Enveloped DNA viruses
  • Herpesviridae - 8 human herpesviruses, all establish lifelong latency
  • Poxviridae - brick-shaped, replicates entirely in cytoplasm
  • Hepadnaviridae - Hepatitis B virus, partly ds / partly ss circular DNA

Envelope is lipid, so these are more fragile environmentally but the lecture stresses HBV is an exception that survives drying for at least a week.

EXAM TRAP Envelope status is asked as a disinfection question

Because parvovirus has no lipid envelope, ordinary inactivation methods fail. It is killed only by formalin, beta-propiolactone, and strong oxidizing agents (oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, chlorine). Do not assume alcohol-type answers work on naked viruses.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY

  • Naked: Parvo, Adeno, Papova (HPV + polyoma). Enveloped: Herpes, Pox, Hepadna (HBV).
  • Parvovirus is the only ssDNA virus in the lecture and the smallest DNA animal virus.
  • Circular genome = HPV/polyoma (supercoiled dsDNA) and HBV (partly single-stranded, not covalently closed).
  • Tegument is the giveaway for Herpesviridae, and it carries enzymes plus transcription factors needed to start infection.
  • Poxvirus alone replicates completely in the cytoplasm - every other DNA virus here uses the nucleus.

2. Parvoviridae - Parvovirus B19

Pages 1-2

Core idea: Parvoviridae are the smallest DNA animal viruses, and the clinically important human pathogen is Parvovirus B19. It has a single defining behaviour - it kills erythroid progenitor cells, so every disease it causes is either a rash or an anemia.

MUST KNOW Stability and disinfection Page 1

  • Lacking a lipid envelope, parvoviruses are extremely resistant to environmental factors and standard inactivation methods.
  • Stable from acidic to basic pH (3-9) and withstands 56°C for 60 minutes, so routine heating does not reliably kill it.
  • Inactivated only by formalin, beta-propiolactone, and strong oxidizing agents - oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, and chlorine.

MECHANISM Replication cycle - why B19 must wait for the host Page 1, Figure 2

The single most important idea: parvovirus cannot push a host cell into S phase by itself, so it is totally dependent on cells that are already dividing rapidly. That is exactly why it targets bone marrow erythroid precursors and the fetus.

1. Entry - virion enters the host cell by endocytosis
↓
2. Uncoating - genome travels to the nucleus and is uncoated there
↓
3. Dormancy - virus waits in the nucleus because it cannot induce S phase on its own
↓
4. Synthesis - once the host naturally enters S phase, the host is forced to synthesize viral DNA
↓
5. Translation and assembly - capsid proteins accumulate in the nucleus, form empty capsids, then encapsulate the genomes
↓
6. Release - the cell lyses to release complete virions, which is why RBC production is interrupted

SHOULD KNOW Receptor sites explain the organs hit Page 1

The P antigen receptor is expressed on mature erythrocytes, erythroid progenitors, megakaryocytes, endothelial cells, the placenta, and fetal liver and heart. That receptor map is the reason B19 causes fetal disease as well as marrow disease.

Culture difficulty: B19 is extremely difficult to culture because only primary erythroid progenitors are permissive for infection, which is why diagnosis is PCR or serology rather than culture.

MECHANISM Pathogenesis - one lesion, three outcomes Page 1

B19 infects immature erythroid lineage cells (adult bone marrow, some blood cells, fetal liver) → viral replication directly kills the cell → RBC production is interrupted → erythrocyte maturation arrest → microscopically visible erythroblast intranuclear inclusions
Then the outcome depends entirely on the host:
├─ Immunocompetent host → transient, mild → Erythema infectiosum (Fifth disease)
├─ Cannot clear the virus (immunocompromised) → persistent B19 infection → chronic anemia
└─ Chronic infection in utero → severe fetal anemia → often fetal death (hydrops fetalis)

SHOULD KNOW Transmission and epidemiology Page 1

  • Portal of entry: respiratory tract.
  • Modes: blood transfusions (including infected blood products such as coagulation factors) and vertical mother-to-fetus transmission. Fifth disease adds respiratory droplets.
  • Epidemiology: occurs year-round in all age groups, especially common in childhood and frequently seen as school outbreaks.

Erythema infectiosum (Fifth disease) - the most frequent manifestation Pages 1-2

MUST KNOW Fifth disease essentials

  • Erythema infectiosum is the most frequent manifestation of Parvovirus B19 and is one of the six classic viral rash illnesses.
  • Primarily affects children aged 5-18 years. Portal of entry is the upper respiratory tract.
  • Transmission is by respiratory droplets, vertically to the fetus, and by blood transfusion or blood-derived products.

MECHANISM Clinical timeline - infectious first, rash later Page 2

Days 1-14: Incubation - lasts 1 to 2 weeks, patient well
↓

↓

↓

↓

↓

EXAM TRAP The rash marks the end of contagiousness, not the start

The intuitive answer is wrong. The contagious period is the prodrome, before anyone suspects Fifth disease. Once the rash is visible, antibody is already being made and an immunocompetent child is no longer infectious, which is why school exclusion after the rash appears is pointless. This is described as a biphasic rash (antibody phase).

SHOULD KNOW Arthralgia - the adult presentation Page 2

Joint pain is more common in adults, especially women, than in children. It presents as a symmetrical, small-joint arthritis of the hands or fingers and can persist for weeks to months, so B19 belongs on the differential for new symmetrical small-joint arthritis after a viral illness.

COMMON CONFUSION Aplastic crisis vs pure red cell aplasia

Aplastic crisis

Abrupt and transient halt of erythropoiesis in a patient with a hemolytic condition (sickle cell, thalassemia). Dangerous because baseline RBC survival is already short. Can be fatal.

Pure red cell aplasia

Chronic marrow suppression in a patient who cannot clear the virus (AIDS, malignancy, transplant). Needs ongoing transfusions and stays infectious for months to years.

Fast distinction: transient crisis on top of hemolysis vs chronic aplasia from failed viral clearance.

Diagnosis, treatment, prevention Page 2

Diagnosis
  • PCR - detects viral DNA in serum, blood cells, tissue, respiratory secretions.
  • IgM turns positive 7-10 days post-exposure = recent infection.
  • IgG appears 15+ days post-exposure = past exposure and immunity.
  • Trap: immunodeficient patients with chronic infection may test antibody-negative, so use PCR in that group.
Treatment and prevention
  • Generally supportive.
  • Severe anemia → blood transfusion therapy.
  • Persistent infection in immunocompromised patients → commercial immunoglobulin preparations containing neutralizing antibodies.
  • Prevention is hand washing, face masks, not sharing drinks or straws.
  • No vaccine and no antiviral drug exist for human parvovirus.

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY

  • ssDNA, naked, smallest DNA animal virus, receptor is blood group P antigen.
  • Cannot induce S phase itself, so it needs rapidly dividing cells - hence erythroid progenitors and the fetus.
  • Erythema infectiosum = slapped cheek then lacy maculopapular rash. Infectious in the prodrome, not once the rash appears.
  • Three severe outcomes: pure red cell aplasia (immunocompromised), hydrops fetalis (pregnancy), aplastic crisis (sickle cell, thalassemia).
  • Adults get symmetrical small-joint arthralgia, especially women.
  • PCR for diagnosis (antibodies may be absent if immunodeficient). No vaccine, no antiviral.

MUST KNOW Structure - each part has a function Page 2, Figure 4

Capsid

Icosahedral shape.

Fibers

Project from each of the 12 vertices (penton bases). Carry type-specific antigens used for serotyping, mediate hemagglutinating activity, and mediate attachment to host cells.

Penton base

Has toxin-like activity causing a rapid cytopathic effect and forcing infected cells to detach from the growing surface. Also carries a group-reactive antigen.

Mnemonic (created as a study aid): Fiber = Find the type (type-specific, serotyping, attachment), Penton = Poison (toxin-like, cells detach), and the Hexon = Huge and most abundant capsid protein used in immunofluorescence.

MECHANISM Replication cycle Page 2, Figure 5

1. Entry and uncoating - the viral fiber attaches, the virion is internalized into an endosome, uncoating begins in the cytoplasm and is completed in the nucleus
↓
2. Early phase - the virus forces the host cell into S phase to hijack its replication machinery, makes gene products for viral DNA replication, and expresses proteins that block host immune defenses
↓
3. DNA synthesis - viral DNA replication occurs in the nucleus
↓
4. Late phase - delayed "L" genes code for structural proteins, which are synthesized in the cytoplasm
↓
5. Assembly and release - empty capsids assemble in the nucleus, naked DNA is packaged inside, and virions are released by host cell lysis

COMMON CONFUSION Adenovirus vs Parvovirus and the S phase

Parvovirus B19

Cannot induce S phase. It waits in the nucleus for the host to divide on its own, so it needs rapidly dividing tissue.

Adenovirus

Forces the host cell into S phase using early gene products, so it does not depend on the cell dividing spontaneously.

Fast distinction: Parvovirus waits for S phase, adenovirus drives it.

The lecture flags this as a key insight: the virus causing EKC can remain viable on surfaces such as sinks and hand towels for several weeks. That is why the correct outbreak answer is single-use paper towels and instrument disinfection, not antivirals - there is no specific antiviral treatment for adenovirus at all.

Gastrointestinal, urinary, and immunocompromised disease Page 3

Pediatric infections
  • Infantile gastroenteritis - accounts for 5-15% of viral gastroenteritis in young children. Serotypes 40 and 41 are abundant in diarrheal stools.
  • Acute self-limited hemorrhagic cystitis - occurs primarily in boys, with hematuria. Virus is recoverable from the urine.
Immunocompromised and transplant patients
  • General transplants (types 1-7) - can progress to severe pneumonia or fatal disseminated infection.
  • Pediatric liver transplant - adenovirus hepatitis in the allograft.
  • Pediatric heart transplant - myocardial infection, often leading to graft loss.
  • Pediatric stem cell transplant - susceptible to a wide variety of serotypes.
  • AIDS patients - frequent severe GI and disseminated infection.

SHOULD KNOW Host resistance - the reinfection paradox Page 3

  • Immunity is tied to circulating neutralizing antibodies, which can persist for life.
  • Clinical limitation: type-specific antibodies protect against disease symptoms but do not always prevent reinfection, so infections frequently occur without overt illness (asymptomatic shedding).
  • Infants are protected against severe respiratory infection by maternal antibodies.

  • Treatment: there is no specific antiviral treatment. Care is entirely supportive.
  • Strict hand hygiene - described as the simplest and most effective measure.
  • Disinfect surfaces with sodium hypochlorite (bleach / Clorox).

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY

  • Naked dsDNA, epithelial only, stays in regional lymph nodes, makes ~100,000 particles per cell.
  • Fiber = attachment, hemagglutination, type-specific. Penton = toxin-like, cells detach, group-reactive. Hexon = most abundant, target of antihexon immunofluorescence.
  • Numbers: 1-7 most common worldwide, 4 and 7 military, 40 and 41 gastroenteritis, 3 and 7 pool conjunctivitis, 8, 19, 37 epidemic keratoconjunctivitis.
  • VA RNA blocks interferon, E3 gp19 blocks MHC I traffic.
  • PCR is the test of choice. No antiviral - supportive care, hand hygiene, bleach, single-use towels, pool chlorination.
  • Antibody prevents disease but not reinfection, so asymptomatic shedding is common.

Pages 3-5

Core idea: HPV belongs to the Papillomaviridae subfamily, one of two genera within the now obsolete family Papovaviridae. It is highly tissue-specific and infects only the surface epithelia of skin and mucous membranes. Everything it does is either a wart (benign hyperplasia) or a carcinoma (via E6 and E7), and the deciding factor is the type number.

MUST KNOW Epidemiology numbers Page 4

  • Genital HPV infection is the most common viral infection of the reproductive tract.
  • Cervical cancer: more than 99% of cervical cancers are linked to HPV. Types 16 and 18 cause more than 70% of these, with Type 16 the most common.
  • Anal cancer: more than 80% are associated with HPV.
  • Oropharyngeal: 25% of mouth cancers and 35% of throat cancers are linked to HPV, primarily Type 16.
  • Benign warts: 90% of anogenital warts are caused by Types 6 and 11.
  • High-risk populations: immunocompromised patients (especially HIV/AIDS) and men who have sex with men carry significantly higher risk of HPV infection, anal cancer, and aggressive disease progression.

MECHANISM Replication follows the keratinocyte, not the clock Page 4, Figure 10

HPV replication is uniquely tied to the natural lifecycle of host skin cells (keratinocytes). This is why warts shed virus from the surface while the reservoir sits deep in the basal layer.

1. Entry and infection - virus targets and infects basal stem cells, the deepest layer of the epithelium
↓
2. Parallel growth - as the keratinocyte matures and moves upward, the viral cycle proceeds in parallel
↓
3. Synthesis - viral early genes are expressed, then the viral genome multiplies
↓
4. Assembly and release - progeny virions are assembled and shed in the most superficial, terminally differentiated keratinized layers of the growing wart

EXAM TRAP Why warts recur after removal

Because HPV remains present in the basal cells of the surrounding tissue even after a wart is removed, recurrence is common. Removing visible tissue does not remove the reservoir.

MECHANISM High-risk HPV malignancy - E6 and E7 Page 4

Cervical cancer and other HPV malignancies develop slowly, often over years to decades. The absolute prerequisite is a persistent infection with a high-risk type, most notably 16 and 18.

Persistent high-risk HPV infection (Types 16, 18)
↓

↓

↓

SHOULD KNOW Immune evasion and site of malignancy

  • The infection is nonlytic - it does not burst the cell - and is restricted solely to the epithelium, so it is highly adept at evading the host immune system.
  • Progression to malignancy occurs primarily in warts located on mucosal surfaces, for example the genital tract. Common, flat, and plantar warts stay benign.
  • Other mucosal lesions can progress to tongue cancer.
  • Laryngeal papillomas are the most common benign epithelial tumors of the larynx, and anogenital warts are benign but sexually transmitted.

Varicella complications
  • Complications are rare in normal children and the mortality rate is very low.

MUST KNOW The one feature that separates mpox from smallpox and chickenpox

  • Mpox was formerly known as monkeypox, caused by the Monkeypox virus, from the same family as smallpox, and is a species of orthopox virus.

MUST KNOW Lesion description, spread, and treatment

  • A benign, epidermal tumor that occurs only in humans, caused by the sole member of the Molluscipoxvirus genus.
  • The virus shape is oval or brick-shaped and resembles vaccinia.
  • Lesions are single or multiple rounded dome-shaped, pink, waxy papules or bumps which are umbilicated and contain caseous plugs.

MECHANISM HBV replication - a DNA virus that uses reverse transcriptase Page 15, Figure 55

1. The virus particle attaches to a cell and is uncoated, and the genome is imported into the nucleus
↓
2. In the nucleus the partially double-stranded genome rcDNA (relaxed circular DNA) is converted to cccDNA (covalently closed circular DNA)
↓
3. The cccDNA serves as the template for all viral transcripts including a pre-genome RNA
↓
4. The pregenome RNA becomes encapsulated with newly synthesized hepatitis B core antigen
↓
5. Inside the cores, the viral polymerase synthesizes a negative strand DNA copy by reverse transcriptase
↓
6. The polymerase then synthesizes the positive DNA strand, but the process is not completed - this is why the genome is partly single-stranded
↓
7. Cores acquire HBsAg-containing envelopes from the pre-Golgi membranes and exit the cell. Alternatively, cores may be imported back into the nucleus to initiate another round of replication in the same cell

MUST KNOW Two replication facts that explain the drugs

cccDNA is the persistent template that keeps making all viral transcripts, and reverse transcription of the pregenome RNA is how the DNA strand is made. That is exactly why the treatment list includes a reverse transcriptase and polymerase inhibitor (Tenofovir) and polymerase inhibitors (Entecavir, Telbivudine, Lamivudine, Adefovir).

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY

  • Only Hepadnavirus infecting humans, causes serum hepatitis, genome is partly ss, partly ds, circular, non-covalently closed with attached DNA polymerase.
  • HBsAg on the envelope and made in excess, HBcAg in the core, HBeAg with the nucleocapsid = active replication and high infectivity.
  • Replication: rcDNA → cccDNA template → pregenome RNA → reverse transcriptase makes the negative strand → positive strand left incomplete.
  • Stability: survives 37°C 60 min and drying at 25°C for a week. Killed by 100°C for 1 min, 60°C for 10 h, or 0.5% sodium hypochlorite in 3 min. UV does not destroy HBsAg.
  • Liver damage is cell-mediated - cytotoxic T cells attack HBcAg and HBeAg on hepatocytes.
  • Course: incubation 45-120 days, preicteric phase days to a week, icteric 1-2 months, tissue restored in 8-12 weeks, 95% of adults clear within about 6 months.
  • HBsAg beyond 6 months without anti-HBs = chronic. Fulminant hepatitis in 1-2% of jaundiced patients within the first 8 weeks, worse with hepatitis D coinfection.

  • Vaccine is the most effective preventive measure. After needlestick, give HBIg and vaccine simultaneously at different sites.

17. Commonly Confused Concepts

Do not confuse Varicella vs Herpes zoster

Varicella (chickenpox)

Defining feature: primary infection with a generalized centrifugal vesicular rash showing all lesion stages at once.
Key clue: child, highly communicable, infectious from just before rash to the first few days of rash.

Herpes zoster (shingles)

Defining feature: reactivation in a partially immune host, rash limited to one sensory ganglion's dermatome, usually unilateral.
Key clue: elderly or immunocompromised, severe pain first, CN V1 in 10-15%.

Fast distinction: generalized and mixed-stage = varicella, dermatomal and painful = zoster.

Do not confuse Smallpox vs chickenpox lesions

Smallpox

Defining feature: lesions all in the same stage of development.
Key clue: incubation 10-14 days, 1-5 days of fever before the rash, spread mainly by inhalation.

Chickenpox

Defining feature: lesions occur in crops, most abundant on the face and less on the trunk.
Key clue: all stages visible simultaneously.

Fast distinction: uniform stage = smallpox, crops = chickenpox.

Do not confuse EBV mononucleosis vs CMV mononucleosis

EBV (HHV-4)

Defining feature: heterophile antibodies present.
Key clue: triad of fever, pharyngitis, cervical lymphadenopathy, plus splenomegaly and Downey cells.

CMV (HHV-5)

Defining feature: absence of heterophile antibodies - flagged as the major distinguishing feature.
Key clue: positive rheumatoid factor and ANA, anemia, thrombocytopenia, abnormal liver function.

Fast distinction: heterophile positive = EBV, heterophile negative with positive RF and ANA = CMV.

Do not confuse Pharyngoconjunctival fever vs epidemic keratoconjunctivitis

Pharyngoconjunctival fever

Defining feature: mild ocular disease in school-aged children from chlorine-deficient pools, serotypes 3 and 7.
Key clue: resolves in 1-2 weeks with no sequelae.

Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis

Defining feature: serious, highly contagious disease of adults progressing to keratitis, serotypes 8, 19, 37.
Key clue: fomites and eye instruments, corneal opacities up to 2 years.

Fast distinction: pool and child and mild = PCF, clinic instruments and adult and keratitis = EKC.

Do not confuse Low-risk vs high-risk HPV

Types 6 and 11 (low risk)

Defining feature: cause 90% of anogenital warts (condyloma acuminata), large but usually benign and often regress spontaneously.
Key clue: in Gardasil to prevent warts.

Types 16 and 18 (high risk)

Defining feature: E6 and E7 inactivate p53 and pRb → carcinoma. Cause more than 70% of cervical cancers.
Key clue: in Gardasil to prevent cancer. Type 16 is the most common.

Fast distinction: 6 and 11 make warts, 16 and 18 make cancer, and Gardasil covers all four.

Do not confuse Adenovirus vs BK virus hemorrhagic cystitis

Adenovirus

Defining feature: acute self-limited hemorrhagic cystitis, primarily in boys, virus recovered from urine.
Key clue: immune status normal.

BK virus

Defining feature: reactivation in the urinary tract of an immunocompromised patient.
Key clue: rarely pathologic in healthy people.

Do not confuse HHV-6 vs HHV-7 in roseola

HHV-6

Key clue: febrile seizures (6B) and HIV acceleration (6A).

HHV-7

Defining feature: also causes roseola but is often asymptomatic, and infects later in childhood.
Key clue: persists in salivary glands.

Fast distinction: both cause sixth disease, but HHV-6B is the main agent and HHV-7 is later and usually silent.

Do not confuse Aplastic crisis vs pure red cell aplasia vs hydrops fetalis (all Parvovirus B19)

Hematologic conditions (sickle cell, thalassemia). Abrupt transient halt of erythropoiesis. Can be fatal.

18. Exam Traps

Pages 1-18

Every trap below is a detail in the trans that flips an answer. None are invented.

TRAP Fifth disease contagiousness is backwards from intuition

The patient is highly infectious during viremia at 7-10 days, before the rash. Once the rash appears, antibody production has started and the immunocompetent patient is no longer infectious.

TRAP Antibody-negative does not mean parvovirus-negative

Immunodeficient patients with chronic B19 infection may test negative for antibodies. Use PCR.

TRAP Naked viruses resist ordinary disinfection

Parvovirus survives pH 3-9 and 56°C for 60 minutes. Only formalin, beta-propiolactone, and strong oxidizing agents inactivate it.

TRAP Adenovirus serotype numbers are deliberately similar

3 and 7 = pool conjunctivitis and also pneumonia and military. 4 and 7 = military recruits. 8, 19, 37 = epidemic keratoconjunctivitis. 40 and 41 = gastroenteritis. 1, 2, 5 (Group C) = pediatric ARD. 3, 7, 21 = pneumonia.

TRAP One serotype, many diseases - and the reverse

A single serotype can cause multiple clinical diseases and more than one type can cause the same illness, so a serotype alone never pins the diagnosis.

TRAP Adenovirus antibody prevents disease but not reinfection

Type-specific antibodies protect against symptoms but do not always prevent reinfection, so asymptomatic shedding is common even in immune people.

TRAP Vaccine availability is population-specific

The adenovirus vaccine is described as available and effective for military recruits, not for the general population. There is no vaccine at all for parvovirus B19.

TRAP Cutaneous HPV warts are benign, with one inherited exception

Progression to malignancy occurs primarily in mucosal warts. Epidermodysplasia verruciformis is the exception - inherited predisposition and squamous cell carcinoma years later on sun-exposed skin.

TRAP Pap smears are still required after vaccination

Pap smears must be performed regularly regardless of the patient's vaccination status, and there is no tissue culture system for HPV recovery.

TRAP HSV type does not prove the site any more

HSV-1 is increasingly transmitted sexually and is indistinguishable from HSV-2 without laboratory testing, and HSV-2 oral infection is increasingly frequent. Genital herpes is more often HSV-2, not always.

TRAP Multinucleated giant cells cannot separate three viruses

Giemsa or Tzanck cytology showing multinucleated giant cells means HSV-1, HSV-2, or VZV. To distinguish, use PCR/NAAT or DFA.

TRAP Latent HSV persists despite antibodies

After primary infection the virus is carried latently in the presence of antibodies, and those antibodies will not prevent infection or reactivation - they may only modify subsequent disease.

TRAP VZIG is prophylaxis only

Varicella-zoster immunoglobulin prevents illness in exposed high-risk patients and has no therapeutic value after varicella has started. The shingles vaccine is the same varicella vaccine at 14 times the potency.

TRAP Zoster patients transmit chickenpox

Herpes-zoster patients can be the source of varicella in susceptible children, not a source of shingles.

TRAP EBV infects B cells but the atypical cells are T cells

EBV targets B lymphocytes via the C3d receptor, while the Downey cells on the smear are large atypical T lymphocytes.

TRAP Acyclovir does not treat infectious mononucleosis

It reduces oropharyngeal shedding only, does not affect immortalized B cells, has no effect on the symptomatology of IM, and gave no significant benefit on illness duration, sore throat, weight loss, or school and work absence.

TRAP EBNA timing decides recent vs past infection

VCA IgM positive with EBNA negative = recent infection. VCA IgG positive with EBNA positive and VCA IgM negative = past infection. All four negative = no previous exposure.

TRAP Congenital CMV is worst with primary maternal infection

HCMV is the most common intrauterine viral infection in women experiencing their first HCMV infection during pregnancy, and severity is most pronounced in the first trimester. Note that CMV crosses the placenta in both primary and reactivated maternal infections, and one third of primary-infected pregnant women transmit.

TRAP Roseola rash comes after the fever ends

The characteristic erythematous macular rash appears when the fever goes away, after 3-5 days of 39-40°C fever. Note that the lecture's own review question option list writes "HSV-6", which means HHV-6.

TRAP Mpox has one feature smallpox and chickenpox lack

Pronounced cervical and submandibular lymphadenopathy occurs in most mpox patients and is not seen in smallpox or chickenpox. The documented mode in the recent outbreak is direct skin contact.

TRAP Cowpox reservoir is rodents, not cattle

Cattle and humans are only accidental hosts, and cowpox is distinguished from vaccinia by deep red hemorrhagic lesions. Human cases may occur without any known animal contact.

TRAP Smallpox vaccination has a time window

Vaccinia is protective if given within 3 days of exposure. Methisazone is prophylactic but not curative. There is no proven effective treatment and Cidofovir is experimental.

TRAP Antigenicity is not infectivity, and UV does not clear HBsAg

HBsAg is stable at pH 2.4 for up to 6 hours but infectivity is lost, and HBsAg is not destroyed by ultraviolet irradiation of plasma or blood products.

TRAP Liver damage in hepatitis B is immune-mediated

The primary cause of hepatic cell destruction is the cell-mediated immune response, with cytotoxic T cells reacting to HBcAg and HBeAg fragments on infected hepatocytes.

TRAP HBsAg positive alone does not mean acute

HBsAg and HBeAg indicate active infection but do not distinguish acute from chronic. You need the anti-HBc class - IgM for acute, IgG for chronic. HBsAg alone points to the incubation stage.

TRAP A DNA virus that uses reverse transcriptase

HBV is a DNA virus, yet the viral polymerase synthesizes a negative strand DNA copy of the pregenome RNA by reverse transcriptase, and Tenofovir is a nucleotide analog inhibitor of HBV reverse transcriptase and polymerase.

TRAP Chronic carriers are not all the same prognosis

Plain chronic HBsAg carriers are described as mild and benign, usually not progressing to cirrhosis, with a favorable prognosis, whereas severe chronic hepatitis (formerly chronic active hepatitis) carries progressive damage, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Risk of cirrhosis is highest when HBeAg is not cleared.

4. HPV in four numbers and one mechanism

Numbers

1-4 cutaneous warts. 6 and 11 = 90% of anogenital warts. 13 and 32 oral. 16 and 18 = cancer, more than 70% of cervical cancers, 16 most common. Gardasil = 6, 11, 16, 18, both sexes.

Mechanism

Infects basal stem cells, sheds from the superficial keratinized layer. E6 and E7 bind and inactivate p53 and pRb → chromosomal abnormalities and blocked apoptosis. Requires persistent high-risk infection. Nonlytic, so immune evasion is easy.

>99% of cervical cancers, >80% of anal cancers, 25% mouth / 35% throat. No tissue culture. Pap smear regardless of vaccination. Epidermodysplasia verruciformis is the inherited cutaneous exception that becomes squamous cell carcinoma.

11. CMV highlights

  • Largest genome of the human herpesviruses, binds Fc of immunoglobulin, species and cell-type specific, owl's eye inclusions in enlarged multinucleated cells.
  • Spread cell to cell, dominantly oral and respiratory, incubation 4-8 weeks, saliva the most common source in children.
  • Most common intrauterine viral infection → cytomegalic inclusion disease, worst in the first trimester, one third of primary-infected pregnant women transmit. Infants: jaundice, petechiae, hepatosplenomegaly, deafness, CNS defects.

13. Poxviruses

Brick-shaped with core and lateral bodies → fuses with the membrane, cores released into the cytoplasm → uses its own DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, DNA polymerase, and thymidine kinase → late mRNA makes structural proteins → envelope acquired by budding → early shut-off of host synthesis kills the cell
Smallpox

First disease eradicated, 1980. Orthopoxvirus, humans are the natural host with no carrier state, spread mainly by inhalation, incubation 10-14 days, 1-5 days of fever then macule → papule → vesicle → pustule with all lesions in the same stage. Highly infectious in the first week of rash. PCR primary, skin lesion specimen. Vaccinia within 3 days, immunity at least 5 years.

The other three

Mpox - zoonotic, milder than smallpox, cervical and submandibular lymphadenopathy not seen in smallpox or chickenpox, spread by direct skin contact, diagnose by PCR.

21. Rapid-Fire Final Recall

Whole lesson

Thirty-six prompts covering the whole lesson. Answer out loud first, then open.

1. Which DNA virus in this lecture has a single-stranded genome, and what is its size rank?
Parvovirus B19. Parvoviridae are the smallest DNA animal viruses, with ss linear DNA, icosahedral capsid, non-enveloped.
2. What is the cellular receptor for Parvovirus B19?
The blood group P antigen (globocyte P antigen), expressed on mature erythrocytes, erythroid progenitors, megakaryocytes, endothelial cells, the placenta, and fetal liver and heart.
3. Why can parvovirus only infect rapidly dividing tissue?
It cannot induce the host cell to enter S phase on its own, so it waits in the nucleus until the host naturally enters S phase. Only then can viral DNA be synthesized.
4. When is a child with Fifth disease infectious?
During the viremia at 7-10 days post-exposure and the prodrome, before the rash. Once the rash appears, antibody production has begun and an immunocompetent patient is no longer infectious.
5. Three high-risk groups for severe B19 disease, and the illness in each?
Immunocompromised → pure red cell aplasia (chronic, transfusion-dependent, infectious for months to years). Pregnant women → fetal hydrops or fetal loss. Hematologic conditions such as sickle cell and thalassemia → aplastic crisis, which can be fatal.
6. What inactivates parvovirus, and what fails?
Works: formalin, beta-propiolactone, and strong oxidizing agents (oxygen, hydrogen peroxide, chlorine). Fails: pH extremes between 3 and 9 and heating at 56°C for 60 minutes.
7. Adenovirus - which structural component carries type-specific antigen and which has toxin-like activity?
The fiber carries type-specific antigens (serotyping), mediates hemagglutination and attachment, and projects from each of the 12 vertices. The penton base has toxin-like activity causing rapid cytopathic effect and cell detachment, and carries the group-reactive antigen.
8. Which adenovirus gene product protects it from interferon?
VA RNA (viral-associated RNA), a non-coding RNA. Separately, E3 gp19 kDa blocks MHC I movement to the cell surface, hiding the cell from cytotoxic T lymphocytes.
9. Adenovirus serotypes for military recruits, gastroenteritis, and epidemic keratoconjunctivitis?
Military: 3, 4, 7 (types 4 and 7, occasionally 3) with a vaccine available. Gastroenteritis: 40 and 41, fecal-oral. Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis: 8, 19, and 37.
10. Which adenoviral eye disease leaves lasting damage, and for how long?
Epidemic keratoconjunctivitis. Acute infection resolves in 2 weeks but corneal opacities may persist for up to 2 years. Pharyngoconjunctival fever resolves in 1-2 weeks with no sequelae.
11. Adenovirus treatment?
No specific antiviral treatment - care is entirely supportive. Prevention is strict hand hygiene (simplest and most effective), sodium hypochlorite on surfaces, single-use paper towels, and pool chlorination.
12. Which cells does HPV infect, and where are virions shed?
It infects basal stem cells, the deepest epithelial layer, and progeny virions are shed from the most superficial, terminally differentiated keratinized layers of the wart.
13. Mechanism of HPV malignancy?
Early genes E6 and E7 from high-risk types bind and inactivate the tumor suppressors p53 and pRb, manipulating cell cycle regulators, inducing chromosomal abnormalities, and blocking apoptosis. It requires a persistent high-risk infection, most notably types 16 and 18.
14. Which HPV types are in Gardasil and what does each pair prevent?
Quadrivalent 6, 11, 16, 18. 6 and 11 protect against most genital warts, 16 and 18 against the majority of cervical and anal cancers. Now recommended for both young males and females.
15. Which cutaneous HPV condition progresses to cancer?
Epidermodysplasia verruciformis - widespread warts with an inherited predisposition that frequently gives rise to squamous cell carcinoma years later, especially on sun-exposed skin.
16. The three polyomaviruses and their target organs?
JCV → CNS white matter (PML), BKV → urinary tract and bladder (hemorrhagic cystitis), MCV → skin neuroendocrine cells (Merkel cell carcinoma). All cause severe disease mainly in the immunocompromised.
17. Which cells does JC virus kill, and how fast is PML fatal?
It carries out a cytocidal infection of oligodendrocytes, the myelin-producing cells, with lesions restricted to white matter. Death commonly occurs within 3-6 months of symptom onset.
18. What is the tegument and why does it matter?
An amorphous proteinaceous layer between the envelope and capsid of herpesviruses. It contains virus-encoded enzymes and transcription factors essential for initiating the infectious cycle.
19. Latency site for each herpes subfamily?
Alpha (HSV-1, HSV-2, VZV): nerve ganglia. Beta (CMV, HHV-6, HHV-7): non-neural, primarily lymphoreticular and glandular tissues. Gamma (EBV, HHV-8): B cells, with HHV-8 specifically latent in and immortalizing endothelial cells.
20. Where does a herpesvirus get its envelope?
By budding through the nuclear membrane. The envelope is described as derived from the host nuclear membrane and carries viral glycoprotein spikes. Completed virions then travel via a vacuole to the cell surface.
21. Latency sites of HSV-1 and HSV-2?
HSV-1 in the trigeminal ganglia, HSV-2 in the sacral ganglia. Reactivation triggers are axonal injury, fever, physical or emotional stress, and UV light.
22. Most common cause of sporadic fatal encephalitis in the US?
HSV-1. High mortality, and survivors often have residual neurologic deficits. PCR of CSF is the test of choice.
23. Most common route of neonatal herpes, and how is it prevented?
During birth, by contact with herpetic lesions during passage through the birth canal. Prevented by Cesarean section in pregnant women with genital herpes lesions. The mother is the most common source in all cases, and disseminated disease carries about 80% mortality.
24. Drug of choice named for HSV, and what do these drugs share?
Acyclovir, Valacyclovir, and Vidarabine. All are inhibitors of viral DNA synthesis. They suppress manifestations, shorten healing, and reduce recurrences of genital herpes.
25. What are the two viremias of varicella?
Primary viremia after replication in lymph nodes near the respiratory or conjunctival entry point, then secondary viremia after replication in the liver and spleen. Infected mononuclear cells then carry virus to the skin.
26. Most common complication of zoster in the elderly, and which zoster makes it worse?
Post-herpetic neuralgia, with protracted pain lasting months. It is especially common after ophthalmic zoster, and the ophthalmic division of the trigeminal nerve is involved in 10-15% of cases.
27. Gold standard diagnostic assay for VZV, and when is blood the specimen?
Nucleic acid amplification test - most sensitive, gold standard, and the recommended test for VZV in CSF. Blood is used in immunocompromised patients with suspected disseminated zoster who lack the characteristic vesicular rash.
28. EBV target cell and receptor?
B lymphocytes, via the complement component C3d receptor. Initial replication happens in the oropharyngeal epithelium first.
29. Classical triad of infectious mononucleosis and the cell on the smear?
Fever, pharyngitis, and lymphadenopathy (anterior and posterior cervical nodes), with splenomegaly and raised liver enzymes. The smear shows large atypical T lymphocytes called Downey cells. Incubation is 4-7 weeks.
30. Single best test to separate EBV from CMV mononucleosis?
Heterophile antibodies. They are present in EBV mononucleosis and absent in CMV, which the trans flags as CMV's major distinguishing feature. CMV also shows positive rheumatoid factor and ANA.
31. Tissue gold standard for detecting EBV, and why?
In situ hybridization targeting EBER, the non-coding EBV-encoded RNA. It is more sensitive than immunohistochemistry because EBER is produced regardless of the stage of infection or latency.
32. Characteristic cytopathic effect of CMV?
Perinuclear cytoplasmic inclusions and intranuclear inclusions. Cells become greatly enlarged (cytomegalic) and multinucleated with "Owl's eye" inclusions.
33. Most frequent clinical signs in a symptomatic infant with congenital CMV?
Jaundice, petechiae, and hepatosplenomegaly. Nervous system damage is the common cause of hearing loss and mental retardation. The disease is called cytomegalic inclusion disease and is worst with first-trimester primary maternal infection.
34. Sequence of fever and rash in roseola infantum?
Sudden high fever of 39-40°C for 3-5 days, and the characteristic erythematous macular rash appears when the fever goes away, starting on chest, back, and belly, then neck and arms, possibly reaching legs and face.
35. Which poxvirus feature explains cytoplasmic replication?
The virus provides all of the enzymes, including a viral DNA-dependent RNA polymerase, for DNA replication and gene expression, plus DNA polymerase and thymidine kinase. So the entire multiplication cycle occurs in the cytoplasm.
36. Which HBV marker indicates active replication and high infectivity, and which marker defines chronicity?
HBeAg indicates active viral replication and high infectivity. Chronicity is defined by the continued presence of HBsAg beyond 6 months with an absence of anti-HBs. The anti-HBc class separates acute (IgM) from chronic (IgG).
