Picornaviridae Foundations

Structure, classification, receptor “canyon,” replication, enterovirus pathogenesis, mutation, and immunity.

What makes a picornavirus

Core properties pp. 1–2

  • Pico = small and the lecture describes picornaviruses as the smallest RNA viruses.
  • Virions are non-enveloped and have an icosahedral capsid.
  • The capsid contains 60 subunits. Each subunit contains VP1–VP4.
  • The genome is single-stranded, positive-sense, non-segmented RNA of about 7–8 kb.
  • The RNA is directly infectious.
  • The 5′ end has VPg, which is needed as the primer or starting point for RNA synthesis.
  • The 3′ end has a poly-A tail, which contributes to RNA stability.

The receptor-binding “canyon”

  • The junction of VP1, VP2, and VP3 creates a surface canyon.
  • The receptor-binding site lies deep within this canyon.
  • The canyon helps protect the binding site from antibodies because it is too narrow for antibody penetration.
  • Host-cell receptors can reach the protected binding site and initiate attachment.

Positive-sense RNA

The genome itself functions as mRNA and can be translated as soon as it enters the cell.

The lecture notes that it is already infectious once inside the cell.

Negative-sense RNA contrast

A negative-sense RNA genome first needs RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to generate positive-sense RNA before translation.

Enterovirus physical characteristics and spread

Physical characteristics p. 3

  • Stable over pH 3–10, allowing survival through the stomach.
  • No lipid envelope, so it is resistant to ether, chloroform, and alcohol.
  • Inactivated by radiation, formaldehyde, and phenol.
  • MgCl₂ reduces thermolability, which the lecture links to vaccines that do not need refrigeration.

Mutation, recombination, and immunity

Must KnowRecombination is possible if co-infection occurs with different enterovirus strains.
The lecture explains that RNA polymerase may jump from one RNA template strand to another when two strains replicate in the same cell.

Mutation and evolution

  • Replication has a high error rate of about 1 error per genome replication.
  • This supports mutation, evolution, immune evasion, genetic drift, and vaccine-derived viruses.
Must KnowNeutralizing IgG persists for life.
IgM appears early.
IgA provides mucosal protection in the GI tract.

Other immunity points

  • Immunity is serotype-specific.
  • Maternal antibodies provide protection through transplacental transfer and breast milk.
  • B-cell deficient patients can develop persistent infection.
  • T cells and macrophages are important for clearance, although T-cell activity may also contribute to immunopathology.

Poliovirus

Neurotropism, clinical spectrum, diagnosis, immunity, vaccination, and lecture epidemiology.

General features and target

  • Enterovirus in the Picornaviridae family.
  • Three antigenic types: 1, 2, and 3.
  • Small, non-enveloped, +ssRNA, acid-stable, and ether-resistant.
  • Inactivated by 55°C for 30 minutes and by chlorine.
  • Humans are the only natural reservoir.
  • Uses the poliovirus receptor PVR, also called CD155.
  • Type 1 most commonly causes paralysis.

Paralytic disease and bulbar involvement

  • Paralysis is typically asymmetric and flaccid.
  • Residual deficits are common.
  • Brainstem involvement can produce incoordination.
  • Painful spasms can occur in non-paralyzed muscles.
  • Bulbar myelitis involves the medulla oblongata and may affect the phrenic nerve, intercostal nerves, and cranial nerves IX, X, and XII.
  • The lecture links this to respiratory compromise, swallowing, and airway-protection problems.
  • Most patients recover within about 6 months, while some retain permanent paralysis.

Immunity, prevention, and vaccines

  • Immunity is type-specific and lifelong, largely humoral and neutralizing.
  • Cross-protection among the three types is limited.

Lecture epidemiology and Philippines notes

  • Before vaccines, the lecture cites 21,000 paralytic cases per year in the United States.
  • Transmission includes fecal–oral spread, pharyngeal secretions, and contaminated water.

Treatment

There are no antiviral drugs for poliovirus infection in the lecture. Management is supportive and symptomatic.

Quarantine is described as ineffective because the large majority of infections are subclinical.

Coxsackieviruses

Group A versus B, classic syndromes, diagnosis, and study cues.

General features and transmission

  • Belong to the Enterovirus genus in Picornaviridae.
  • Single-stranded, positive-sense RNA, non-enveloped.
  • Historically grouped as A and B based on effects in mice. The V2 lecture also notes a Group C.
  • Spread by fecal–oral and respiratory routes and by direct or indirect contact with infected bodily fluids.
  • Virus can be recovered from blood early, throat for a few days, and stool for 5–6 weeks.
  • Many infections are subclinical.
  • Incubation period: 2–9 days.

Coxsackie A: Herpangina

  • Severe febrile pharyngitis with abrupt fever and sore throat.
  • Discrete vesicles and ulcers on the posterior half of the palate, pharynx, tonsils, or tongue.
  • The lecture differentiates it from streptococcal pharyngitis by noting that strep produces exudative tonsillitis with white or yellow pus rather than these ulcers.

Coxsackie A: Hand-foot-and-mouth disease

  • Coxsackie A16 is a common cause, although other strains can also cause HFMD.

Coxsackie B: Pleurodynia and myocarditis

Pleurodynia / Bornholm disease

  • Abrupt fever and stabbing chest pain.
  • May be preceded by malaise, headache, and anorexia.
  • Duration: about 2 days to 2 weeks.
  • Self-limited with complete recovery, although relapse can occur.

Acute myocarditis

  • Coxsackie B is identified as the most common Coxsackie type associated with acute myocarditis.
  • May be fatal in neonates.
  • May cause permanent heart damage.
  • Virus enters myocytes through receptors including CAR, the coxsackievirus and adenovirus receptor.

Hepatitis A Virus

Transmission, enterohepatic pathogenesis, immune-mediated injury, serology, prevention, and lecture mnemonics.

General features

  • Family Picornaviridae, genus Hepatovirus.
  • Small, non-enveloped, icosahedral, positive-sense ssRNA virus, about 27–32 nm.
  • Three genotypes infect humans: I, II, and III.
  • One serotype → lifelong immunity after infection.
  • Stable in the environment and resistant to acid at pH 3, ether, and moderate heat.
  • The lecture describes HAV as more heat-stable than other picornaviruses.
  • Inactivated by sodium hypochlorite and quaternary ammonium formulations containing HCl.

Transmission and epidemiology

  • Main route: fecal–oral via contaminated food, water, or close contact.
  • Outbreak settings include daycares, camps, schools, military settings, and nursing homes.
  • Major sources include raw or undercooked shellfish and contaminated food or water.
  • The lecture cites the Shanghai 1988 epidemic involving about 300,000 clam-associated cases.
  • No chronic carrier state, unlike other hepatitis viruses.
  • Risk groups listed in the lecture: travelers, MSM, drug users, patients with clotting factor disorders, and patients with chronic liver disease.

Key mechanismHAV is not directly cytopathic in the lecture.
The main liver injury is immune-mediated, not direct viral destruction.
  • At peak replication, many hepatocytes can contain HAV antigen without widespread necrosis.
  • Liver damage appears as the adaptive immune response develops.
  • CD8+ T cells are cytotoxic against HAV-infected cells.
  • NK cells can lyse HAV-infected cells.
  • Antibodies mainly limit viral spread and are not presented as the primary drivers of pathology.

Clinical features

  • Incubation: about 4 weeks.
  • Acute inflammatory liver disease with non-specific symptoms: fever, malaise, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, loss of appetite.
  • Weight loss is common.
  • Dark urine is often the first sign prompting consultation, linked in the lecture to bilirubin buildup.
  • May have pale or clay-colored stools.
  • By the third week, most patients feel better.
  • Symptomatic disease and jaundice become more common with increasing age.
  • Children younger than 5 years are often asymptomatic.
  • No chronic infection and no cirrhosis.
  • Most cases are benign and self-limited.
  • Severe or fatal disease is rare and risk is higher in older patients and people with chronic liver disease.

Supportive only. The lecture lists prevention of dehydration, good nutrition, and rest. Fulminant cases require appropriate management and may require liver transplantation.

  • Transmission resembles Coxsackievirus and is mainly fecal–oral.
  • Worldwide distribution, especially in children younger than 1 year.
  • Seasonality: summer–autumn.

  • Family clusters and rapid spread can occur.
  • The lecture lists historically common serotypes and states that 15 serotypes account for about 80% of reported cases.

Laboratory tests and control

  • PCR is rapid and sensitive.
  • Virus can be isolated from throat, stool, or CSF.

Rhinovirus and Parechovirus

Two distinct picornavirus patterns: temperature-limited common cold versus neonatal sepsis-like illness.

Rhinovirus: core properties

  • Common cold virus and most common cause of mild URTIs in the lecture.
  • Positive-sense, non-enveloped ssRNA picornavirus.
  • Now taxonomically included within Enterovirus, although clinically discussed separately.
  • Approximately 150+ serotypes with no cross-protective immunity.
  • Major receptor group uses ICAM-1.
  • Minor receptor group uses the LDL receptor family.
  • Acid-labile below pH 5–6, so it cannot survive passage through the stomach.
  • Best growth at 33°C, corresponding to the nasopharynx.
  • Survives for hours on surfaces.

Rhinovirus pathogenesis and clinical findings

  • Entry through the upper respiratory tract.
  • Replication is mainly limited to the nasal mucosal epithelium.
  • Symptoms correlate with viral load in nasal secretions.
  • Histopathology is minimal: edema, mild inflammation, and increased nasal secretions.
  • Usually benign, but it can rarely cause lower respiratory disease and is a major trigger of asthma exacerbations.
  • The lecture explicitly rejects wet clothes or being drenched in rain as direct causes of a cold. It instead links rainy weather to indoor close contact.

Rhinovirus immunity, epidemiology, diagnosis, control

  • Neutralizing antibodies in serum and secretions develop about 7–21 days after infection.
  • They appear after recovery, so recovery is not dependent on antibody.
  • Protection is type-specific and short-lived.
  • Serum antibodies may persist but titers decline.
  • Reinfection with new serotypes is common.
  • Worldwide in all ages, with fall and spring peaks in temperate regions.
  • Transmission is mainly direct contact through hands and contaminated objects, plus droplets.
  • The lecture emphasizes self-inoculation more than airborne spread.
  • Highest rates occur in children, who often introduce the infection into families.
Laboratory / treatmentSpecific diagnosis is best done by PCR, but it is rarely useful for routine patient management.
There is no vaccine because of the many serotypes and short-lived immunity.
There is no specific antiviral in the lecture.
Experimental intranasal interferon-α reduced family spread by inducing an antiviral state in host cells.

Parechovirus

  • Genus defined in the 1990s.
  • The lecture lists 14 types.
  • Types 1 and 2 were formerly Echoviruses 22 and 23.
  • Highly divergent from enteroviruses, with less than 30% protein sequence identity.
  • Capsid has only 3 proteins because VP0 is not cleaved.
  • Replicates in respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts.

Reoviridae and Rotavirus

Segmented dsRNA, reassortment, rotavirus antigens, NSP4 pathogenesis, diagnosis, and vaccination.

Reoviridae general features

Lecture mnemonicR = Respiratory
E = Enteric
O = Orphan, reflecting the historical mistaken belief that they did not cause disease.
  • Medium-sized, double-stranded RNA viruses with 10–12 genome segments.
  • Non-enveloped with an icosahedral capsid.
  • Includes Rotavirus, Orthoreovirus, Coltivirus, and Orbivirus.
  • The dsRNA genome contains both positive and negative strands and requires viral polymerase to transcribe mRNA.
  • Because the genome is segmented, reassortment can occur when two strains co-infect the same cell.
  • The lecture links reassortment to genetic shift.
Lecture visual reconstruction: segmented dsRNA genome
Rotavirus = 11 segments
Orthoreovirus / Orbivirus = 10
Coltivirus = 12
The segmented genome allows reassortment and genetic diversity. p. 10

Structure and classification

  • Virion size: about 60–80 nm.
  • Rotavirus has a triple-layered capsid.
  • The virion core contains enzymes needed for transcription and RNA capping.
  • Rotaviruses are stable to heat at 50°C, pH 3–9, and lipid solvents according to the lecture.
  • They are inactivated by ethanol, phenol, and chlorine.
  • Proteolytic cleavage increases infectivity.

  • Group A is the most common human pathogen.

  • VP4 and VP7 are neutralizing type-specific antigens.

Caliciviruses: Norovirus and Sapovirus

Cup-shaped +ssRNA viruses and epidemic gastroenteritis.

Family-level features

  • Small, non-enveloped, non-segmented, positive-sense ssRNA viruses, about 27–40 nm.
  • Capsid is made of a single major structural protein, VP1.
  • Distinctive cup-shaped depressions under electron microscopy.
  • The lecture links the name to calyx = cup.
  • Key human members: Norovirus and Sapovirus.
  • Norovirus genogroups GI, GII, and GIV cause human disease. The lecture identifies GII.4 as a major cause of worldwide outbreaks.
  • Sapoviruses are the Sapporo-like viruses associated with childhood gastroenteritis.

Norovirus clinical picture

  • Incubation: 24–48 hours.
  • Duration: 12–60 hours and self-limited.
  • Sudden-onset vomiting.
  • Watery diarrhea.
  • Low-grade fever.
  • Abdominal cramps.
  • Malaise.
  • Main complication: dehydration, especially in the young and elderly.
  • No chronic sequelae.
  • Viral shedding may continue for up to 1 month.

Diagnosis, treatment, prevention

  • RT-PCR is the gold standard.
  • Possible specimens include stool, vomitus, food, and water.
  • EM and IEM have limited use because of low sensitivity.
  • Serology using ELISA and rising IgG is mainly a research tool.
  • Treatment is supportive with fluid and electrolyte replacement.
  • Prevention: rigorous handwashing, safe food handling and cooking, isolation and disinfection of contaminated areas, and safe water supplies.
  • No vaccine is available in the lecture.

Cram Sheet

Fast final recall. High-yield only, with lecture mnemonics and must-know markers preserved.

Picornavirus essentials

  • +ssRNA, non-segmented, naked, icosahedral.
  • Capsid = VP1–VP4 repeated in 60 subunits.
  • 5′ VPg primes RNA synthesis.
  • 3′ poly-A stabilizes RNA.
  • Canyon from VP1–VP3 protects receptor-binding site from antibodies.
  • Replication in cytoplasm, about 5–10 h.
  • +RNA → immediate translation as one polyprotein.

Enterovirus must-knows

  • Stable at pH 3–10 → survives stomach.
  • Entry → pharynx + Peyer’s patches → lymph nodes → minor viremia → major viremia → CNS / heart / skin.
  • 📌 Recombination possible with co-infection.
  • 📌 Neutralizing IgG persists for life.
  • IgM early. IgA protects GI mucosa.

Poliovirus

  • PVR / CD155.
  • Targets anterior horn LMNs.
  • Asymmetric flaccid paralysis + hypotonia + hyporeflexia.
  • No sensory deficits.
  • Does not replicate in muscle.
  • Stool = longest diagnostic yield.
  • CSF: lymphocytic pleocytosis, protein ↑ or normal, glucose normal.
  • PPS occurs decades later and is not viral reactivation.

IPV vs OPV

  • IPV: inactivated, injected, humoral immunity, prevents CNS disease, does not block gut replication.
  • OPV: live oral, systemic + mucosal immunity, interrupts transmission.
  • OPV risk: rare vaccine-associated paralysis / vaccine-derived virus.
  • No antiviral treatment for polio.

Coxsackie

  • A = Above the neck.
  • Triple H: HFMD, Herpangina, Hemorrhagic conjunctivitis.
  • Herpangina = posterior oral vesicles / ulcers.
  • HFMD = oral ulcers + palms / soles vesicles, no crusting.
  • A24 variant → hemorrhagic conjunctivitis.
  • B = Below the neck: pleurodynia, myocarditis, neonatal systemic disease.
  • Coxsackie B myocarditis uses CAR.
  • RT-PCR = method of choice.

Hepatitis A

  • Fecal–oral, shellfish / food / water.
  • Lower intestine → liver → bile / feces.
  • Not directly cytopathic.
  • Liver damage from CD8+ T cells + NK cells.
  • IgM anti-HAV = acute.
  • IgG without IgM = prior infection or vaccine.
  • No chronic infection or cirrhosis.
  • PEP immune globulin within 2 weeks.
  • 📌 Philippines: not in NIP, recommended age ≥1 yr.

HAV memory aids

  • A = Acute.
  • A = Anus-to-mouth.
  • HAV = Handwashing Avoids Virus.
  • A = Adolescents Aboard.
  • A = No Afterlife: usually benign, no chronic disease, no cancer in the lecture.

Echovirus

  • ECHO = Enteric Cytopathogenic Human Orphan.
  • Classic: aseptic meningitis, febrile illness, rash.
  • EV70 → acute hemorrhagic conjunctivitis.
  • EV71 → CNS disease + severe HFMD outbreaks.
  • PCR rapid and sensitive.
  • Serology impractical due to many types.

Rhinovirus

  • Most common cause of common cold.
  • 150+ serotypes.
  • Major receptor = ICAM-1.
  • Minor receptor = LDLR family.
  • Acid-labile.
  • Best at 33°C.
  • Fever uncommon.
  • Reinfection common because immunity is type-specific and short-lived.

Parechovirus

  • Former echoviruses 22 & 23.
  • Only 3 capsid proteins, VP0 not cleaved.
  • Mild GI / respiratory disease, meningitis, neonatal sepsis-like illness.
  • RED HULK = High fever, Irritable, reddish skin.
  • May be missed by routine enterovirus PCR.

Reoviridae

  • dsRNA, segmented, naked.
  • Rotavirus = 11 segments.
  • Reassortment → genetic shift.
  • R-E-O mnemonic: Respiratory, Enteric, Orphan.

Rotavirus

  • 📌 Villus cells of small intestine, not stomach / colon.
  • VP4 = P type.
  • VP7 = G type.
  • VP6 = group-specific antigen.
  • NSP4 enterotoxin inhibits SGLT1 → Na⁺ + glucose remain in lumen → water follows.
  • Villus destruction → immature crypt cells → malabsorption.
  • Incubation 1–3 d.
  • Severe in infants and young children.
  • EIA detects VP6. PCR most sensitive / specific.
  • RotaTeq = oral pentavalent reassortant. Rotarix = oral monovalent human.

ROTA mnemonic

  • R = RNA, Reoviridae, dsRNA segmented.
  • O = Oral-fecal and oral vaccine.
  • T = Toxin NSP4, Tiny People.
  • A = Acute diarrhea in infants.

Norovirus

  • Calicivirus with cup-shaped capsid.
  • 24–48 h incubation.
  • Sudden vomiting + watery diarrhea.
  • As few as 10 particles can cause disease.
  • Outbreaks: cruise ships, nursing homes, schools, shelters.
  • Transmission includes aerosolized vomitus.
  • RT-PCR = gold standard.
  • No vaccine in the lecture.

NORO mnemonic

  • N = Nausea and vomiting.
  • O = Outbreaks.
  • R = Rapid onset and recovery.
  • O = Oral-fecal transmission.

Sapovirus

  • Sapporo-like viruses.
  • Childhood gastroenteritis.
  • +ssRNA, non-segmented, naked calicivirus.
  • Fecal–oral spread.
  • Stool / vomitus specimens.
  • RT-PCR is the lecture’s most-used technique for human caliciviruses.
Last-minute exam trapsPolio weakness is neurogenic, not primary muscle infection.
HAV liver injury is immune-mediated, not direct cytopathic injury.
Rhinovirus is acid-labile and temperature-limited to the upper airway.
Rotavirus is segmented dsRNA. Picornavirus and Calicivirus are non-segmented +ssRNA.
Norovirus outbreaks are compatible with aerosolized vomitus transmission in addition to fecal–oral spread.
