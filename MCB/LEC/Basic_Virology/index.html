General Characteristics

Page 1

Core properties

  • Acellular
    • No cell organelles.
    • No independent metabolic processes required by living cells.
    • The lecture therefore does not consider viruses living organisms.
  • Submicroscopic
    • Cannot be demonstrated with a compound light microscope.
    • Cannot be demonstrated by Gram staining or other ordinary light microscopy techniques.
    • Require an electron microscope for direct visualization.
  • Very small
    • Virions are about 20–300 nm.
    • They can pass through bacterial filters.
    • Filtration alone is therefore insufficient for complete virus removal from water in the lecture explanation.
  • Genetically dependent on the host
    • No genes encode complete protein synthesis machinery.
    • No genes encode the full set of proteins for energy production and membrane biosynthesis.
  • Obligate intracellular parasites
    • Outside living cells they cannot self-replicate.
    • The lecture compares this dependence with Rickettsia, Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia.
  • Resistant to antibiotics.
  • Inhibited by interferon and antiviral agents.

Interferon as a natural antiviral defense

  • The lecture describes interferons as proteins that interfere with viral multiplication.
  • The IFN gene is activated in the infected cell.
  • Interferon is released outside the infected cell.
  • Neighboring cells receive the signal and produce antiviral proteins.
  • These antiviral proteins help destroy viral particles.
High-Yield RecapVirion: 20–300 nm.
Prokaryotic cells: 1–10 μm.
Eukaryotic cells: 10–100 μm.

Virion + Envelope

Pages 2–3

Virion

A virion is the complete infectious viral particle.

  • Naked nucleocapsid virion
    • Capsid forms the protein covering.
    • Nucleic acid is the genome inside the capsid.
  • Enveloped virion
    • Capsid.
    • Nucleic acid.
    • Envelope with protruding surface spikes.

Envelope

  • A phospholipid bilayer derived from a host cell membrane during maturation.
  • Produced during budding and release.
  • All enveloped viruses derive their envelope from host membranes in the lecture.
  • Herpes is the lecture exception for membrane source. It derives its envelope from the nuclear membrane.
  • The envelope contains inserted viral proteins called peplomers.

Protein-coding range

The lecture states that the simplest viruses encode about four proteins, while the most complex may encode about 100–200 proteins.

Enzymes

  • RNA polymerase is important in copying RNA information during viral replication.
  • Influenza is a single-stranded RNA virus that requires an RNA-dependent polymerase.
  • Reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy of viral RNA in retroviral replication.
  • The lecture connects reverse transcriptase with HIV, HTLV, and Hepatitis B in its retrovirus summaries.

Matrix protein

The matrix protein lies between the envelope and nucleocapsid. The lecture states that it may have enzymatic activities or biologic functions related to infection.

Antigenic variants and serotyping

  • Some viruses express different antigens that can be used for serotyping.
  • Lecture example: Hepatitis B virus with HBsAg, HBcAg, and HBeAg.
  • The host immune response is directed against antigenic determinants on viral proteins or glycoproteins.
  • Patient antibodies can help identify the antigenic pattern and stage of disease in the lecture explanation.

Polarity + Genome Replication

Page 6
PolarityMeaningConsequenceLecture examples
Positive sense (+)Same polarity as mRNA.Can function as mRNA inside the infected cell and can be immediately translated by host ribosomes.Picornavirus, Togavirus, Coronavirus
Negative sense (−)Complementary to or antisense of mRNA.Cannot be directly translated. Must first be copied to (+) viral mRNA by virion-associated RNA-dependent RNA polymerase.Rhabdovirus, Orthomyxovirus

Why positive-sense RNA is infectious in the lecture

A purified (+) ssRNA genome can function as mRNA after entering the cell. Host ribosomes can therefore translate it directly into viral protein.

Why negative-sense RNA is not directly infectious

A (−) ssRNA genome is not in the correct orientation for direct host translation. The virion must carry RNA-dependent RNA polymerase to create (+) mRNA first.

Transcription and translation

  • Transcription copies genetic information into RNA that serves as a blueprint.
  • Translation uses mRNA and host cellular machinery to synthesize protein.
  • These processes ultimately produce viral components and progeny.

Viral Replication Cycle

Pages 9–11
Lecture MnemonicAPUSAR = Attachment → Penetration → Uncoating → Synthesis → Assembly → Release.

1. Attachment or adsorption

  • Viral proteins such as spikes on the capsid or envelope interact with specific host receptors.
  • Specificity determines host range and tissue tropism.
  • Lecture attachment examples include SARS and HIV.

2. Penetration

MechanismWho uses it in the lectureWhat happens
Direct fusionSome enveloped virusesVirus adsorbs and fuses with the host cell membrane, releasing the nucleocapsid into the cytoplasm.
ViropexisSeveral enveloped viruses and all naked virusesReceptor-mediated endocytosis. The host membrane invaginates and encloses the virus in an endosome.

3. Uncoating

  • Capsid is degraded or removed by viral or host enzymes.
  • The nucleic acid or genome is released.
  • Uncoating marks the beginning of the eclipse phase.
  • During the eclipse period, infectious particles cannot be recovered from the collected specimen.

4. Synthesis

  • Includes viral genome replication and protein synthesis.
  • This step is the basis for Baltimore classification.
  • DNA viruses usually replicate in the nucleus. The main lecture exception is Poxvirus.
  • RNA viruses usually replicate in the cytoplasm.
  • The main synthesis section lists Orthomyxoviruses and retroviruses as RNA-virus exceptions.
  • Early proteins are non-structural, including enzymes.
  • Late proteins are structural components such as envelope and glycoprotein components.

5. Assembly

  • Structural proteins, genomes, and viral enzymes are gathered into new virus particles.
  • The viral genome is enclosed by the protein coat or capsid.

6. Release

Release routeLecture associationEffect
BuddingEnveloped virusesEnvelope is acquired from host membrane during release.
LysisMost naked virusesCan produce host cell death. The lecture also mentions apoptosis in this context.
Source Consistency NoteThe main synthesis section lists RNA replication exceptions as Orthomyxoviruses and retroviruses.
A later summary table lists Orthomyxoviridae and Reoviridae.
This reviewer preserves the main lesson wording and flags the later summary difference rather than silently reconciling it.

Detection Methods

Pages 12–14

1. Hemadsorption

Hemadsorption means RBCs adhere to the surface of infected cells because the infected cell expresses viral components that can bind RBCs.

2. Hemagglutination

If a virus with hemagglutinin is present, RBCs form a diffuse agglutination pattern. If influenza virus has not grown successfully, the lecture shows a compact dot or button at the bottom of the well.

Serial dilution interpretation from the lecture figure

The lecture’s example shows hemagglutination in samples 1–3 and a negative button in sample 4. The teaching point is the visual distinction between a diffuse positive pattern and a compact negative button.

3. PCR or RT-PCR

The lecture uses polymerase chain reaction for direct detection of virus-specific nucleic acid.
