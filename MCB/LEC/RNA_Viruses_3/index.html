Overview

This lesson is organized around two ecological transmission patterns. Arboviruses depend on blood-feeding arthropods. Roboviruses use rodents as hosts without an arthropod vector. The lecture then moves through Flaviviridae, Togaviridae, Filoviridae, Bunyaviridae, and Arenaviridae.

Must KnowArbovirus is an ecological classification.
It is not a viral family and is not based on genetics.
The three major clinical syndromes emphasized for arboviruses are febrile illness, encephalitis, and hemorrhagic fever. pp. 1–3

Arbovirus Framework

Arbovirus vs robovirus p. 1

  • Arbovirus: arthropod-borne. Blood-feeding arthropods transmit virus from one vertebrate host to another.
    • Examples of vectors in the lecture: mosquitoes, ticks, and sandflies.
  • Robovirus: rodent-borne. The rodent is the host and there is no arthropod vector.
    • Human transmission occurs through contact with rodent body fluids or excretions.

Why geography matters pp. 1–2

Arboviruses have distinct geographic distributions. The lecture ties distribution to the presence of the vector species, reservoir hosts, climate, rainfall, and human activity.

Transovarial transmission p. 2

Some arboviruses are maintained when an infected arthropod passes virus to its offspring through the eggs. Newly hatched mosquitoes may already be infected.

Dead-end host concept p. 2

A dead-end host does not develop enough viremia to efficiently pass virus back to a feeding mosquito.

Examples: Japanese encephalitis, West Nile, and St. Louis encephalitis.

Lecture exceptions: dengue, urban yellow fever, Zika, and chikungunya.

Reservoirs and vectors p. 2

  • Reservoir: natural habitat or host where virus lives, reproduces, survives, and remains a continuous source of infection.
    • Lecture examples: birds, rodents, horses, pigs, and bats.
    • Birds are major hosts for several mosquito-borne encephalitides.
    • Pigs are important in Japanese encephalitis.
  • Vector: vehicle that transmits the virus from one host to another.
    • Lecture examples: mosquitoes, ticks, and sandflies.
    • Mosquitoes are emphasized as prominent in the Philippines.

Flaviviridae

Family and major arboviruses p. 3

The lecture places the following under Flaviviridae → Flavivirus: yellow fever, dengue, West Nile, Japanese encephalitis, St. Louis encephalitis, Zika virus, and several tick-borne encephalitis viruses.

Clinical rangeMild febrile illness and rash can progress across the family spectrum to hemorrhagic fever, shock syndrome, hepatitis, and encephalitis.

Flavivirus structure p. 3

FeatureLecture detail
ShapeSpherical
SizeAbout 50 nm in diameter
EnvelopeEnveloped
GenomeLinear +ssRNA
Meaning of +ssRNAThe RNA genome is infectious and functions directly as mRNA for translation.

What +ssRNA means here

The genome can be used directly by the host translation machinery. The lecture therefore notes that no transcription step is required before the initial translation event.

Study cue+ssRNA → direct translation in the lecture’s flavivirus framework.
Must KnowFlavivirus replication uses APUSAR: Attachment → Penetration → Uncoating → Synthesis → Assembly → Release. p. 4

Flavivirus replication, step by step p. 4

Attachment: virus attaches to its receptor.
Penetration: receptor-mediated endocytosis → enclosure in an endosome → endosomal acidification → pH-dependent fusion in the endosome.
Uncoating: viral nucleocapsid is released into the cytoplasm.
Synthesis: +ssRNA functions directly as mRNA → one large polyprotein is produced → polyprotein is cleaved into structural and non-structural proteins → RNA replication follows. In dengue, NS1 means non-structural protein 1.
Assembly: structural proteins assemble with RNA → envelope is acquired from the ER → particle moves through the Golgi → maturation involves further cleavage of viral proteins.
Release: mature virions are released by exocytosis.
Lecture emphasisFamiliarize yourself with the geographic distribution of arboviruses. The lecture maps yellow fever to tropical Africa and the Americas, Japanese encephalitis to South and Southeast Asia, St. Louis encephalitis to the Americas, and West Nile across Africa, Europe, Asia, the Middle East, and the Americas.

Enzootic cycle and amplifying host p. 8

1. Waterbirds
Egrets and herons participate in the enzootic cycle.
2. Mosquitoes
Mosquitoes acquire and transmit the virus.
3. Pigs amplify
Pigs develop high and prolonged viremia, so feeding mosquitoes can acquire enough virus.
4. Humans are dead-end hosts
Humans and other vertebrate animals are incidental targets and do not maintain the transmission cycle.

Clinical manifestations and neuropathology p. 8

  • Clinical spectrum
    • Most infections are asymptomatic or mild.
    • Possible disease includes nonspecific febrile illness, aseptic meningitis, seizures, encephalitis, and poliomyelitis-like flaccid paralysis.
    • Death can occur, especially in children, within 3–5 days of CNS symptoms.
  • What happens in the brain
    • Neuronal degeneration and necrosis.
    • Microglial nodule formation.
    • Perivascular parenchymal leukocyte infiltrates.
    • Focal hemorrhage.
    • Damage reflects direct cytopathic effects plus inflammatory injury.
    • Gray matter in the cortex, midbrain, and brainstem is emphasized.
  • Long-term sequelae
    • Chronic seizures.
    • Motor dysfunction.
    • Neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Genetic change and vector fitness p. 9

The lecture describes a single amino-acid change in the E1 glycoprotein from alanine to valine at position 226 as associated with improved fitness in Aedes albopictus. The lecture flags 266 in Jawetz as a typo and gives 226 as the correct position.

Aedes albopictus is described as having a broader geographic distribution than Aedes aegypti.

Key DifferentiatorChikungunya → synovial tissue of joints → severe polyarthralgia.
Polyarthralgia is the lecture’s distinguishing symptom.

Clinical manifestations p. 9

  • Typical onset
    • Very rapid onset.
    • Fever, nausea, headache, and joint pains, especially in the extremities.
  • Rash
    • About half of patients develop a rash.
  • Joint disease
    • Joint pains may be prolonged and recur.
    • The lecture cue is Chikungunya → synovial tissue of joints → severe polyarthralgia.
  • Neurologic disease
    • Not common.
    • Children may have seizure or encephalopathy.
  • Mortality
    • Generally not life-threatening.
    • Estimated at about 1 case per 1000 infected in the lecture.

Pathogenesis p. 9

1. Virus infects endothelial cells, macrophages, and local tissues.
2. Macrophages carry virus to secondary lymph glands, where replication occurs.
3. A pro-inflammatory innate response produces cytokines and activates adaptive immunity.
4. Virus enters the circulation and disseminates.
5. Infected monocytes and macrophages carry virus to synovial tissue, producing inflammation and polyarthralgia.
6. Adaptive immunity clears infection and establishes immunity.

Cell tropism and organ effects p. 10

Arbovirus framework

  • Definition
    • Arboviruses are an ecological group, not a viral family.
    • Transmission occurs through blood-feeding arthropods.
  • Three major clinical syndromes
    • Febrile illness.
    • Encephalitis.
    • Hemorrhagic fever.
  • General pathogenesis
Mosquito bite
Local replication
Regional lymph node → further replication
Viremia → virus reaches blood
Target organ → depends on viral tropism
Clinical disease
Dead-end host: does not develop enough viremia to efficiently pass virus back to a feeding mosquito.
Diagnosis: PCR and serology are the major lecture anchors.
Prevention: vector control, personal protection, and vaccination where available.

Filoviridae: Ebola and Marburg

  • Structure
    • Filum = thread.
    • Pleomorphic, long filamentous forms.
    • Single-stranded negative-sense RNA.
    • Seven genes: NP, VP35, VP40, GP, VP30, VP24, polymerase L.
  • Transmission and pathogenesis
    • Direct contact with blood or body fluids.
    • Early targets: macrophage-system cells, dendritic cells, interstitial fibroblasts, and endothelial cells.
    • Later targets: liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys.
    • Lecture must-know: both internal and external bleeding.
  • Reston strain
    • Detected in monkeys imported from the Philippines in the lecture history.
    • Low pathogenicity for humans.
    • Ebola Reston virus is also noted in pigs in the Philippines.
  • Diagnosis and safety
    • ELISA for antibody or antigen.
    • RT-PCR.
    • Patient specimens can contain virulent virus.
    • BSL-4 maximum containment is emphasized.
  • Control and care
    • Traditional funeral practices can drive transmission.
    • Safe body isolation and modified burial protocols reduce spread.
    • Hospital isolation is described as the most effective outbreak-control measure.
    • Support renal function and electrolyte balance, and combat hemorrhage and shock.
