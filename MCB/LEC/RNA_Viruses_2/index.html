Review: Classification of Viruses

Baltimore classification and the lecture’s RNA-virus orientation.

General classification features p. 1

  • Capsid symmetry: icosahedral or helical.
  • Envelope: present or absent.
    • Envelopes are generally derived from the host cell.
    • Because of this, enveloped viruses are easily deactivated by chemicals or antimicrobials.
  • Genome architecture: single- or double-stranded, positive- or negative-sense.

Baltimore classification p. 1

The system was founded by David Baltimore. It is non-phylogenetic. It classifies viruses according to genome type and method of replication.

Rubella Virus

Structure, postnatal infection, immunity, diagnosis, congenital rubella syndrome, and prevention.

Core virology p. 2

  • Single-stranded, positive-sense, nonsegmented, enveloped RNA virus.
  • Sole member of genus Rubivirus in Matonaviridae.
  • Morphology and physicochemical properties resemble togaviruses, but rubella is not transmitted by arthropods.
  • Only one serotype has been identified.
  • Classified into two related clades and nine genotypes.
  • Three immunogenic antigens:
    • E1 envelope antigen
    • E2 envelope antigen
    • C capsid antigen

Postnatal rubella

Transmission and pathogenesis p. 2

  • Transmitted by direct or droplet contact with respiratory secretions.
  • Neonatal, childhood, and adult infections enter through the mucosa of the upper respiratory tract.
  • Initial replication occurs in the respiratory tract, then multiplication occurs in the cervical lymph nodes.
  • Viremia: develops after about 7–9 days.
  • Viremia lasts until antibody appears around day 13–15.
  • The appearance of antibody coincides with the rash, which supports an immunologic basis for the rash.
  • After rash appears, virus remains detectable mainly in the nasopharynx, where it can persist for weeks.
  • 20–50% of primary infections are subclinical.

Immunity

Antibody kinetics p. 3
Rubella antibodies appear as the rash fades.
Antibody titers rise during the next 1–3 weeks.
Initial IgM does not persist beyond about 6 weeks.
IgG persists for life.
  • One attack gives lifelong immunity because there is one antigenic type.
  • Immune mothers transfer antibody to offspring.
  • Maternal antibody protects the infant for about 4–6 months.

Serologic principles p. 3

  • There is little antigenic variation among rubella strains circulating worldwide.
  • Antigen prepared from one strain can detect antibodies induced by other rubella strains.
  • Rubella-specific IgM appears after primary infection or vaccination.
  • Rubella-specific IgG can be detectable from about day 4 after rash onset and peaks around 1–2 weeks.

Congenital Rubella Syndrome

Pathogenesis and pathology p. 3

  • Maternal viremia during pregnancy can infect the placenta and fetus.
  • A limited number of fetal cells are infected.
  • Infected fetal cells have a reduced growth rate.
  • This causes deranged and hypoplastic organ development, producing structural anomalies.
  • Timing determines teratogenic severity.
    • Earlier pregnancy → greater fetal damage because organogenesis is occurring.
    • After the 20th week → birth defects are uncommon.

SARS-CoV-2 transmission and replication

Transmission cue p. 5
Crowded spaces facilitate easier person-to-person transmission.

Replication cycle p. 5

The lecture emphasizes that replication occurs in the cytoplasm.

  1. SARS-CoV-2 uses its S glycoprotein to attach to ACE2 on target cells such as those in the lungs, heart, and kidneys.
  2. The virus may be internalized by absorptive endocytosis, releasing its genome into the cytoplasm.
  3. Alternatively, S can promote fusion of the viral envelope with the cell membrane and allow direct RNA entry.
  4. Viral RNA is translated into polyproteins.
  5. Polyproteins are cleaved into functional proteins that form the replication-transcription complex, RTC.
  6. The RTC drives genomic replication and subgenomic transcription.
  7. S, E, and M structural proteins are translated and assemble with nucleocapsid in the ER-Golgi intermediate compartment, ERGIC.
  8. New virions acquire their envelope and are released.

Host immune response to SARS-CoV-2

  • Both innate and adaptive immunity are activated.
  • Adaptive immunity is described as predominantly Th1-mediated.
  • CD8+ cytotoxic T cells and CD4+ helper T cells participate.
  • B cells produce neutralizing antibodies.
  • Excessive innate and adaptive activation raises cytokines and chemokines.
  • A cytokine storm can cause lymphopenia through T-cell necrosis or apoptosis.
  • The inflammatory response can progress to viral sepsis, lung injury, and other serious complications.
  • Disease severity is associated with reduced circulating B cells, CD4+ cells, and CD8+ cells.

Paramyxoviridae: Core Concepts

Classification, virion structure, attachment and fusion, transcription, maturation, syncytia, and inclusion bodies.

Core genome features pp. 5–6

  • Single-stranded, negative-sense, nonsegmented, enveloped.
  • Group V in the Baltimore classification.
  • Lecture notes relation to Rhabdoviridae, Bornaviridae, and Filoviridae.

Antigenic pattern p. 6
Most members are monotypic, meaning a single serotype.
The lecture states that all are antigenically stable.

Structure and attachment proteins

  • Important components highlighted in the lecture:
    • Matrix protein
    • Fusion protein
    • Hemagglutinin-neuraminidase
  • HN, H, and G proteins are attachment proteins.
  • Attachment proteins plus the fusion protein allow host-cell attachment, entry, and fusion of adjacent cells.
Fusion protein p. 6
The fusion protein forms giant or syncytial cells.
This cytopathic effect is important for recognition of paramyxovirus infection.

Replication

Attachment, penetration, and uncoating p. 6

  1. Viruses attach through HN, H, or G attachment glycoproteins.
  2. For measles, the lecture gives CD46 or CD150 as receptors.
  3. The virion envelope fuses with the cell membrane through the F1 cleavage product.
  4. F1 undergoes refolding at neutral pH.
  5. This directly releases the nucleocapsid into the cell.
  6. Cleavage of the F0 precursor is essential. Without it, fusion and infection cannot occur.
  7. This allows paramyxoviruses to bypass endosomal internalization.

Transcription, translation, and RNA replication p. 6

  • Because the genome is negative-sense RNA, viral RNA polymerase transcribes mRNA in the cytoplasm.
  • Nuclear functions and primers are not required for this transcription.
  • Viral proteins are synthesized in the cytoplasm.
  • Glycoproteins are glycosylated through the secretory pathway.
  • The genome is nonsegmented, so genetic reassortment through segment reshuffling does not occur.
  • HN/H/G and F proteins show minimal antigenic variation over time.
Primary distinction from orthomyxoviruses p. 6
The lecture states that paramyxoviruses do not undergo antigenic drift.
Their RNA polymerase is error-prone, but many mutations are not tolerated because of structural and functional constraints.

Maturation

  • Virions mature by budding from the cell surface.
  • Progeny nucleocapsids form in the cytoplasm and move to the cell surface.
  • They are attracted to membrane regions containing viral HN/H/G and F0 glycoprotein spikes.
  • M protein links viral envelope and nucleocapsid and is essential for particle formation.
  • Most host proteins are excluded from the membrane during budding.
Neuraminidase activity p. 7
Neuraminidase activity is present in the HN protein of parainfluenza and mumps viruses.
It prevents self-aggregation of virus particles.
The lecture states that it is absent in other paramyxoviruses.

Syncytium formation and inclusion bodies

  1. Infected cells express F0 proteins on the surface.
  2. Host proteases cleave and activate F0.
  3. Activated fusion protein fuses adjacent cell membranes.
  4. This creates large multinucleated cells called syncytia.
  5. Fusion also allows efficient cell-to-cell viral spread.
Hallmark p. 7
Syncytium formation is a hallmark and common response to paramyxovirus infection.
  • Acidophilic cytoplasmic inclusions are regularly formed.
  • Inclusion bodies contain recognizable nucleocapsid and viral proteins.
  • Inclusion bodies are sites of viral synthesis.
  • The lecture reinforces that RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm.
  • Measles additionally produces intranuclear inclusions.

Disease pattern by type, immunity, diagnosis, and epidemiology.

General pathogenesis p. 7

  • Viremia is uncommon and may not occur.
  • Disease may remain in the nose and throat and produce a common-cold syndrome.

Newcastle disease virus p. 7

  • Avian paramyxovirus.
  • Can cause conjunctival inflammation in humans.
  • Described as an occupational disease in people handling infected birds.

Immunity

  • Types 1, 2, and 3 are distinct serotypes with little cross-neutralization.
  • Antibody to one type does not prevent infection with another type.
  • Maternal antibodies and antibodies from prior infection do not fully prevent infection or reinfection.
  • They can, however, modify disease severity, often producing a nonfebrile URTI.
  • Secretory IgA gives short-term protection.
  • Reinfections are common even in adults.
  • Serum antibodies are made to HN and F surface proteins.
  • Repeated infections can produce a less specific antibody response because of shared antigenic determinants with related viruses such as mumps, complicating serologic diagnosis.

Epidemiology

Most prevalent type p. 7
Parainfluenza type 3 is the most prevalent.
  • About two-thirds of infants are infected with type 3 during the first year of life.
  • By age 2, children commonly have antibodies to type 3.
  • Types 1 and 2 are less prevalent.
  • Before age 5, most children have acquired infection with types 1 and 2.
  • Reinfections continue through childhood and adulthood and usually cause mild URTI.
  • Transmission is by direct person-to-person contact or large-droplet aerosols.
  • The lecture highlights occurrence among school children and in hospitals.

Mumps Virus

Pathogenesis, clinical findings, immunity, diagnosis, and prevention.

Pathogenesis and pathology

  • Humans are the only natural hosts.
  • Initial replication occurs in upper respiratory epithelial cells.
  • Infection of mononuclear cells in regional lymph nodes promotes viremia.
  • Viremia disseminates virus to the salivary glands and other organs.
  • Systemic target tissues described in the lecture include:
    • Salivary glands
    • Testes
    • Ovaries
    • Pancreas
    • Mammary glands
    • Central nervous system
Do not over-assume parotitis p. 8
Parotid-gland involvement is not obligatory.
A patient can have mumps without parotitis.
The submandibular glands may also be involved.
Incubation and shedding p. 8
Incubation period: 14–18 days.
Virus can be shed in saliva from 3 days before to 9 days after onset of parotid or salivary-gland swelling.
About one-third of infections are inapparent but can still shed virus.

Why transmission is difficult to control p. 8

  • Long and variable incubation period.
  • Virus is present in saliva before symptoms.
  • Many infections are asymptomatic but infectious.

Signs and symptoms

  • Mild pain in front of the neck or in front of the ears.
  • Salivary or parotid swelling and soreness.
  • Trouble chewing.
  • Fever, headache, myalgia, fatigue, loss of appetite.
  • Testicular pain and tenderness in males.

Clinical findings and complications

  • About one-third are subclinical.
  • Most infections in children under 2 years are subclinical.
  • May cause swelling of one parotid gland and other salivary glands.
  • CNS involvement: 10–30%.
  • Can cause aseptic meningitis that may resolve spontaneously.
  • After puberty, testes and ovaries may be involved.
  • Among males, orchitis may develop, often unilateral.
  • Pain occurs because inflamed testes swell within the tight tunica albuginea.

Immunity

  • A single infection gives permanent immunity.
  • Only one antigenic type.
  • No antigenic variation.
  • Antibodies after natural infection:
    • HN glycoprotein antibody: develops slowly at about 4 weeks after onset and persists for years.
    • F glycoprotein antibody.
    • NP nucleocapsid antibody: earliest, about 3–7 days after onset, and disappears within about 6 months.
  • Subclinical infection can still generate lifelong immunity.
  • Maternal passive immunity helps explain why mumps is rare under about 6 months of age.

Laboratory diagnosis

MethodInterpretation

ELISA / HIDetection of antibody alone is not adequate for diagnosis.

IgMPresent in early illness up to around 60 days.

Prevention and control

  • Best immunization approach: live attenuated mumps vaccine.
  • Vaccine produces a subclinical, noncommunicable infection.
  • Usually given as the combined MMR live-virus vaccine.

Measles Virus — Rubeola

Pathogenesis, rash, Koplik spots, complications, immune effects, diagnosis, and prevention.

Do not confuse p. 8
Measles = rubeola.
Rubella = German measles.

Pathogenesis and pathology

  • Humans are the only natural host.
  • Virus enters through the respiratory tract and multiplies locally.
  • It spreads to regional lymph nodes for continued multiplication.
  • Primary viremia disseminates virus to the reticuloendothelial system.
  • Secondary viremia seeds epithelial surfaces including skin, respiratory tract, and conjunctiva, where focal replication occurs.
  • Viremia is not confined to the respiratory tract.

  • Discrete pink maculopapules may coalesce into blotches.
  • Lesions become brownish within about 5–10 days.

Nipah Virus and Hendra Virus

Henipavirus epidemiology, transmission, symptoms, risk reduction, and pathogenesis.

Nipah virus

Background and reservoir pp. 10–11

  • “Nipah” comes from the name of a Malaysian village.
  • Nipah can spread between animals and people.
  • Carried by fruit bats of genus Pteropus, also called flying foxes.
  • First discovered in 1999 after an outbreak in pigs and people in Malaysia and Singapore.
  • About 300 people became ill and more than 100 died in that outbreak.
  • Lecture outbreak chain: bats → pigs → people working closely with infected pigs.
  • Reported outbreaks in Bangladesh, India, Malaysia, Philippines, and Singapore.
  • Fruit bats carrying Nipah occur throughout Asia, the South Pacific, and Australia, so cases may occur where no prior outbreak has been reported.

Signs and symptoms

  • Disease ranges from mild to severe and can be fatal.
  • People are typically ill for about 3–14 days.
  • Initial symptoms include fever, headache, cough, sore throat, and difficulty breathing.
  • Encephalitis may appear later.
  • Neurologic manifestations include confusion, drowsiness, and seizures.
  • Patients with severe neurologic symptoms may progress to coma within 24–48 hours.
  • Symptoms usually start about 4–14 days after infection.

How Nipah spreads

  • Direct contact with infected animals such as bats or pigs.
  • Consumption of food or drinks contaminated by infected animals.
    • Examples: fruit or raw date-palm sap.
  • Close contact with body fluids of an infected person.
  • A spillover event is the initial animal-to-person spread.
  • Once a person is infected, person-to-person transmission can occur.

Risk reduction

Bat-to-human

  • Wash hands with soap and water.
  • Avoid contact with flying fox bats and sick pigs.
  • Avoid bat-roosting areas.
  • Avoid touching items that may be soiled by bats.
  • Avoid raw date-palm sap or potentially contaminated fruit.

Animal-to-human and human-to-human

  • Wear gloves and protective clothing when handling sick animals or tissues and during slaughter or culling.
  • Avoid contact with infected pigs.
  • Protect pig feed and sheds from bats when feasible.
  • Avoid close unprotected physical contact with infected people.
  • Wash hands after caring for or visiting sick people.

Hendra virus

  • First identified in a 1994 outbreak of lung and brain disease in horses and people in Hendra, Australia.
  • The lecture compares it with Nipah but emphasizes the important horse link.
  • Pteropus flying foxes maintain the virus in nature.
  • Virus has been found in all four Australian flying-fox species.
  • Flying foxes may infect horses by bites or through exposure to urine, droppings, or saliva.
  • Spread from flying foxes to horses is limited to coastal and forested regions of Queensland and New South Wales.
  • Horses can transmit infection to people.
  • Human infection is rare. The lecture notes only seven reported human cases.
Species-to-species transmission p. 12
The lecture lists horse-to-horse and human-to-human under species-to-species transmission.

Signs and symptoms

Clinical course p. 12
Symptoms typically begin 9–16 days after contact with an infected horse.
Hendra can cause severe flu-like lung illness.
Some cases progress to encephalitis.
The lecture reports a 57% mortality among infected people.

Henipavirus pathogenesis in humans

  1. Initial stage: respiratory tract, especially bronchi and alveoli, becomes involved and inflammatory mediators are released.
  2. Systemic stage: virus disseminates through the bloodstream either freely or attached to leukocyte surfaces.
  3. Late stage: virus reaches organs including spleen, kidney, and CNS. The blood-brain barrier is disrupted and cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β are released, producing neurologic signs.

Exposure risk

  • People living in areas with Australian flying foxes.
  • People who have contact with horses that may have been exposed to flying foxes.

Human Respiratory Syncytial Virus — hRSV

Detailed airway pathogenesis, bronchiolitis, cytopathic effects, neutrophils, immunity, and diagnosis.

Basic pathogenesis p. 13

  • Replication begins in epithelial cells of the nasopharynx.
  • Virus spreads to the lower respiratory tract.
  • Viremia is rare.
  • Incubation period: 3–5 days.
  • Young children may have high viral titers in respiratory secretions.

RSV pathogenesis in the lungs

  1. RSV enters the upper respiratory tract and mainly targets ciliated cells of the large airways.
  2. Infected cells round up and detach from the apical side of the airway epithelium.
  3. Infection extends to the lower respiratory tract.
  4. Bronchioles support more efficient viral replication.
  5. Sloughing of ciliated bronchial cells increases debris in the airway lumen.
  6. Debris contributes to airway obstruction.
  7. Virus and cytokines gain access to airway smooth-muscle cells.
  8. This promotes inflammation and hyperreactivity and further narrows already-small airways.
  9. RSV infection of basal cells induces goblet-cell proliferation.
  10. Goblet cells produce high mucus levels and recruit eosinophils and neutrophils.
  11. Activated neutrophils release neutrophil extracellular traps, NETs.
  12. Mucus, debris, inflammatory cells, and NET components accumulate in small airways.
  13. The result is further bronchial obstruction, alveolar collapse, acute inflammation, and lung pathology.

Giant cells and inclusions p. 14

  • There are many giant cells in RSV infection, fitting the typical paramyxovirus pattern.
  • Intracytoplasmic inclusions are present and indicate viral replication.
  • Severe RSV can require hospitalization in infants.

Neutrophils in severe RSV bronchiolitis

  • Neutrophil degranulation releases enzymes that increase inflammation and epithelial-cell death.
  • The lecture figure links four neutrophil actions to severe disease:
    1. Excess inflammatory cytokine production
    2. Degranulation
    3. Reactive oxygen species production
    4. NETosis
  • These are associated with lung inflammation, systemic fever, mucus hypersecretion, airway obstruction, epithelial-cell death, and greater lung damage.

Immunity

  • Maternal neutralizing antibody can protect against lower respiratory-tract infection.
  • As maternal antibody falls after about 2–4 months, severe RSV disease may occur.
  • Primary infection with one paramyxovirus subfamily can induce cross-reactive antibodies to other subgroups.
  • hRSV infection does not produce an effective memory response that reliably protects from future exposures.
  • During convalescence, circulating RSV IgG is present.
  • No IgA-producing memory B cells are described in the lecture.
  • Lack of IgA memory may contribute to recurrent infections, particularly in childhood.
  • Secretory IgA is associated with epithelial surfaces lining the airways.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Clinical symptoms are nonspecific and overlap with other viral and some bacterial respiratory infections.
  • Commonly used tests:
    • rRT-PCR — more sensitive than culture and antigen testing.
    • Antigen testing — sensitive in children, less sensitive in adults.
  • Less commonly used:
    • Viral culture
    • Serology, mainly for research and surveillance

Orthomyxoviridae and Influenza: Structure & Classification

Segmented genome, reassortment, structure, HA and NA, types, hosts, and nomenclature.

Core genome feature p. 16
Orthomyxoviridae are single-stranded, negative-sense, segmented, enveloped viruses.
The lecture repeatedly links segmentation with increased opportunity for variation.

Segmented RNA genome and reassortment

  • A major consequence of segmentation is the ability to undergo reassortment during co-infection.
  • Genome segments can be exchanged among different viral strains.
  • Reassortment can generate progeny containing genes from more than one parent.
  • This can confer fitness advantages or disadvantages.
  • Reassortment requires genetic compatibility between parental strains, including conserved packaging signals and compatible RNA-protein interactions.

Influenza genome

  • Influenza viruses encode their genome in 8 genomic segments.
  • If a cell is infected by more than one virus, segment exchange can produce a new variant.
  • Reassortment involving human and avian or swine strains can produce viruses with pandemic potential.
  • Lecture examples: avian flu and swine flu.

Virion structure

  • Influenza virions are usually spherical, though the lecture image also emphasizes pleomorphism.
  • The virus is enveloped. The outer lipid membrane comes from the host cell.
  • Envelope spikes include:
    • HA — hemagglutinin
    • NA — neuraminidase
  • NA is a target of Tamiflu according to the lecture.
  • HA and NA determine:
    • Antigenic variation and host immunity.
    • Influenza subtype and strain, such as A/H1N1.
  • Protection against reinfection is mainly linked to antibodies to HA, though antibodies to NA are also protective.
  • The 7–8 RNA molecules are individually coated with capsid proteins, forming helical nucleocapsids.

Influenza A clades

  • Influenza A is organized into evolutionary lineages called clades.
  • Clades are characterized by genetic divergence and phylogenetic clustering.
  • The lecture links clade formation to continuous reassortment and mutation.
  • Clade tracking helps assess antigenic variation and select vaccine strains.
  • There are 8 genome segments. Changes in segments can alter the proteins or antigens produced.

Classification by internal proteins

  • Influenza A includes human and animal strains.
  • Influenza B contains human strains.
  • Influenza C contains human and swine strains.
  • Antigenic differences in NP nucleocapsid and M matrix proteins divide influenza into types A, B, and C.
  • These proteins do not cross-react among the three types.

Host-specific HA and NA

Birds: H1–H16, N1–N9.
Humans: H1N1, H3N2, H2N2.

Nomenclature

Type A viruses are subtyped using antigenic variation in surface glycoproteins HA and NA.

  • Standard isolate nomenclature includes:
    • Type
    • Host of origin
    • Geographic origin
    • Strain number
    • Year of isolation
  • HA and NA are placed in parentheses for type A.
  • Host origin is omitted for human isolates.
  • Example: A/Hong Kong/03/68(H3N2).
  • Host origin is included for nonhuman isolates.
  • Example: A/swine/Iowa/15/30(H1N1).
  • The lecture states that 18 HA and 11 NA subtypes have been recovered from humans and animals.

Hemagglutinin

  • HA binds influenza virions to susceptible cells.
  • It is the major antigen targeted by protective neutralizing antibodies.
  • HA variability is a major cause of continual emergence of new strains and epidemics.
  • Its name comes from its ability to agglutinate erythrocytes under certain conditions.
  • Cleavage into HA1 and HA2 is required for infectivity.
  • Cellular proteases mediate cleavage.
  • Influenza usually remains in the respiratory tract because the proteases that cleave HA are expressed at those sites.
  • The amino terminus of HA2 generated by cleavage is required for viral-envelope fusion with the cell membrane.

Neuraminidase

  • NA acts near the end of the replication cycle.
  • It is a sialidase that removes sialic acid from glycoconjugates.
  • Facilitates release of virions from infected-cell surfaces during budding.
  • Prevents virion self-aggregation by removing sialic acid residues from viral glycoproteins.
  • May help the virus move through respiratory mucus to reach target epithelium.

Influenza: Antigenic Drift and Antigenic Shift

Mechanisms of antigenic change, direct adaptation, reassortment, and re-emergence of old strains.

What changes? p. 18
Antigenic drift and shift affect the surface glycoproteins HA and NA.
The lecture states that internal proteins such as nucleoprotein do not undergo these antigenic changes.

Antigenic drift

Definition p. 18
Antigenic drift = gradual antigenic change caused by point mutations affecting major antigenic sites on viral glycoproteins.
  • Mutational changes accumulate over time.
  • Drift occurs as virus passes from person to person under varying antibody pressure.
  • Antibodies recognize antigenic regions of HA.
  • A mutant that changes a critical amino acid in an antibody-targeted region may escape neutralization.
  • That mutant can survive, multiply, and be transmitted to the next host, where selection continues.
  • Adults exposed to many influenza strains have broad antibody histories that can impose different selective pressure than immunologically naïve children.
  • The lecture defines immunologically naïve people as those without exposure to multiple strains or without prior influenza vaccination.

Antigenic shift

Definition p. 19
Antigenic shift = abrupt antigenic change associated with genetic reassortment with an unrelated strain.
  • Classic lecture example:
    • Avian influenza infects a pig.
    • Human influenza infects the same pig.
    • The pig acts as a mixing vessel.
    • A new mixed strain can emerge and may cause a pandemic.
  • Changes in HA and NA can occur independently.
Mechanism of major change discussedLecture explanation

Direct transmission and adaptation

  • Direct avian influenza A transmission, particularly highly pathogenic strains, can occur from birds to humans.
  • Virus can then adapt in humans.
  • The lecture describes mutation of HA receptor specificity from avian α2,3-linked sialic acid recognition toward human α2,6-linked sialic acid recognition.
  • A novel human strain may then be capable of sustained transmission and pandemic spread.
  • The 1918/1919 Spanish flu is given as an example thought to involve direct avian-to-human transmission and adaptation.

Genetic reassortment

  • A virus already adapted to another host, such as birds or humans, can co-infect a pig with a swine-adapted strain.
  • Reassortment can produce a new virus with HA and/or NA different from prior swine strains.
  • It occurs when two influenza A strains infect one cell and their RNA segments mix during progeny-virus assembly.
  • The result is a third strain containing genes from both parental strains.
  • The lecture notes that pigs and humans possess receptors for both human and avian influenza strains, allowing dual infection.
  • Examples attributed to reassortment:
    • Asian flu, 1957/1958
    • Hong Kong flu, 1968/1969
    • Pandemic H1N1, 2009–2010

Re-emergence of old strains

  • When population immunity to an older strain weakens, the strain can re-emerge.
  • The lecture cites Russian flu in 1950 and 1977 as probably involving re-emergence of the same H1N1 subtype.

Influenza: Life Cycle, Transmission, Pathogenesis & Disease

Nuclear replication, airway injury, T-cell responses, uncomplicated influenza, pneumonia, and Reye syndrome.

Life cycle

  1. Virus enters through receptor-mediated endocytosis.
  2. Viral ribonucleoprotein complexes are released into the cytoplasm.
  3. They are transported to the nucleus, where replication and transcription occur.
  4. Viral mRNAs are exported to the cytoplasm for translation.
  5. Early proteins required for replication and transcription, including NP and polymerase protein PB1, return to the nucleus.
  6. PB1 RNA polymerase synthesizes positive-sense RNA from genomic negative-sense RNA.
  7. These +ssRNA templates are copied to produce new genome segments.
  8. Some genome segments serve as templates for additional viral mRNA.
  9. Later, new segments become progeny genomes.
  10. HA and NA mRNAs are translated on ER-associated ribosomes and the proteins are delivered to the cell membrane.
  11. Genome segments are packaged while progeny virions bud from the host cell.
High-yield contrast p. 20
Unlike the paramyxoviruses emphasized earlier, influenza replication and transcription take place in the nucleus.

Transmission and epithelial injury

  • Influenza spreads person to person through airborne droplets or contact with contaminated hands or surfaces.
  • Infection destroys superficial respiratory mucosa but does not affect the basal epithelial layer.
  • Complete repair may take up to 1 month.
  • Viral damage lowers resistance to secondary bacterial pathogens.

Pathogenesis: T-cell recognition

  1. Respiratory epithelial cells and professional APCs process viral proteins.
  2. Viral peptides are presented on MHC-I.
  3. CD8+ T-cell receptors recognize peptide-MHC-I complexes.
  4. Activated CD8+ T cells release cytotoxic granules such as granzymes and perforin.
  5. They kill infected cells and produce proinflammatory cytokines, including IFNs, TNF, and IL-2.

Uncomplicated influenza

  • Symptoms usually start abruptly.
  • Chills
  • Headache
  • Dry cough
  • High fever lasting about 3–5 days
  • Generalized muscular aches
  • Malaise
  • Anorexia
  • Mild or asymptomatic infection may also occur.

Pneumonia

  • Serious complication, especially in elderly and debilitated patients with chronic disease.
  • May be:
    • Viral pneumonia
    • Secondary bacterial pneumonia
    • A combination of viral and bacterial pneumonia
  • Increased mucus can carry infectious agents deeper into the respiratory tract.
  • Predisposing mechanisms stated in the lecture:
    • Loss of ciliary clearance
    • Dysfunction of phagocytic cells
    • Alveolar exudate acting as a growth medium for bacteria
  • Common secondary bacteria listed:
    • Staphylococcus aureus
    • Streptococcus pneumoniae
    • Haemophilus influenzae

Reye syndrome

  • Acute encephalopathy of children and adolescents, usually 2–16 years.
  • Rare, but can affect all organs.
  • Most harmful to brain and liver.
  • Associated findings highlighted:
    • Acute increase in intracranial pressure
    • Massive accumulation of fat in liver and other organs

Influenza: Immunity, Aging, Diagnosis & Prevention

Innate and humoral responses, original antigenic sin, aging-related immune changes, testing, and vaccine regimens stated in the lecture.

Innate immune response

  • Important cells:
    • Macrophages
    • Natural killer cells
    • Dendritic cells
  • The initial phase activates alveolar macrophages and monocytes.
  • Alveolar macrophages can limit viral spread through phagocyte-mediated opsonophagocytosis.
  • NK cells are cytotoxic lymphocytes that contribute to elimination of influenza virus.

Humoral immune response

  • Antibodies are produced against several influenza antigens.
  • HA-specific antibody is most important for neutralization.
  • Binding to the globular head of HA inhibits viral attachment to host cells.
  • HA-specific antibodies can also facilitate phagocytosis of virus particles through Fc receptor-expressing cells.
  • Mucosal tissues are a major portal of entry.
  • IgA and, to a lesser extent, IgM can neutralize mucosal pathogens and help prevent entry and replication.

Immunity

  • The three influenza types are antigenically unrelated and therefore provide no cross-protection.
  • After antigenic drift, pre-existing antibody may make infection with the new strain milder rather than fully prevent it.
  • Original antigenic sin: later infections or immunizations reinforce the antibody response to the first influenza subtype encountered years earlier.
  • Cell-mediated immunity mainly helps clear established infection.
  • Cytotoxic T cells lyse infected cells.
  • The cytotoxic T-cell response is cross-reactive and can lyse cells infected with different influenza subtypes.
  • Targets include internal proteins such as NP and M as well as surface glycoproteins.

Rubella

  • IgG is the only test for immunity.
  • Rash coincides with antibody appearance.
  • IgM does not persist beyond about 6 weeks.
  • IgG persists for life.
  • Congenital damage is worse with earlier pregnancy infection.
  • After week 20, birth defects are uncommon.
  • Classic congenital triad: heart disease + ocular defects + hearing loss.
  • Infant IgM supports congenital infection because IgM does not cross placenta.

Coronavirus / SARS-CoV-2

  • Largest RNA-virus genome.
  • S protein = receptor binding and entry.
  • N = RNA-bound nucleocapsid.
  • M = most abundant structural protein and central assembly organizer.
  • SARS-CoV-1/2 receptor = ACE2.
  • MERS receptor = DPP4 / CD26.
  • S, E, M + nucleocapsid assemble in ERGIC.
  • Severe disease: cytokine storm, lymphopenia, ARDS, coagulopathy, shock, MOF.

Paramyxovirus core

  • Fusion protein → syncytia / giant cells.
  • Attachment proteins = HN, H, or G.
  • F0 cleavage is essential for fusion and infection.
  • Replicates in cytoplasm.
  • Nonsegmented → no segment reassortment.
  • Does not undergo antigenic drift per lecture.
  • HN neuraminidase activity in parainfluenza + mumps.
  • Inclusion bodies = sites of viral synthesis.

Parainfluenza

  • Type 1/2 → croup, 6–18 months.
  • Type 3 → lower airway, pneumonia / bronchiolitis, <6 months.
  • Type 4 → usually mild, otitis media.
  • Type 3 most prevalent.
  • Preferred diagnosis = nucleic-acid amplification.
  • Paired sera: 4-fold rise.

Mumps

  • Humans only.
  • Parotitis not obligatory.
  • Incubation 14–18 days.
  • Salivary shedding 3 days before to 9 days after swelling.
  • CNS involvement 10–30%.
  • Postpubertal orchitis can be painful because the tunica albuginea is tight.
  • One antigenic type → permanent immunity.
  • IgM = recent infection.

Measles

  • Measles = rubeola. Rubella = German measles.
  • Humans only.
  • Rash around day 14 when circulating antibodies are detectable.
  • Koplik spots = pathognomonic.
  • Most common complication = otitis media.
  • Most common life-threatening complication = pneumonia.
  • SSPE = rare, years later, measles antigen in brain-cell inclusions.
  • Infection confers lifelong immunity.
  • Measles causes immune suppression.

Nipah and Hendra

  • Nipah reservoir = Pteropus fruit bats.
  • Nipah classic chain: bat → pig → human.
  • Raw date-palm sap / contaminated fruit = Nipah clue.
  • Nipah symptoms start 4–14 days after infection and may progress to encephalitis.
  • Hendra reservoir = Australian flying foxes.
  • Hendra key chain: bat → horse → human.
  • Hendra symptoms 9–16 days after infected-horse contact.

RSV and hMPV

  • RSV → bronchiolitis in very young infants.
  • RSV pathogenesis: sloughed ciliated cells + mucus + neutrophil inflammation → airway obstruction.
  • RSV giant cells and intracytoplasmic inclusions.
  • Maternal antibody falls after 2–4 months, when severe disease can occur.
  • No IgA-producing memory B cells in RSV.
  • hMPV symptoms resemble RSV.
  • hMPV peak = 6–12 months.
  • RSV peak = <6 months.

Influenza drift vs shift

  • Drift: gradual, point mutations, immune selection over transmission chains.

  • Reassortment examples: 1957/58 Asian flu, 1968/69 Hong Kong flu, 2009 H1N1 pandemic.

Influenza disease and diagnosis

  • Reye syndrome: children/adolescents, brain and liver injury.
  • RT-PCR = highly sensitive and specific.
  • Paired sera needed for serology because baseline influenza antibodies are common.
  • Original antigenic sin: later exposures reinforce antibody response to first subtype experienced.
