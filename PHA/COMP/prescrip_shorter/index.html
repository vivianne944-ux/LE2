① Prescription Writing + SGD Cases

≈ 5 exam items (items 101–105 in past papers) · built from SGD Case 2, SGD Case 3 (both files), past papers, and trans 2.08

Big picture: every past Rx case is one antibiotic choice with a trap
  • Every year the case is an oral antibiotic for a UTI, pyelonephritis, or tonsillopharyngitis.
  • The organism is given (culture or Gram stain). The sensitivity list gives you many drugs.
  • The trap is always one patient clue that removes drugs: allergy, pregnancy, G6PD, arrhythmia, tendon tear, age.
  • Points are lost for a drug in a contraindicated group (minus 2 in 2028), or partial credit for a second-best group (minus 0.5 or minus 1 in 2027).
  • Co-amoxiclav got no credit in 2027 (not recommended by the National Antibiotic Guidelines for that case).
Patient clue → cross out drug groups → pick narrowest oral drug the organism is sensitive to → strength + form → dispense no. → label
Prescription format (copy this exactly)
Name: Maria S. Age/Sex: 24/F Address: Quezon City Date: 9/28/2026 ℞ Ciprofloxacin tablet 500 mg Dispense No. 14 Label: Take one tablet by mouth every 12 hours for 7 days. (Signature) Juan Dela Cruz, M.D. Clinic address License No. 000000 PTR No. 000000

Parts the answer keys always show

  • Superscription: patient name, age, sex, address, date
  • ℞ symbol
  • Inscription: generic name + dosage form + strength (e.g. "Cefixime tablet 400 mg")
  • Subscription: "Dispense No. __" = total units for the whole course
  • Signa / Label: how many, route, how often, how long
  • Prescriber: signature, name + M.D., clinic address, License No., PTR No.

Dispense math

  • Doses per day × days = Dispense No.
  • Every 12 hours × 7 days = 14 · every 12 hours × 10 days = 20
  • Once daily × 10 days = 10 · once daily × 3 days = 3
  • Every 6 hours × 2 days = 8 (paracetamol as needed)
  • Suspension: mg per dose ÷ mg per mL = mL per dose. Total mL ÷ bottle size, round up = number of bottles.
  • 250 mg / 5 mL = 50 mg per mL.

SGD Case 3 "Burning Pee": Maria S., 24/F, acute uncomplicated pyelonephritis

In one line: G6PD + immediate-type amoxicillin allergy + vomiting, so every obvious oral drug has a problem.

Case facts that decide the answer

  • Fever 39.1 °C, heart rate 108, right flank pain, positive right costovertebral angle tenderness
  • Vomiting, poor oral intake, second UTI this year
  • G6PD deficiency
  • Amoxicillin: generalized urticaria + shortness of breath = immediate (IgE) hypersensitivity
  • Normal creatinine 0.8 and BUN 14 (no renal dose change)
  • Not pregnant (last period 1 week ago), weight 54 kg

Guide-question answers

  • Organism: E. coli (80–90% of UTI). Others: Klebsiella pneumoniae, Proteus mirabilis (National Antibiotic Guidelines 2017)
  • Goals: cure infection, relieve pain/fever/vomiting and restore fluids, avoid drugs that cause G6PD hemolysis or allergy, prevent sepsis and kidney damage, prevent recurrence
  • Non-drug: oral rehydration and plenty of fluids (flush bacteria), tepid sponge bath and cooling pads (fever), wipe front to back, do not hold urine, warm compress on painful area

Route and disposition (your two files disagree)

Main SGD fileInitial IV antibiotic, inpatient or close observation. Reason: high fever, vomiting, poor intake, tachycardia → cannot absorb oral drug. Switch to oral when improving, stable, afebrile, and tolerating fluids. Adjust to culture results.
"(1)" fileOutpatient, start oral, re-evaluate at 48 hours. Admit if no improvement. Treat as complicated UTI if no response after 48 hours.

Admission criteria (Philippine Society for Microbiology and Infectious Diseases guideline, SGD)

  • Marked debility or signs of sepsis
  • Uncertain diagnosis
  • Concern about adherence
  • Cannot maintain oral hydration or take oral medication ← Maria's main criterion
  • Failed outpatient treatment after 48–72 hours
  • Other sources: pregnancy, major comorbidity or immunocompromise, urinary tract abnormality, complicated pyelonephritis (obstruction, abscess, acute kidney injury), poor social situation

SGD Case 2 "Clear and Fluffy": Makateo, 4/M, 15 kg, bullous impetigo

In one line: fluid-filled bullae + Gram-positive clusters = Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA), healthy afebrile child → narrow oral anti-staph drug.

Organism

  • Bullous impetigo → exfoliative toxin-producing S. aureus (toxin cleaves desmoglein-1 → roofed blisters). No honey crust.
  • Non-bullous → Group A Streptococcus (S. pyogenes) ± S. aureus → honey-colored crusts.
  • Gram stain is not routine. Test only for treatment failure, suspected community MRSA, outbreaks, severe cases.

Goals and non-drug measures

  • Eradicate S. aureus, heal lesions, stop new ones, prevent spread and complications, minimize adverse effects
  • Clean wounds and abrasions, good hygiene
  • Prevent scratching
  • Isolate patient's belongings (towels, clothes)

How to handle any changed case (use this every time)
1 Where is the infection? bladder only (cystitis) · kidney (pyelonephritis) · skin (impetigo) · throat 2 Who is the patient? age / weight · pregnant · kidney · liver · heart (QT) · tendon · G6PD 3 What is the allergy TYPE? mild or delayed rash → cephalosporin allowed hives + wheeze / anaphylaxis → NO β-lactams (penicillins or cephalosporins) 4 Can they take oral? vomiting, septic, pregnant pyelonephritis, infant → IV + admit 5 Cross out → pick the narrowest oral drug the organism is sensitive to → dose → Dispense No. → Label
The 6 swaps they love
  • Make her pregnant → quinolones out
  • Add G6PD → sulfa, nitrofurantoin out (quinolones risky)
  • Change the allergy (amoxicillin, sulfa, cephalosporin, quinolone) or its severity
  • Add arrhythmia / tendon tear / elderly → quinolones out
  • Make it a child → quinolones and tetracyclines out, dose by kg
  • Remove vomiting → outpatient oral. Add sepsis → admit IV
Suspension shortcut (250 mg/5 mL = 50 mg per mL)
  • At 50 mg/kg/day, total mL per day = weight in kg.
  • mL per dose = weight ÷ doses per day.
  • Total mL for the course = weight × days. Bottles = total ÷ bottle size, round up.
  • Example 15 kg: 15 mL/day ÷ 4 = 3.75 mL every 6 h. 15 × 7 = 105 mL → 2 bottles.

② 2.08 Antimicrobial Groups and Antimicrobial Resistance 1 & 2

Dr. Faye Karissa Lourdes M. Bautista · 36 pages · ≈ 10 questions on the exam

Big picture (start here)
  • Antibacterials are grouped by the bacterial part they hit: cell wall, cell membrane, 30S ribosome, 50S ribosome, folate pathway, DNA, RNA.
  • The target predicts whether the drug kills (bactericidal) or only stops growth (bacteriostatic).
  • The target also predicts the side effect. Human mitochondria have bacteria-like 70S ribosomes, so some ribosome drugs hurt human marrow.
  • Resistance follows 4 moves: destroy the drug, change the target, keep the drug out, pump the drug out.
  • Most exam cases are "which drug fits this patient": check organism, site, kidney, liver, allergy, pregnancy, age, and drugs already taken.

⑥ Topic block: β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactam, carbapenems)

In one line: all bind PBPs and kill growing bacteria. Side chain changes decide spectrum, stability, and route.

Penicillins

How it works
Mimic D-Ala-D-Ala → covalent bond to PBP → failed cross-link → autolysis → osmotic lysis
Kinetics
Half-life under 1 h. Kidney: 10% filtration, 90% tubular secretion. Probenecid prolongs. Nafcillin biliary (no renal adjustment). Oxacillin renal + biliary
Uses
Pharyngitis, rheumatic fever, pneumococcus, syphilis, gonorrhea, diphtheria, tetanus, gas gangrene, anthrax. Prophylaxis: endocarditis, rheumatic fever
Adverse
Hypersensitivity (hapten from penicilloic acid), anaphylaxis 0.05%. Seizures at very high dose with kidney failure

Cephalosporins

How it works
Same as penicillin, different PBPs. Dihydrothiazine ring (penicillin has thiazolidine)
Rule
Later generations → more Gram-negative. Exceptions: ceftazidime and cefepime = Pseudomonas, ceftaroline = MRSA, ceftriaxone ≠ Pseudomonas
Allergy
Cross-allergy 5–10%. 📌 Contraindicated after severe immediate penicillin reaction
Toxicity
Ceftriaxone: kernicterus in neonates, biliary pseudolithiasis. Cefotetan/cefamandole MTT: bleeding + disulfiram. Cefepime neurotoxicity in kidney failure

Aztreonam (monobactam)

Spectrum
Aerobic Gram-negative only (Neisseria, Pseudomonas). No Gram-positive, no anaerobes
Key
Unfused ring → antibodies do not recognize it → safe in severe penicillin allergy. Exception: ceftazidime allergy (identical side chain)

Carbapenems

Key
Broadest β-lactams. Sulfur replaced by carbon. Thienamycin first (1976)
Imipenem
Broken down by renal dehydropeptidase-1 → give with cilastatin. Seizures (GABA competition)
Meropenem, doripenem
Stable to dehydropeptidase-1, no cilastatin. Meropenem for CNS use
Ertapenem
Once daily, long-acting. Lacks Pseudomonas, Acinetobacter, Enterococcus
All
Renal clearance → reduce dose in kidney failure

In one line: vancomycin binds the substrate (D-Ala-D-Ala), not the enzyme, so bacteria resist by changing the substrate.

Vancomycin

How it works
5 hydrogen bonds to D-Ala-D-Ala → blocks transglycosylase + transpeptidase. Late, outside the membrane. MW ~1500 → no Gram-negative entry
Uses
IV: MRSA sepsis, endocarditis, bone, pneumonia. Oral: C. difficile only
Kinetics
Half-life 6 h. 90% glomerular filtration → accumulates in kidney failure. Trough 15–20 mcg/mL (checked every 4th dose)
Trough rule
Below target → give again. Too high → skip, give next scheduled dose
Adverse
Red man syndrome, dose-related AKI (common), ototoxicity (rare, irreversible)
Resistance
VRE/VRSA: D-Ala-D-Lac (or D-Ser) → loses 1 hydrogen bond → 1000× weaker binding. VISA: thick wall traps drug

Active vs VRSA, VRE

Bacitracin

③ 2.09 Antifungal Drugs

Dr. Carolynn Pia Jerez-Bagain · 15 pages · ≈ 8 questions on the exam

Big picture (start here)
  • Fungi have ergosterol in their membrane (humans have cholesterol) and a β-glucan cell wall. Most antifungals hit one of these.
  • Drugs are grouped by use: systemic drugs for systemic infection, systemic drugs for skin/nail infection, and topical drugs.
  • Fungi by form: yeasts (Candida, Cryptococcus, budding), moulds (Aspergillus, Mucor, dermatophytes, hyphae), dimorphic (mould in environment, yeast in tissue).
  • By site: superficial/cutaneous (topical often enough), subcutaneous, systemic (life-threatening, IV).
  • Not every fungal infection needs an oral drug. A small ring lesion in a healthy person → topical.

Class
Broad-spectrum polyene macrolide. Fungicidal at high levels, static at low

Use
Nearly all life-threatening mycoses. Induction, then azole for maintenance
Monitor
Creatinine, potassium, magnesium, CBC

Low membrane ergosterol or altered sterol

How it works
Enters via cytosine permease → converted to 5-FU → blocks thymidylate synthase (DNA) + fluorinated RNA (protein)

Cryptococcus, some Candida, chromoblastomycosis moulds
Rule
Never alone (resistance develops fast). Unavailable in low- and middle-income countries

Marrow depression, hepatotoxicity, toxic enterocolitis, GI upset

④ 2.10 Antimalarials

Dr. Rey Christian Cabreros · 17 pages (pages 1–9) · ≈ 8 questions on the exam

Big picture (start here)
  • Pick an antimalarial by the 3 P's: Plasmodium species, Patient status (uncomplicated vs severe, pregnant, child), Pharmacology (chloroquine-sensitive or resistant area).
  • Each drug kills the parasite at a specific life-cycle stage. That stage decides the use: prevention, relapse cure, symptom cure, or blocking spread.
  • Only the red blood cell (asexual erythrocytic) stage causes clinical malaria (fever from red cell rupture). Most drugs act here.
  • P. vivax and P. ovale hide as dormant liver forms (hypnozoites) → relapse months to years later → only primaquine clears them.
  • Artemisinins are the gold standard, always given as a combination (ACT).
