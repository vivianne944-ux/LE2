I. Introduction: Definitions, MIC/MBC, Risks, and Resistance Overview Must knowp. 1
Core idea. Drugs are chemicals which when administered to living organisms produce a biological effect.
Antibiotic comes from “antibiosis” (against life): compounds produced by one microorganism that selectively kill or inhibit the growth of other microorganisms. p. 1

A. Definition of Antimicrobial Drugs and Antibiotics p. 1, Fig. 1

  • Bactericidal antibiotics: directly kill bacteria
  • Bacteriostatic antibiotics: primarily inhibit bacterial growth and replication, allowing the host immune system to clear the infection
  • Major biological targets of antibacterial drugs (five):
    • Cell wall
    • Bacterial proteins
    • Nucleic acids
    • Metabolic pathways
    • Cell membrane
    • 💬These are also targets of pharmaceutical companies, so they can control bacterial survival and/or multiplication
  • Different antibiotic classes act at different points with the same goal: interfering with processes essential for bacterial survival or multiplication

MIC vs MBC Must know p. 1

  • Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC): lowest concentration of antimicrobial that prevents visible bacterial growth
  • Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC): lowest concentration that actually kills the organism
  • MBC:MIC ratio relates the two
    • Relatively low ratio (low MBC) → suggests bactericidal activity
    • Much higher ratio → bacterial growth is inhibited without efficient killing
    • 💬MBC:MIC ratio is used to test the potency or killing properties of the antimicrobial drug
  • Tolerance: growth is suppressed but bacterial killing is relatively ineffective

Exam clue: a low MBC:MIC ratio points to a bactericidal drug, a high ratio points to inhibition without efficient killing. p. 1

Clinical risks of antibiotic therapy p. 1

  • Predictable adverse effects: renal or hepatic toxicity
  • Unpredictable adverse effects: immune-mediated hypersensitivity reactions and idiosyncratic reactions, which are specific to individual patients
  • Risk of superinfection
    • Broad spectrum antibiotic use can disrupt the normal microbiota and predispose patients to superinfection
    • Examples: Clostridioides difficile infection, fungal overgrowth, and fungal candidiasis

Four mechanisms of bacterial resistance (overview) Must know p. 1

  • Drug inactivation: bacterial enzymes destroy or modify the antibiotic
  • Target modification: the structure the antibiotic normally binds to is altered by the bacterial enzyme
    • 💬Target is modified or structurally changed, resisting its action
  • Active efflux pumps: the bacteria pumps the drug out of the cell
  • Reduced permeability: drug is prevented from entering the bacterial cell effectively
    • 💬The cell has a thin membrane acting like a net, which prevents the drug from entering
    • 💬The bacteria has mutated forming a thicker layer, so the antibiotic can no longer penetrate the area
  • Detailed examples per mechanism are in VI. Antimicrobial Resistance
What successful therapy requires (the lecture's framework) p. 1
Successful antibiotic therapy requires more than simply choosing a drug that is active against a microorganism. We need to understand:
  • How the drug acts and whether it inhibits or kills the organism
  • What concentration is required
  • What toxicities may occur
  • How resistance limits the treatment
From the answer key Post-antibiotic effect and hospital organisms p. 6, Answer Key
  • Post-antibiotic effect: the persistent suppression of bacterial growth after a brief exposure (1–2 hrs) of bacteria to an antibiotic, even in the absence of host defense mechanisms. It refers to the period after complete removal of the antibiotic during which there is no growth of the target organism.
  • Hospital (nosocomial) organisms frequently have greater antimicrobial resistance, making treatment more complicated. Treatment of nosocomial infections may be difficult and challenging.

Review: Gram-positive vs Gram-negative envelope Should know p. 2–3, Fig. 5
Gram-positive envelope
  • Peptidoglycan density: surrounded by a thick, highly cross-linked peptidoglycan (murein) mesh that provides shape and shields from massive internal osmotic pressures
  • 💬The cytoplasmic membrane is surrounded by a tough, rigid mesh called a cell wall, which gives the cell its shape and shields from mechanical and osmotic stresses
  • Chemistry: higher peptidoglycan content, lower lipid concentration. Retains crystal violet dye during solvent treatment
  • Key pathogens: Streptococcus species, Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus anthracis, Clostridium botulinum
Gram-negative envelope
  • Outer membrane (OM): an additional chemical barrier, preventing the entry of many large or hydrophobic substances
  • Porin channels: specialized transmembrane porin proteins in the OM permit entry of small hydrophilic molecules, including drugs, into the periplasmic space
  • 💬Gram (−) bacteria have a thinner membrane and are surrounded by another protective layer, the OM, which prevents many substances from entering
  • Periplasmic membrane: space between the outer membrane and cytoplasmic membrane, well-defined
  • Key pathogens: Klebsiella pneumoniae, Shigella, Yersinia pestis, Salmonella species

III. Bacterial Cell Wall Synthesis and Its Inhibitors Must knowp. 3–4
Core idea. Bacteria must constantly build and remodel peptidoglycan to stay alive, using Penicillin Binding Proteins (PBPs).
Beta-lactams bind PBPs → no cross-linking → weak cell wall → cell lysis and bacterial death. p. 3

A. Peptidoglycan synthesis and cross-linking by PBPs p. 3, Fig. 6 (full size on p. 7)

  • Peptidoglycan (or murein in older sources): the strong mesh-like layer that surrounds the cell wall
  • Made of long sugar polymers
    • Backbone: alternating sugars linked by B-1,4 glycosidic bonds between N-acetylglucosamine (NAG) and N-acetylmuramic acid (NAM)
  • Attached to each NAM is a short peptide chain (pentapeptide)
    • Fig. 6 panel 3 lists the side chain: L-Ala, D-Glu, meso-DAP (or L-Lys), D-Ala, D-Ala
  • PBPs have two key activities:
    • Transglycosylase: adds disaccharide-pentapeptide units (NAG-NAM with peptide tail) to the end of the existing glycan strand to extend the polymer
    • Transpeptidase: catalyzes cross-linking between peptide chains of adjacent strands
  • PBPs coordinate both extension of glycan strands and cross-linking of peptide chains, continuously remodeling the cell wall as the bacterium grows and divides

High-yield summary Section III
  • Peptidoglycan = NAG–NAM backbone (B-1,4 bonds), pentapeptide on NAM.
  • PBPs: transglycosylase (extend strand) + transpeptidase (cross-link). Transpeptidase removes terminal D-Ala then links to glycine or meso-DAP/L-lys.
  • Beta-lactam → PBP → no cross-linking → weak wall → lysis.
  • Beta-lactam ring = structural analog of D-Ala-D-Ala.
  • Vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Ala terminus, sterically blocks assembly.
  • Polymyxins bind LPS lipid of Gram (−) OM, disrupt membrane.
  • Early (cytoplasm): Fosfomycin (MurA), Cycloserine. Late (periplasm): beta-lactams, Vancomycin, Bacitracin.
  • Beta-lactamase (penicillinase) breaks the ring. Inhibitors: Clavulanic acid, Sulbactam, Tazobactam. Augmentin, Zosyn, Sulperazone.
IV. Bacterial Cellular Processes Inhibitors: Ribosome, Nucleic Acid, Metabolism Must knowp. 4–5

A. Ribosome as bacterial target Must know p. 4

Bacterial ribosome: 70S
  • Made of 2 subunits: small 30S, large 50S
  • Excellent antimicrobial targets due to structural differences from human cytoplasmic ribosomes
  • These differences make them a primary target for many antibiotics
Human ribosome: 80S
  • Also 2 subunits: small 40S, large 60S
  • Distinct structure helps antibiotics target bacteria specifically, minimizing harm to human cells
Exam trap S is not additive p. 4
Sedimentation coefficient (S) is not an additive quantity (e.g. 30 + 50 ≠ 70). They are measurements of the size. Same for human: 40 + 60 ≠ 80.

Drug card Tetracyclines (Doxycycline, Tetracycline)
Class
30S protein synthesis inhibitor. "Reversible and Static." Bacteriostatic (Table 1). Broad-spectrum example (p. 2)
Mechanism of action
  • Subunit docking: bind reversibly to highly conserved sequences of 16S rRNA on 30S subunit
  • tRNA exclusion: sterically block aminoacyl-tRNA from accessing or binding to the A-site on the mRNA-ribosome complex
  • Bacteriostatic arrest: without the incoming amino acid loaded tRNA, protein chain elongation is inhibited → bacterial growth suppression
  • 💬Suppress growth without killing
Pharmacokinetics
  • Absorbed in stomach and proximal small intestine, 75–100% bioavailability (p. 5)
  • Oral intake with divalent cations or food can reduce absorption by 50% (p. 5)
  • Variable Vd, distributes by lipophilicity. More lipophilic agents → greater distribution into fatty tissues. Moderately protein-bound (20–60%), concentrates in bile, bone, and teeth (p. 5, Fig. 9)
  • Hepatic and renal: glomerular filtration and reabsorption. Half-life 8–25 hours. No renal dosing change needed (Fig. 9)
Drug interactions
💬Don't take it with food, or wait until 1 hour after/before eating to ensure an empty stomach (why: food and divalent cations cut absorption by 50%)
Resistance

Human drivers of global resistance p. 5
  • Selective pressures heavily accelerated by over-prescription of antibiotics
  • Massive use of antibiotics in animal husbandry and spread of multidrug resistant strains in hospitals due to unhygienic practices
  • Unnecessary antibiotic exposure allows organisms to "study" these drugs and how to resist them → they proliferate more
  • Unnecessary prolonged courses, inadequate dosing, and prescribing a broad spectrum antibiotic even if not needed promote resistance
  • Broad spectrum antibiotics should be used judiciously (e.g. hard-to-treat disease entities or septic shock), but always reassess after 2–3 days using culture and susceptibility testing
Clinical interventions p. 5
  • Restricting antibiotic use: preserve specialized, broad-spectrum agents (e.g. carbapenems or linezolid) exclusively for severe, documented infections
    • 💬Infection prevention is equally important aside from just prescribing
  • Constant exposure controls: exposing bacterial colonies to sustained, high concentrations is crucial to completely eradicate pathogens and prevent mutant survival
    • Antibiotic stewardship: you don't just want to prevent the re-emergence of the organisms, you also want to prevent the transmission
Strategic combination measures p. 5
  • Multiple agent regimens: combination therapies with different mechanisms of action is powerful to halt resistance (connective: co-trimoxazole's sequential block, p. 4–5)
  • Strategic preservation: hygienic lifestyle and sterile hospital environments restrict survival and transmission of dangerous, hospital-selected strains
Exam trap Match the drug to its resistance mechanism p. 5
  • Beta-lactams: beta-lactamase (enzyme), altered PBPs (target), porin loss (permeability)
  • Aminoglycosides: AGEs (enzyme), ribosomal mutation (target), diminished uptake pumps (permeability)
  • Tetracyclines: ribosomal mutation (target), efflux
  • Chloramphenicol: CAT (enzyme), efflux
  • Quinolones: mutated DNA gyrase (target), efflux
High-yield summary Section VI
  • 4 mechanisms: target modification, permeability, inactivating enzymes, efflux.
  • Enzymes: beta-lactamases, AGEs, CAT. Efflux: tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, quinolones.
  • Porin loss blocks hydrophilic beta-lactams.
  • Drivers: over-prescription, animal husbandry, hospital spread, prolonged courses, inadequate dosing, unnecessary broad spectrum.
  • Reserve carbapenems, linezolid for severe documented infections. Reassess broad therapy after 2–3 days.
  • Stewardship = prevent re-emergence and transmission. Combine different MOAs.
VII. Lecture Summary p. 5
  • Chemotherapy refers to selective use of drugs to eradicate pathogenic microorganisms
  • Antimicrobials include synthetic chemotherapeutic agents and antibiotics derived from microorganisms
  • Antibiotics are classified based on target microorganism, spectrum (narrow, extended, broad), pharmacological effect (bactericidal vs bacteriostatic), and mechanism of action
  • Major mechanisms: inhibition of cell wall synthesis, protein synthesis, nucleic acid synthesis, and folate metabolism

Commonly Confused Concepts
Bactericidal vs Bacteriostatic Confusion
Cidal: kills, no host defense needed, needs proliferating bacteria.
Static: arrests growth, host immunity clears.
Fast: kill vs stop. Don't combine.
MIC vs MBC Confusion
MIC: prevents visible growth. MBC: kills.
Fast: low MBC:MIC = cidal, high = static-like.
Tolerance vs Resistance Confusion
Tolerance: growth suppressed, killing ineffective.
Resistance: the 4 mechanisms that evade the drug.
Fast: tolerance = won't die, resistance = won't be hit.
Narrow vs Extended vs Broad Confusion
Narrow: single/limited group (Isoniazid). Extended: Gram (+) plus significant Gram (−) (Ampicillin). Broad: wide variety (Tetracycline).
Fast: coverage widens Isoniazid → Ampicillin → Tetracycline (study aid).
Antimicrobial vs Antibiotic Confusion
Antimicrobial: any natural, semisynthetic, or synthetic substance vs microorganisms (all microbes).
Antibiotic: the antibacterial drug, from antibiosis.
Fast: antimicrobial is the umbrella.
Transglycosylase vs Transpeptidase Confusion
Transglycosylase: extends glycan strands (NAG-NAM units).
Transpeptidase: cross-links peptide chains, removes terminal D-Ala.
Fast: glycos = sugar chain, peptid = cross-link. Beta-lactams hit transpeptidation.
Beta-lactam vs Vancomycin target Confusion
Beta-lactam: PBP enzyme (ring mimics D-Ala-D-Ala).
Vancomycin: D-Ala-D-Ala terminus itself.
Fast: enzyme vs substrate.
Cycloserine vs Vancomycin (both "D-Ala-D-Ala") Confusion
Cycloserine: D-Ala-D-Ala step in synthesis, early, cytoplasm.
Vancomycin: D-Ala-D-Ala terminus, late, assembly.
Fast: early step vs late terminus.
Early vs Late stage cell wall inhibitors Confusion
Early: cytoplasm, precursor synthesis (Fosfomycin/MurA, Cycloserine).
Late: periplasm, assembly and cross-linking (beta-lactams, Vancomycin, Bacitracin).
Fast: inside = early.
Beta-lactamase vs Beta-lactamase inhibitor Confusion
Beta-lactamase (penicillinase): bacterial enzyme that destroys the ring.
Inhibitor (Clavulanic acid, Sulbactam, Tazobactam): protects the partner drug.
Fast: enzyme = resistance, inhibitor = rescue.
Aminoglycosides vs Tetracyclines (both 30S, 16S rRNA) Confusion
AG: irreversible, misreading, O2-dependent, cidal, parenteral.
TC: reversible, A-site block, static, oral.
Fast: "Cationic and Lethal" vs "Reversible and Static".
Macrolides vs Lincosamides vs Chloramphenicol vs Oxazolidinones (50S) Confusion
Macrolides/Lincosamides: translocation, overlapping site. Chloramphenicol: peptidyl transferase. Oxazolidinones: 70S initiation.
Fast: move / bond / start.
Fluoroquinolones vs Rifampin Confusion
FQ: DNA gyrase / topo IV (DNA). Rifampin: RNA polymerase beta-subunit (RNA).
Fast: gyrase = DNA, polymerase = RNA.
Gyrase vs Topoisomerase IV (FQ targets) Confusion
Gyrase (topo II) A subunit: Gram (−), strand cutting/resealing. Topo IV: Gram (+), separating daughter chromosomes.
Fast: negative = gyrase.
Sulfonamides vs Trimethoprim Confusion
Sulfonamide: competes with PABA at dihydropteroate synthase (first).
Trimethoprim: dihydrofolate reductase (later).
Fast: synthase then reductase, together = co-trimoxazole.
Ceftriaxone / Cefoperazone vs other beta-lactams Confusion
Most beta-lactams: renal, unchanged. These two: biliary.
Fast: the two "cef" biliary exceptions.
Efflux vs Reduced permeability Confusion
Efflux: drug enters then is pumped out (tetracyclines, chloramphenicol, quinolones).
Permeability: drug never gets in (porin loss vs beta-lactams, reduced uptake vs aminoglycosides).
Fast: out vs never in.
Post-antibiotic effect vs Tolerance Confusion
PAE: growth suppressed after the drug is removed (brief 1–2 h exposure).
Tolerance: drug present, growth suppressed, but killing ineffective.
Fast: PAE is a good thing, tolerance is not.

Key callouts
  • Beta-lactam ring = structural analog of D-alanyl-D-alanine
  • Ceftriaxone, Cefoperazone = biliary exceptions among renally cleared beta-lactams
  • Chloramphenicol: static vs Gram (−) rods, cidal vs Gram (−) cocci (S. pneumoniae)

Traps
  • S values are not additive (30 + 50 ≠ 70)
  • Never combine cidal + static for the same species
  • Folate antagonists do not directly target wall, RNA, or DNA
