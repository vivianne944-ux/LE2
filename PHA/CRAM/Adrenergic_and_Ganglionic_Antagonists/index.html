If You Only Remember These

Pages 1–5
Adrenergic neuron blockers do not directly block postsynaptic adrenergic receptors. They interfere with norepinephrine release, storage, uptake, or synthesis.
Methyldopa is the lecture’s drug of choice for hypertension in pregnancy.
Reserpine irreversibly inhibits VMAT-2 and depletes vesicular monoamines. Recovery requires synthesis of new storage vesicles.
Reserpine can cause severe depression. The PDF says avoid it in patients with a history of depression and discontinue if depression occurs.
Guanethidine requires neuronal uptake for its action. TCAs and cocaine block that uptake and can reduce its antihypertensive effect.
Guanethidine is polar and does not cross the BBB. This is why the lecture contrasts it with sedating CNS-penetrant drugs.
Rapid IV guanethidine gives a triple response → initial depressor → pressor → late progressive depressor. The lecture says this is not seen with oral administration.
Adrenergic blockade produces parasympathetic predominance, not direct cholinergic stimulation.
Peripheral edema after adrenergic blockade is linked in the lecture to BP reduction → JG stimulation → RAAS activation → sodium and water retention.
Methyldopa toxicity clues → sedation, lactation, positive Coombs autoimmune hemolytic anemia, drug-induced hepatitis, and drug fever.
Metyrosine inhibits the rate-limiting step in catecholamine synthesis. It is used as an adjunct in symptomatic inoperable or metastatic pheochromocytoma.
Bretylium initially releases NE, then inhibits NE release. It is presented as a Class III antiarrhythmic that blocks potassium channels.
Ganglionic blockers act at nicotinic receptors in both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia. Their effects depend on the dominant autonomic tone of each organ.
Nicotine is dose dependent. Low dose stimulates ganglia, high dose produces ganglionic blockade after persistent depolarization.
Trimethaphan is the lecture’s ganglionic blocker for acute hypertensive emergencies and controlled hypotension during surgery.
Mecamylamine crosses the BBB and blocks central nicotinic receptors. The lecture connects this to CNS effects and smoking-cessation use with transdermal nicotine.

Adrenergic Antagonists

Pages 1–3
1. Core Concept, Sites of Action, and ClassificationPage 1
MUST KNOWAdrenergic antagonists inhibit activity at adrenergic synapses without directly blocking postsynaptic adrenergic receptors.
Core idea
  • They may interfere with NE release.
  • They may alter uptake of neurotransmitter into the adrenergic nerve.
  • The lecture emphasizes that these agents are now rarely used because newer agents are more effective and have fewer adverse effects.

Affect NE release from nerve terminals

  • Block NE release → Guanethidine, Bethanidine, Guanadrel, Bretylium, Debrisoquine
  • Deplete catecholamine stores → Reserpine, Guanethidine

Affect NE synthesis or nerve integrity

  • False neurotransmitters → Methyldopa, Guanethidine, Pargyline
  • Inhibit catecholamine synthesis → Methyltyrosine or metyrosine
  • Destroy adrenergic nerve fibers → 6-hydroxydopamine
Lecture emphasis: Guanethidine appears in more than one mechanism category. The PDF specifically tells students to study what its actions share with other agents and what differentiates them.
Recall check: What key feature separates adrenergic neuron blockers from postsynaptic adrenergic blockers?
They do not directly block postsynaptic adrenergic receptors. They act on the adrenergic neuron by changing release, storage, uptake, or synthesis.
Recall check: Which two agents are listed under depletion of catecholamine stores?
Reserpine and Guanethidine.
2. Shared Uses and Adverse EffectsPage 1

Therapeutic uses in the lecture

  • Mild hypertension → Reserpine, Methyldopa
  • Pregnancy hypertension → Methyldopa, lecture’s drug of choice
  • Severe hypertension → Guanethidine, now largely superseded
  • Anti-arrhythmia → Bretylium was used, safer drugs are preferred

Shared adverse-effect pattern

  • Orthostatic hypotension, greatest with guanethidine and congeners
  • Reflex tachycardia, greater with guanethidine than reserpine or methyldopa
  • Peripheral edema through RAAS activation after BP falls
  • Parasympathetic predominance → GI symptoms, bradycardia, congestion, miosis
  • Sexual dysfunction → delayed or retrograde ejaculation

3. ReserpinePage 2
MUST KNOWPlant alkaloid from Rauwolfia serpentina that irreversibly inhibits VMAT-2 and depletes biogenic amines.
MECHANISM
Reserpine irreversibly binds storage vesicles and inhibits ATP-Mg²⁺ dependent VMAT-2
↓
NE and other amines cannot be transported into synaptic vesicles
↓
Catecholamines passively diffuse into cytoplasm
↓
Intraneuronal MAO metabolizes them
↓
Tissue NE falls → sympathetic transmission is blocked
↓
Pharmacological sympathectomy

Pharmacologic effects

  • Slow onset, long duration, effects may persist for days after stopping
  • Depletes NE, dopamine, and 5-hydroxytryptamine in the CNS
  • Low-dose long-term treatment decreases PVR, HR, CO, and BP
  • BP reduction may activate RAAS and cause salt and water retention

Uses and toxicity

  • Low-dose treatment of mild hypertension, often with diuretics, especially in the elderly
  • CNS → sedation, depression, extrapyramidal or Parkinsonian symptoms
  • GIT → mild diarrhea, abdominal cramps, increased gastric acid secretion
  • Low-dose effects include nasal stuffiness and impaired concentration
EXAM TRAPMost serious adverse effect in the lecture: occasional psychotic depression that can lead to suicide. The PDF says discontinue the drug if depression occurs and avoid it in patients with a history of depression. It also says avoid in patients with a history of peptic ulcer because the drug may exacerbate it.
SHOULD KNOWRecovery from reserpine requires synthesis of new storage vesicles, which takes days to weeks after discontinuation.
Recall check: Why can reserpine’s effects persist after the drug is stopped?
It irreversibly inhibits vesicular storage, so sympathetic function recovers only when new storage vesicles are synthesized.
Recall check: Which enzyme degrades the catecholamines that leak into the cytoplasm?
Intraneuronal monoamine oxidase, MAO.

  • Half-life about 5 days

Adverse effects

  • Diarrhea and nasal congestion
  • Delayed or retrograde ejaculation
  • Marked reflex tachycardia

INTERACTION TRAPTCAs or cocaine block neuronal uptake of NE and guanethidine. Because uptake is necessary for guanethidine’s hypotensive action, the lecture says the antihypertensive effect falls and severe hypertension can occur.
EXAM TRAPOTC cold preparations containing sympathomimetics such as phenylephrine can cause hypertension in a patient taking guanethidine. The PDF connects this to receptor sensitivity after pharmacologic sympathectomy.
PHEOCHROMOCYTOMAGuanethidine can provoke a hypertensive crisis in pheochromocytoma by releasing catecholamines.
Dosing precaution: do not increase the dose at intervals shorter than 2 weeks because of accumulation and toxicity.
Recall check: What property explains the lack of guanethidine sedation?
It is a polar drug and does not cross the BBB.
Recall check: What is required for guanethidine’s hypotensive action?
Neuronal uptake into the adrenergic nerve terminal.

7. Core Concept and ClassificationPages 3–4
MUST KNOWGanglionic blockers target nicotinic receptors in both sympathetic and parasympathetic autonomic ganglia. They are nonselective between the two divisions and are not effective as neuromuscular antagonists.
  • They can block the entire autonomic output at the nicotinic receptor.
  • Responses are complex and unpredictable because each organ has a different dominant autonomic tone.
  • The lecture treats these drugs mainly for academic, historical, and experimental pharmacology because severe toxicities made them clinically unattractive.
ClassificationExamples listed in the PDF
Persistent depolarizing blockersNicotine prototype, Lobeline, Coniine, Tetramethylammonium, Dimethylphenylpiperazine, Decamethonium
Non-depolarizing or competitive blockersQuaternary ammonium: Pentolinium, Chlorisondamine, Tetraethylammonium. Triethylsulfonium: Trimethaphan. Secondary amine: Pempidine
Noncompetitive blockersQuaternary ammonium: Hexamethonium. Secondary amine: Mecamylamine
Source consistency alert: Page 4 contains a general statement that, except for nicotine, the other drugs in the category are nondepolarizing competitive antagonists. The same page separately classifies hexamethonium and mecamylamine as noncompetitive blockers. This reviewer preserves the explicit classification table and flags the inconsistency rather than silently resolving it.
Recall check: Why are ganglionic blocker effects hard to predict?
They block both sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, so the result depends on the dominant autonomic tone in each organ.
Recall check: Which drug is the prototype persistent depolarizing blocker?
Nicotine.
8. Therapeutic Uses and PharmacokineticsPage 4

Therapeutic uses in the PDF

  • Trimethaphan → acute hypertensive emergencies
  • Controlled hypotension during surgery → minimize bleeding and blood loss, facilitate microsurgery
  • Mecamylamine → the PDF states orphan-drug use in Tourette’s syndrome after usual agents fail
  • Mecamylamine + transdermal nicotine → adjunct to smoking cessation by blocking central nicotinic receptors

Pharmacokinetics

  • Quaternary ammonium compounds → incomplete, unpredictable GIT absorption, mostly urinary excretion unchanged
  • Trimethaphan → polar, rapid onset, brief duration, renal excretion with 30% unchanged
  • Mecamylamine → secondary amine, almost completely absorbed, crosses BBB, enters CNS, concentrates in liver and kidneys
Mecamylamine PK values stated on Page 4: fast onset of 37 minutes, duration of action 22 hours, slow renal excretion.
Source consistency alert: Page 4 states a mecamylamine duration of action of 22 hours, while Page 5 later states the duration is about 10 hours after a single administration. The PDF does not reconcile these values.
Therapeutic-use caveat: the lecture repeatedly stresses that ganglionic blockers are rarely used because more selective, better tolerated agents have replaced them.

MUST KNOWGanglionic-blocker effects depend on the dominant autonomic tone in a given organ. Blocking that dominant input allows the opposite influence or loss of tone to become apparent.

Sympathoplegia

  • Marked postural hypotension
  • Sexual dysfunction, potentially affecting erection and ejaculation
  • Loss of arteriolar and venomotor tone → decreased PVR and venous return
  • Venous pooling from loss of postural vasoconstrictor reflexes

Parasympathoplegia and related effects

  • Urinary hesitancy and retention, especially in men with BPH
  • Marked constipation
  • Cycloplegia and moderate pupillary dilation
  • Moderate tachycardia from loss of vagal tone at the SA node
  • Reduced exocrine secretions → xerostomia and anhidrosis
CNS: Mecamylamine can cause sedation, tremors, choreiform movements, and mental aberrations.
LECTURE WARNINGThe PDF links anhidrosis to a risk for heat-related problems and notes a risk for stroke especially during summer.
Recall check: Why does ganglionic blockade cause tachycardia?
The SA node is usually under parasympathetic or vagal dominance. Blocking that ganglionic input removes the dominant slowing influence.
Recall check: What happens to blood pressure when sympathetic vascular tone is blocked?
Arteriolar and venous tone fall → decreased PVR, venous return, and blood pressure, with marked postural hypotension.

Commonly Confused Concepts

Pages 1–5
DO NOT CONFUSEAdrenergic neuron blocker vs postsynaptic receptor blocker
  • Neuron blocker → changes NE release, storage, uptake, or synthesis
  • Postsynaptic blocker → directly blocks adrenoceptors
  • Fast distinction: this lecture’s adrenergic antagonists are neuron-focused, not postsynaptic receptor blockers.
DO NOT CONFUSEReserpine vs Guanethidine
  • Reserpine → irreversible VMAT-2 inhibition, CNS plus peripheral depletion
  • Guanethidine → uptake-dependent adrenergic neuron blocker, peripheral because it is polar
  • Fast distinction: reserpine can sedate and depress, guanethidine does not cross the BBB.
DO NOT CONFUSEParasympathetic predominance vs parasympathetic agonism
  • Adrenergic antagonists do not directly stimulate cholinergic receptors
  • Parasympathetic signs appear because sympathetic competition is removed
DO NOT CONFUSENicotine low dose vs high dose
  • Low dose → ganglionic stimulation
  • High dose → persistent depolarization → ganglionic blockade
DO NOT CONFUSETrimethaphan vs Hexamethonium
  • Trimethaphan → competitive ganglionic blocker at the receptor
  • Hexamethonium → noncompetitive channel blocker
DO NOT CONFUSEGuanethidine reflex tachycardia vs ganglionic blocker tachycardia
  • Guanethidine → baroreceptor compensation after BP falls
  • Ganglionic blockers → loss of dominant vagal input at the SA node

Last-Minute Cram Sheet

≈ 5-minute review

Adrenergic antagonists

  • Core: inhibit adrenergic synaptic activity without direct postsynaptic receptor blockade
  • Reserpine: irreversible VMAT-2 block → monoamine depletion → depression risk
  • Guanethidine: uptake-dependent, polar, no BBB, postural hypotension, sexual dysfunction, rapid IV triple response
  • Methyldopa: central α₂ → pregnancy hypertension, sedation, lactation, Coombs-positive hemolysis
  • Metyrosine: catecholamine synthesis inhibitor → pheochromocytoma adjunct, crystalluria
  • Bretylium: early NE release then blockade, Class III, K⁺ channel block

Ganglionic blockers

  • Core: block nicotinic receptors in both SNS and PSNS ganglia
  • Dominant tone predicts effect → vessels dilate, heart speeds up, pupil dilates, accommodation is lost, GI and bladder activity fall
  • Nicotine: low dose stimulation, high dose depolarization block
  • Trimethaphan: competitive blocker, acute hypertensive emergency, controlled surgical hypotension
  • Hexamethonium: noncompetitive ion-channel blocker
  • Mecamylamine: orally absorbed, BBB crossing, central nicotinic effects, source has mechanism and duration inconsistencies
Five traps to memorize: Methyldopa = pregnancy. Reserpine = severe depression. Guanethidine = polar and uptake-dependent. Ganglionic blockade = effect follows dominant tone. Nicotine = low-dose stimulation, high-dose blockade.
