I. Beta-Lactams MUST KNOW pp. 1–5
Core idea: all β-lactams (penicillins, cephalosporins, monobactam, carbapenems) are cell wall synthesis inhibitors that bind PBPs (transpeptidases)
The β-lactam ring is the active part.
They are bactericidal and work best against actively dividing bacteria because those are the ones synthesizing cell wall.
Bacteria evade them 4 ways: destroy the drug, change the target, keep the drug out, pump the drug out.
A. Penicillin PROTOTYPE: Penicillin G pp. 1–2

Overview and chemistry p. 1

  • NICE In 1941 penicillin was accidentally obtained from Penicillium notatum. Currently largely obtained from Penicillium chrysogenum.
  • Cell wall synthesis inhibitor, used mainly against Gram-positive bacteria.
  • The mainly used penicillin is Penicillin G (benzylpenicillin).
  • The antibacterial (bactericidal) activity is due to the β-lactam ring, which is attached to a thiazolidine ring.
    • Base architecture: 6-aminopenicillanic acid nucleus (A-ring thiazolidine fused to B-ring β-lactam) Fig. 1
    • COOH + Na⁺ forms a salt for greater stability. Amidases substitute the amide group to produce semisynthetic penicillins.
    • Modification of the side chain changes the properties, but all penicillins still interfere with cell wall synthesis.

Mechanism of action MECHANISM pp. 1–2, Figs. 2–3

β-lactam antibiotic (structurally resembles the terminal D-Ala-D-Ala of the peptide precursor = molecular mimicry)
↓
PBP (transpeptidase) recognizes the β-lactam and binds it
→ instead of completing transpeptidation, the β-lactam forms a stable covalent complex
→ PBP functionally inactivated (irreversible covalent inhibition)
↓
Failure of peptidoglycan cross-linking
→ progressive weakening of the cell wall.
Also inhibits the suppression of autolysis: autolysins keep degrading existing peptidoglycan while no new cross-links form
→ structural integrity lost.
↓
Cell wall-deficient (CWD) bacterium
→ cell is hyperosmotic, cannot withstand internal osmotic pressure
→ water enters
→ cell swells
→ bursts
→ dies (osmotic lysis)
↓
Bactericidal. Works best against actively dividing bacteria (they are synthesizing cell wall).

Indications and side effects p. 1

  • SHOULD Indications: pharyngitis and laryngitis, rheumatic fever, pneumococcal infections, gonorrhea, syphilis, diphtheria and tetanus, gas gangrene, rat-bite fever, anthrax.
    • Prophylactic use: bacterial endocarditis, rheumatic fever.
  • Side effects listed: local irritation, pain at injection site, thrombophlebitis, muscular twitching, convulsions, bleeding due to interference with platelets, hallucinations, hypersensitivity (rashes, allergy, photosensitivity).

Penicillin kinetics SHOULD KNOW p. 2

  • Active renal clearance: 10% glomerular filtration, 90% active tubular secretion. Half-life brief (<1 hour normally).
  • Probenecid competitively blocks active tubular secretion → raises serum concentration and prolongs penicillin half-life.
  • Biliary clearance exception: Nafcillin cleared primarily via biliary excretion → no dose adjustment in renal impairment
    • Oxacillin uses both renal and biliary routes.
    • (Ceftriaxone is the cephalosporin exception, biliary, no renal adjustment.)
  • Tissue penetration: excellent in pleural, pericardial, peritoneal, synovial fluids. Ceftriaxone/cefotaxime cross the BBB to therapeutic levels in inflamed CSF.

Penicillin toxicities MUST KNOW p. 2

  • Hypersensitivity, the major adverse effect: mild maculopapular rashes to life-threatening anaphylactic shock (0.05% of patients).
    • Why: breakdown products like penicillinic acid bound to host proteins serve as hapten antigens.
  • Neurotoxicity and seizures: extremely high doses provoke seizures, confusion, encephalopathy, particularly with pre-existing renal dysfunction (drug accumulation).
  • Organ-specific: Nafcillin → neutropenia. Oxacillin → hepatitis. Methicillin → acute interstitial nephritis (obsolete clinical use).
EXAM TRAP
  • Antistaphylococcal penicillins (nafcillin, oxacillin, dicloxacillin, methicillin) cover MSSA, not MRSA
  • MRSA resists them by PBP2a (mecA), an altered target, so a "penicillinase-resistant" penicillin does not help.
  • Also: do not label every rash as anaphylaxis, clarify timing, symptoms and severity (allergy delabeling improves care, p. 24, Table 19).
B. Cephalosporins MUST KNOW pp. 2–4
  • Semi-synthetic, bactericidal. Derived from Acremonium chrysogenum (formerly Cephalosporium acremonium), a fungus. Among the most commonly prescribed antibiotics.
  • Cell wall synthesis inhibitor by interacting with PBPs, but the binding proteins are different from penicillin → spectrum is entirely different from penicillin.
    • Same 3 actions: inhibit transpeptidases, promote autolysis, increase CWD.
  • Chemistry: β-lactam ring combined with a dihydrothiazine ring (vs thiazolidine in penicillin)
    • At position 7 of the β-lactam ring, amidases allow substitution → differences in potency, efficacy, pharmacokinetics p. 3, Fig. 4.
  • Mechanism of resistance: change in affinity of transpeptidases, change in structure of transpeptidases, production of penicillinase (β-lactamase), decreased permeability of the cell wall.
  • Indications: alternative to penicillin.
    • URTI, urinary and soft tissue infections, septicemia, pharyngitis and meningitis, gonorrhea, typhoid fever, mixed aerobic and anaerobic infections, hospital-acquired infections, surgical prophylaxis.

Cephalosporin toxicity MUST KNOW p. 3

  • Cross-allergenicity with penicillins ≈ 5% to 10%
    • 📌 Cephalosporins are contraindicated in patients with a history of severe immediate penicillin hypersensitivity (anaphylaxis).
  • Neonatal jaundice: ceftriaxone displaces bilirubin from serum albumin → risk of kernicterus in neonates → avoid in newborns.
  • Biliary pseudolithiasis: ceftriaxone has high affinity for biliary calcium → calcium-ceftriaxone precipitation in the gallbladder.
  • Methylthiotetrazole (MTT) ring in Cefotetan and Cefamandole → inhibits vitamin K synthesis (hypoprothrombinemia/bleeding risk) and provokes a severe disulfiram-like reaction with alcohol.
  • Other listed side effects: pain at injection site, thrombophlebitis, diarrhea, hypersensitivity (rashes, shock, photosensitivity), hemolysis, nephrotoxicity, hematologic adverse effects.
    • Cefepime neurotoxicity in renal dysfunction (p. 24, Table 21).

  • Ceftaroline = MRSA (but not Pseudomonas)
  • Ceftriaxone ≠ Pseudomonas
  • Cefazolin, the 1st gen, is inactive against MRSA.

C. Monobactam (Aztreonam) and β-Lactamase Inhibitors p. 4
DRUG CARD Aztreonam PROTOTYPE monobactam p. 4
Class
  • Monobactam: monocyclic β-lactam isolated from Chromobacterium spp. The β-lactam ring is not fused to another ring (there is a second thiazole ring but it is not fused)
  • Other listed monobactams: tigemonam, carumonam, nocardicin.
Mechanism
Binds PBPs (transpeptidases) → inhibits peptidoglycan synthesis. Works only against aerobic Gram-negative bacteria.
Spectrum
Aerobic Gram-negative rods only, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa (also Neisseria). No Gram-positive, no anaerobic coverage → "narrower spectrum."
Uses
  • Gram-negative coverage in patients with β-lactam allergy: generally safe for severe penicillin-allergic patients who have a Gram-negative infection
  • Listed: hospital-acquired infections of urinary, biliary, GI and female genital tracts, cystic fibrosis, skin infections, pyelonephritis, septicemia, peritonitis, endometritis.
Adverse effects
  • Skin rash, occasional abnormal liver function, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, abnormal taste.
  • Cross-sensitivity with cephalosporin allergy is listed as a side effect.
Cross-allergy
No cross-allergenicity with penicillins or cephalosporins because the monocyclic core is not recognized by antibodies → EXCEPT ceftazidime: identical chemical side chain, so a ceftazidime allergy means an aztreonam allergy.
Resistance
Change in affinity/structure of transpeptidases. Most bacteria produce β-lactamases.
Recall cue
"Aztreonam = aerobic Gram-negatives only, allergy-safe except ceftazidime."

β-Lactamase inhibitors MUST KNOW p. 4, p. 24 Table 20

  • Suicide inhibitors: clavulanic acid, sulbactam, tazobactam are structurally related to β-lactams but have weak inherent antibacterial activity.
  • Mechanism: covalently bind and irreversibly inactivate serine β-lactamases (especially Class A plasmid-mediated enzymes) → protects the co-administered antibiotic.
    • Limited against AmpC and many carbapenemases.
  • Clinical synergies: Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (oral, Augmentin), Ampicillin-Sulbactam (IV, Unasyn), Piperacillin-Tazobactam (IV, Zosyn) expand spectrum against resistant strains.
  • Newer inhibitors (Table 20): avibactam, relebactam are non-β-lactam inhibitors active against selected class A/C and some class D enzymes.
    • Vaborbactam is a boronic inhibitor designed particularly for KPC, combined with meropenem.
  • Rule: an inhibitor does not add activity if the organism is resistant by altered PBP, permeability, efflux, or an uninhibited enzyme.
D. Carbapenems MUST KNOW pp. 4–5
  • Among the broadest-spectrum β-lactams: Imipenem, Meropenem, Doripenem, Ertapenem
    • Considered the "big guns" 💬.
    • Inhibit cell wall synthesis through PBPs, with excellent stability against many β-lactamases
    • Inhibits penicillinase-producing staphylococci and some MRSA (as written, p. 4).
  • Stewardship: reserved for serious infections, particularly resistant organisms. Use judiciously, emergence of carbapenem resistance limits treatment options.
  • NICE Thienamycin was the first carbapenem discovered (1976)
    • Chemistry: very similar to penicillins (penams) but the sulfur at position 1 is replaced by carbon and unsaturation is introduced, hence "carbapenem.".
  • Classification (Table 6): Group 1 = Ertapenem. Group 2 = Imipenem, Meropenem, Doripenem.
  • Uses: nosocomial infections (septicemia, febrile neutropenia, intra-abdominal, pelvic), respiratory, ENT, genitourinary, multidrug-resistant infections.
    • Resistance: decreased permeability, target modification, modified β-lactamases (carbapenemases).

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: β-Lactams
  • β-lactam ring mimics D-Ala-D-Ala → covalently inactivates PBP/transpeptidase → no cross-linking + autolysins → osmotic lysis → bactericidal, best on dividing bacteria.
  • 4 escapes: destroy (β-lactamase → penicilloic acid), change target (PBP2a/mecA in MRSA), keep out (porin loss), pump out (efflux).
  • Penicillin G IV / V oral.
    • Antistaph (nafcillin, oxacillin, dicloxacillin) = MSSA not MRSA
    • Piperacillin = Pseudomonas
    • BLIs (clavulanate, sulbactam, tazobactam) = suicide inhibitors of serine β-lactamases.
  • Hypersensitivity = major AE (hapten from penicillinic acid, anaphylaxis 0.05%)
    • High dose + renal failure → seizures.
    • Nafcillin neutropenia, oxacillin hepatitis, methicillin interstitial nephritis.
    • Nafcillin/oxacillin/ceftriaxone = biliary, no renal adjustment.
    • Probenecid ↑ penicillin level.
  • Cephalosporins: 1st cefazolin (surgical prophylaxis), 2nd cefoxitin/cefotetan (anaerobes), 3rd ceftriaxone (meningitis, gonorrhea) and ceftazidime (Pseudomonas), 4th cefepime (Pseudomonas, febrile neutropenia), 5th ceftaroline (MRSA)
    • Cross-allergy 5–10%, contraindicated if penicillin anaphylaxis.
    • Ceftriaxone: neonatal kernicterus, biliary pseudolithiasis.
    • MTT ring (cefotetan, cefamandole): bleeding + disulfiram-like.
  • Aztreonam: aerobic Gram-negatives only, safe in penicillin allergy except ceftazidime side chain.
  • Carbapenems: broadest. Imipenem + cilastatin (dehydropeptidase-1), imipenem seizures (GABA). Ertapenem once daily but no Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter.
II. Glycopeptides and other cell wall / membrane agents MUST KNOW pp. 5–7
Core idea: vancomycin also stops cell wall building, but by binding the substrate (D-Ala-D-Ala), not the enzyme (PBP)
It is a huge molecule, so it cannot cross Gram-negative porins → Gram-positive only
Resistance = change the substrate to D-Ala-D-Lac.
A. Vancomycin PROTOTYPE glycopeptide pp. 5–6
DRUG CARD Vancomycin pp. 5–6, p. 24 Table 23
Class
Glycopeptide antibiotic. A massive, water-soluble tricyclic glycopeptide (MW ~1500) produced by Amycolatopsis orientalis.
Mechanism
  • Does NOT inhibit PBPs
  • Binds directly to the terminal D-alanyl-D-alanine (D-Ala-D-Ala) of nascent peptidoglycan pentapeptides (NAM/NAG peptides), forming five hydrogen bonds → caps the precursor → sterically inhibits both transglycosylase (glycan chain elongation, linking the sugars) and transpeptidase (cross-linking the peptides) → halts cell wall construction.
  • Action is outside the cell membrane, at a late stage of cell wall assembly.
Spectrum
  • Gram-positive only: the large molecule cannot pass the narrow outer membrane porin channels of Gram-negatives.
  • Covers MRSA, susceptible enterococci, streptococci, C. difficile (luminal).
Pharmacokinetics
  • Routes: oral, continuous infusion, intermittent injection.
  • Poor oral absorption → IV for systemic infection.
  • 90% excreted by glomerular filtration, accumulates in renal failure.
  • Half-life 6 hours
  • TDM is mandatory: target trough 15–20 mcg/mL for severe MRSA infection (p. 21 notes trough is measured every 4th dose, and Table 17 notes monitoring commonly focuses on AUC rather than trough alone).
Uses
  • Septicemia, infective endocarditis, bone infections, skin and skin structure infections, pneumonia, MRSA infections, C. difficile infection.
  • Oral vancomycin only for C. difficile colitis: it is not absorbed and stays concentrated in the GI tract (patients have watery or bloody diarrhea with colon inflammation).
Adverse effects
  • Vancomycin infusion reaction / flushing syndrome ("Red man syndrome", outdated term): infusion-related flushing and erythematous rash of the face and upper torso.
    • Why: non-immunologic, direct mast-cell histamine release, rate-dependent, not a true allergy
    • Prevented by slow infusion over 1–2 hours or antihistamines beforehand.
  • Nephrotoxicity: dose-related acute kidney injury, very common.
    • Risk ↑ with prolonged high troughs, pre-existing renal dysfunction, concurrent nephrotoxins (aminoglycosides)
    • Modern preparations are purer.
  • Ototoxicity: less common, often irreversible, tinnitus, vertigo, hearing loss, higher risk in severe renal impairment.
  • Also listed: stomach pain, nausea, diarrhea, rash, frequent urination, neutropenia (Table 23).
Toxicity management
Trough below target → administer vancomycin again. Trough too high → skip the dose, give the next scheduled dose.
Drug interactions
Listed: acyclovir, cefepime, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, furosemide, gentamicin, tobramycin (nephrotoxins), ibuprofen, meloxicam, naproxen, sirolimus, tacrolimus, sulfamethoxazole, metformin, methotrexate, piperacillin/tazobactam.
Resistance
See flowchart below: D-Ala-D-Lac (VRE, VRSA), D-Ala-D-Ser, thickened wall (VISA).
Recall cue
"Vanco caps the D-Ala-D-Ala, five H-bonds, Gram+ only, trough 15–20, slow infusion or red man."

Vancomycin resistance MUST KNOW p. 5

Vancomycin binds a substrate, so bacteria resist by altering the substrate
↓
Replace terminal D-Ala-D-Ala
→ D-Ala-D-Lactate (VanA, VanB phenotypes) or D-Serine
→ this replaces an NH group with an oxygen atom
↓
Loss of one crucial hydrogen bond → binding affinity decreases ~1,000-fold
↓
Drug ineffective = genetic basis of VRE and VRSA
Separate mechanism: VISA (vancomycin-intermediate S. aureus) makes thickened cell walls with excess non-functional D-Ala-D-Ala that sequester vancomycin before it reaches active membrane sites.
CLINICAL PEARL (p. 6, Clinical considerations)
  • Antimicrobial selection is not simply about which drug kills the organism.
  • Always consider renal function, hepatic function, allergy history, concomitant medications, patient-specific toxicity risk.
  • For a renally eliminated drug ask: What is the patient's renal function? Does the dose need renal adjustment? Does the drug require therapeutic monitoring, such as vancomycin? Vancomycin is inferior to an appropriate anti-staphylococcal β-lactam for MSSA, and oral vancomycin does not treat systemic infection (p. 24, Table 23).

B–C. Oritavancin and Teicoplanin SHOULD KNOW p. 6
Oritavancin
  • Newer glycopeptide: prevents transglycosylation and transpeptidation during cell wall synthesis.
  • Retains strong Gram-positive activity including resistant organisms, with PK advantages over traditional agents. Effective against VRS and VRE.
  • Phase IV trial for skin, soft tissue and MRSA infections. Very long half-life (>10 days) permits once-weekly dosing (Fig. 7).
Teicoplanin
  • Mechanism similar to vancomycin: binds D-Ala-D-Ala terminus of peptidoglycan precursors → prevents cross-linking → cell lysis, bactericidal vs Gram-positives.
    • 💬 "Think of vancomycin with prolonged pharmacokinetics and Gram-positive activity.".
  • Spectrum: S. aureus (incl. MRSA), S. epidermidis, streptococci, enterococci, C. difficile (alternative to vancomycin). Not Gram-negatives.
  • PK: long half-life ~70–100 h, dosing every 3–4 days, excreted mostly unchanged (the trans line "hot tissue penetration" is garbled, likely "good").
  • Uses: MRSA/MRSE, endocarditis, bone and joint, bacteremia, pneumonia, skin/soft tissue, β-lactam-allergic patients, C. difficile colitis (oral form).
  • AEs: rash/pruritus, fever, nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity (rare).

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: Glycopeptides and others
  • Vancomycin: binds D-Ala-D-Ala (5 H-bonds), blocks transglycosylase + transpeptidase, Gram+ only, IV for systemic MRSA, oral only for C. difficile, 90% GFR clearance, trough 15–20 mcg/mL.
  • Resistance: D-Ala-D-Lac (VanA/VanB) loses 1 H-bond → 1,000-fold ↓ affinity (VRE). VISA = thick wall sequesters drug.

  • Daptomycin: membrane depolarization, myopathy (check CPK), not for pneumonia (surfactant)

III. Protein Synthesis Inhibitors: Overview and Ribosomal Targets MUST KNOW p. 7
Core idea: bacterial ribosomes are 70S (30S + 50S), human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S (40S + 60S) → drugs bind bacterial ribosomes selectively.
Most are bacteriostatic (stop growth, host immune system clears the infection)
Exception: aminoglycosides are bactericidal because they bind irreversibly and cause misreading.
Mitochondria also have 70S ribosomes, which explains toxicity with prolonged, high-dose use.
💬 MNEMONIC (from lecture, p. 7)
  • "Buy AT 30, CELL for 50": AT = Aminoglycosides, Tetracyclines are 30S inhibitors.
  • CELL = Chloramphenicol, Erythromycin (macrolides), Linezolid, Lincosamides (clindamycin) are 50S inhibitors.

A. Selective toxicity p. 7

Prokaryotic 70S target
  • High therapeutic index.
  • Subunit split: small 30S + large 50S.
  • Precise targeting: conserved rRNA folds and distinct ribosomal proteins allow selective drug binding.
  • Bacteriostatic arrest: reversible binding halts translation without killing the host cell, relying on intact immune defenses to clear bacteria.
Eukaryotic 80S exclusions
  • Cytoplasmic ribosomes are 40S and 60S.
  • Steric resistance: structural variations in host rRNA and auxiliary translation factors prevent binding.
  • Spare host machinery: protects mammalian protein synthesis at therapeutic levels.
  • But mitochondrial 70S ribosomes can be hit → toxicity with prolonged/high doses (chloramphenicol marrow suppression, linezolid).

IV. Tetracyclines MUST KNOW pp. 8–9
Core idea: broad-spectrum, bacteriostatic 30S inhibitors (tetracycline, doxycycline, minocycline, tigecycline) that block aminoacyl-tRNA from the A-site
Clinically the drugs for atypicals (Chlamydia, Mycoplasma, Rickettsia) and spirochetes.
💬 "Very popular because doxycycline is useful as prophylaxis against leptospirosis." Hallmarks: chelate cations, teeth and bone effects → contraindicated in pregnancy and children under 8.

Overview and classification p. 8

  • NICE First isolated from soil bacteria (Actinomycetes). Semi-synthetic tetracycline is water soluble and bitter.
  • Broad spectrum (Gram-positive and Gram-negative, even protozoa and anaerobes), bacteriostatic.
    • Spectrum: cocci (G+ and G−), bacilli, spirochetes, Mycoplasma, Actinomyces.

Mechanism: translation arrest, step by step MECHANISM p. 8

  1. Entry and concentration: passive diffusion across the outer membrane of Gram-negatives, then active transport concentrates the drug intracellularly (high target-site levels).
  2. Ribosomal binding: reversibly binds specific pockets on the bacterial 30S subunit, blocking access to the acceptor (A) site.
  3. tRNA exclusion: physical barrier blocks aminoacyl-tRNA from associating with the ribosome, the anticodon cannot bind the matching codon on mRNA.
  4. Translation stop: no new amino acids added → growing peptide chain stops elongating → reversible bacteriostatic arrest, replication suppressed without destroying the existing cell wall.

  • Atypical pathogens: primary drugs for Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydia, Rickettsia (Rocky Mountain spotted fever).

    • Second choice: tetanus, anthrax, gonorrhea, syphilis, pneumonia, empirical therapy, prophylaxis for COPD.
  • PK: doxycycline and minocycline well absorbed orally, tigecycline IV only. Doxycycline eliminated non-renally via feces → no renal dose adjustment.

Adverse effects and contraindications, with the why MUST KNOW p. 9

  • Calcium chelation: tetracyclines chelate divalent and trivalent cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Al³⁺) → absorption prevented by dairy, antacids, iron (separate from them, take with water, remain upright, p. 25).
  • Tissue binding and discoloration: bind tightly to newly forming bone and teeth → permanent brown enamel discoloration and bone growth inhibition.
    • → Pediatric contraindication: strictly avoided in children younger than 8 and throughout pregnancy (also lactating mothers)
      • Exception (p. 27): doxycycline can still be appropriate for life-threatening rickettsial disease.
  • Demeclocycline → nephrogenic diabetes insipidus. Older agents: phototoxicity, hepatic necrosis at high doses.
  • Other listed side effects: jaundice, hepatic cell necrosis, superinfection, epigastric pain, nausea, diarrhea, esophageal ulcer, odynophagia, Fanconi syndrome, antianabolic action, nystagmus/ataxia/vertigo (minocycline vestibular effects, p. 25), hypersensitivity, photosensitivity.
EXAM TRAP
  • Tigecycline is the tetracycline that escapes efflux (Tet AE) and Tet(M) protection, but it is IV-only and has low serum/urine levels, no Pseudomonas/Proteus
  • Doxycycline is the versatile oral one with non-renal (fecal) elimination.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: Tetracyclines
  • Reversible 30S/16S binding → A-site blocked → bacteriostatic.
  • DOC for atypicals (Mycoplasma, Chlamydia, Rickettsia), cholera, Lyme, leptospirosis prophylaxis (doxy).
  • Resistance: efflux Tet(AE)/Tet(K), ribosomal protection Tet(M). Tigecycline overcomes both.
  • Chelates Ca/Mg/Fe/Al → no dairy/antacids/iron. Teeth/bone binding → avoid <8 y and pregnancy. Demeclocycline → nephrogenic DI. Doxycycline = fecal elimination.

IX. Aminoglycosides and Spectinomycin MUST KNOW pp. 11–15
Core idea: rapidly bactericidal 30S inhibitors for aerobic Gram-negative bacilli.
Four concepts to connect (p. 11): how the drug enters (oxygen-dependent), how it hits the 30S (irreversible, misreading), why killing is concentration dependent (once-daily dosing, PAE), and why toxicity (nephro-, oto-, neuromuscular) dictates dosing and monitoring.
📌 Major toxicities: nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, neuromuscular blockade.

Overview and chemistry pp. 11–12

  • Major agents: Gentamicin, Tobramycin, Amikacin, Streptomycin, Neomycin, kanamycin, paromomycin, plazomicin.
    • Particularly useful vs Gram-negatives
    • Activity vs Gram-positives is limited → useful when combined with cell-wall-active antibiotics
    • Uses: empiric therapy for endocarditis, sepsis, intra-abdominal infections.
  • NICE 1943: first aminoglycoside streptomycin from soil Actinomycetes. Systemic: streptomycin, gentamicin, kanamycin, tobramycin. Topical: neomycin, framycetin.
  • Chemistry: central hexose ring (streptidine in streptomycin, 2-deoxystreptamine in gentamicin, tobramycin, amikacin) attached to amino sugars via glycosidic linkages.
    • Numerous amino groups → highly polar, positively charged (polybasic) → very water soluble, poorly cross lipid membranes, poor oral absorption, poor CSF penetration, structure unchanged in urine.
    • pH sensitivity: more active in alkaline environments, less effective in acidic ones (abscesses, infected lung tissue) 💬 → clinical implication for pneumonia and abscesses.
    • Polybasic subclass (neomycin, paromomycin, kanamycin): extreme nephrotoxicity → restricted to topical/oral routes.

Three-phase uptake MECHANISM p. 12

  1. Outer membrane passive diffusion: cationic AG binds negatively charged LPS, crosses the Gram-negative outer membrane rapidly and passively through hydrophilic porin channels (OmpF, OmpC) into the periplasmic space.
    • Concentration dependent, no energy needed.
  2. Active inner membrane transport (Energy Dependent Phase I): the more important step.
    • Crossing the cytoplasmic membrane is energy-dependent, driven by the bacterial electron transport chain / proton motive force (PMF) and transmembrane electrochemical gradient (needs ATP and oxygen).
  3. Oxygen dependence and limits: Phase 2 requires aerobic cellular respiration
    • Under anaerobic conditions or low pH the gradient collapses → transport blocked → strict anaerobes are intrinsically resistant, and abscesses/necrotic tissue prevent entry.
  • Factors limiting uptake: oxygen required, pH dependent, porin expression (loss or mutation decreases passive diffusion), biofilm and necrosis limit penetration.

30S translation blockade: three simultaneous disruptions p. 12

3. Polysome breakup (monosomes)Blocks ribosomal translocation down mRNA → polysomes break into non-functional isolated monosomes that permanently freeze translation

Bacterial biochemical escapes MUST KNOW p. 13, Fig. 13

1. Enzymatic modification (most important clinically)

  • Modified drug fails to bind 30S.
  • Amikacin and plazomicin were designed to resist these enzymes.
2. Decreased penetration
  • Porin mutations/deletions prevent early passive diffusion.
  • Proton-pump / electron transport disruption lowers the PMF → blocks active energy-dependent transport.
3. Altered ribosomal target
  • Chromosomal single-step mutations: receptor deletion of the 30S protein receptor, or binding site shift (16S rRNA) → drastically lower affinity.
  • Observed readily during streptomycin monotherapy.
  • Resistance is multifactorial. Overcoming it often requires combination therapy and stewardship.

Strategic dosing MUST KNOW p. 14, Fig. 14

Concentration-dependent killing
  • Rate and extent of killing ↑ as the peak concentration rises above the MIC.
  • Critical parameter: Cmax/MIC ratio, not time above MIC.
  • Peak target for gentamicin/tobramycin in serious Gram-negative bacteremia: 8–10 mcg/mL.
Post-antibiotic effect (PAE)
  • Continued suppression of bacterial growth after drug falls below the MIC or becomes undetectable.
  • Lasts 2–6 hours vs Gram-negatives.
  • Why: sublethal persistent damage to translation machinery, irreversibly bound 30S prevents rapid recovery.
Once-daily (extended-interval) dosing: single large IV dose, 5–7 mg/kg gentamicin/tobramycin

  • Toxicity threshold and TDM: toxicity is time- and concentration-dependent, occurs when tissue accumulation crosses a threshold over time.

Excretion and renal dosing MUST KNOW p. 15

  • Strictly renal clearance, proportional to GFR (creatinine clearance)
    • Half-life normally 2–3 h, in severe renal impairment 24–48 h → doses accumulate → toxicity → adjust to renal function.
    • 📌 Know renal function before giving and monitor during therapy.
  • Poor dialysis clearance: only partially and irregularly removed by hemodialysis (40–60% for gentamicin), even less by peritoneal dialysis.
  • Once-daily dosing is avoided if CrCl < 60 mL/min (safe at GFR > 60). Below the threshold use traditional dosing:
    • Method A (increased interval): keep the mg/kg dose constant, lengthen the interval (q8h → q12h → q24h) to give kidneys time.
    • Method B (reduced dose): keep the interval (q8h), reduce the dose by formulas relating GFR to dose.

Major adverse effects MUST KNOW p. 15

1. Nephrotoxicity — saturable lysosomal accumulation in tubular epithelium
Mechanism
Selective accumulation in the renal cortex, proximal tubular cells via saturable transport → over-accumulation → lysosomal swelling, rupture, cell damage → acute tubular necrosis (p. 21).
Incidence / course
Reversible AKI in 5–25% of patients treated >3–5 days. Reversible after discontinuation. Risk ↑ with prolonged exposure.
Most nephrotoxic
Neomycin, tobramycin, gentamicin. Plazomicin lower AKI rates.
Earliest indicator
A sudden rise in the trough serum aminoglycoside level itself, NOT rising creatinine.
Potentiators
Concurrent nephrotoxins: loop diuretics (furosemide), vancomycin, amphotericin B.
2. Ototoxicity — irreversible hair cell death
Nature
  • Progressive destruction of inner-ear sensory hair cells, strictly irreversible (unlike nephrotoxicity)
  • Incidence 1–5%, risk ↑ beyond 5 days, elderly, impaired GFR.
Auditory (cochlear)
Tinnitus, high-frequency hearing loss. Main auditory toxins: Neomycin, Kanamycin, Amikacin (p. 21 also lists tobramycin as cochlear).
Vestibular
Severe vertigo, ataxia, loss of balance. Main vestibulotoxins: Streptomycin, Gentamicin.
Genetics
Susceptibility is linked to maternal mitochondrial DNA point mutations (A1555G).
Amplifier
  • Loop diuretics (furosemide, ethacrynic acid) accelerate tissue accumulation → permanent deafness.
  • Prevention (p. 21): baseline audiometry if >5 days, keep peak <10 mg/L, extended-interval dosing.
3. Neuromuscular blockade — curare-like respiratory paralysis
Mechanism
Very high doses block presynaptic calcium channels at the motor nerve terminal → prevents calcium-dependent exocytosis of acetylcholine vesicles → skeletal muscle weakness, respiratory paralysis.
Historic setting
Massive intraperitoneal instillation of kanamycin (3–5 g) or neomycin post-operatively.
📌 Antidotes
  • First-line: calcium gluconate IV (floods the terminal with calcium to restore ACh release)
  • Second-line: neostigmine (acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, maximizes ACh at the junction).
Contraindication
  • Myasthenia gravis: transmission already impaired → AG can precipitate a lethal respiratory crisis → strictly contraindicated.
  • Effect enhanced with muscle relaxants.
L. Spectinomycin SHOULD KNOW p. 15
  • Aminocyclitol structurally related to aminoglycosides but lacks amino sugars and glycosidic bonds.
  • Binds reversibly to 30S → protein synthesis inhibition. Unlike aminoglycosides it is bacteriostatic, not bactericidal, because binding is reversible.
  • Use: almost exclusively an alternative for drug-resistant gonorrhea or severe penicillin/cephalosporin allergy.
    • Not for pharyngeal gonorrhea (poor efficacy, high failure)
    • No cross-resistance with other anti-gonorrheal agents.
  • Single IM injection (2–4 g), rapidly absorbed. Pain at injection site, fever, nausea common. Nephrotoxicity and anemia extremely rare.
EXAM TRAPS: aminoglycosides
  • No anaerobes, no abscesses, no pneumonia monotherapy: uptake needs oxygen and alkaline pH.
  • Nephrotoxicity is reversible, ototoxicity is irreversible. Gentamicin/streptomycin = vestibular, neomycin/kanamycin/amikacin = cochlear.
  • Earliest sign of nephrotoxicity = rising trough level, not creatinine.
  • Once-daily dosing only if CrCl > 60. Trough >2 mcg/mL = toxicity.
  • Neuromuscular blockade antidote = calcium gluconate first, neostigmine second. Contraindicated in myasthenia gravis.
  • Furosemide potentiates both nephro- and ototoxicity.
  • Spectinomycin looks like an aminoglycoside but is static (reversible binding).
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: Aminoglycosides
  • Cationic, polar → porins → O₂/PMF-dependent inner membrane transport → irreversible 30S binding → initiation block + misreading + polysome breakup → aberrant proteins in membrane → self-feeding uptake → cidal.
  • Aerobic Gram-negatives.
    • Synergy with cell-wall agents ("penicillin opens the door"): gentamicin + penicillin/ampicillin/vanco for endocarditis, tobramycin + antipseudomonal β-lactam for Pseudomonas sepsis.
  • Resistance: AAC/APH/ANT enzymes (amikacin, plazomicin resist), porin/PMF loss, 30S target change (streptomycin monotherapy).
  • Concentration-dependent + PAE 2–6 h → once daily 5–7 mg/kg, peak 8–10, trough <1 (toxic >2)
    • Avoid once-daily if CrCl <60.
    • Renal excretion, half-life 2–3 h (24–48 h in renal failure).
  • Tobramycin = Pseudomonas + inhaled CF (300 mg, 28 on/28 off). Amikacin = resists enzymes, MDR-TB. Plazomicin = CRE, trough-only <3.
  • Nephrotoxicity (lysosomal, reversible, 5–25%), ototoxicity (irreversible, 1–5%, A1555G, furosemide), NM blockade (Ca channel, antidote calcium gluconate → neostigmine, avoid in myasthenia).
X–XI. Antimetabolites: Sulfonamides and Trimethoprim MUST KNOW pp. 15–18
Core idea: bacteria cannot import folate and must synthesize THF de novo, mammals absorb dietary folate → selective toxicity.

Trimethoprim mimics folic acid and blocks dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR)
Each alone is bacteriostatic, together (sequential blockade, TMP-SMX 5:1) they become bactericidal
📌 "TMP-SMX: two hits, one folate pathway.".

Sulfonamide mechanism: competitive antimetabolite p. 16

  • PABA structural nucleus: high structural similarity to PABA, the natural precursor substrate.
  • Competitive active-site binding: bind reversibly and competitively to DHPS, blocking PABA → halts dihydropteroic acid production.
  • False substrate incorporation: the enzyme can incorporate the sulfonamide instead of PABA → non-functional, dead-end pteridine-sulfonamide conjugate.
  • Bacteriostatic arrest: folate depletion halts cell division but does not actively kill existing cells.
  • Chemistry/PK: weak acids, solubility reduced at pH < 5.5 → precipitation and crystalluria in acidic urine
    • Modest tissue penetration, bind extensively to plasma albumin (competing with bilirubin), hepatic N-acetylation, renal excretion.
  • Resistance (p. 16): change in enzyme affinity, ↑ PABA, alternate pathway.
    • Uses listed: UTI, upper and lower RTI, bacterial diarrhea/dysentery, pneumonia prevention, typhoid, burn infection prevention.
    • Side effects listed: nausea, vomiting, epigastric pain, crystalluria, photosensitivity, hepatitis, hemolysis, kernicterus.

Trimethoprim mechanism: selective DHFR blockade p. 17

  • Folic acid structural analogue: resembles the pteridine portion of folic acid → binds reversibly and competitively to bacterial DHFR → halts reduction of DHF to THF → depletion of one-carbon donors for nucleotide synthesis.
  • 100,000-fold selectivity: bacterial DHFR is 4 to 5 orders of magnitude (10,000 to 100,000×) more sensitive to trimethoprim than mammalian DHFR → selective toxicity even though the enzyme is conserved.
  • Pyrimethamine parallel: related DHFR inhibitor with high affinity for protozoal DHFR → malaria, toxoplasma.
  • Weak base → ion trapping in acidic environments → exceptionally high concentrations in prostatic and vaginal fluids → ideal for bacterial prostatitis and vaginitis.
  • Renal: 60–80% excreted unchanged in urine → high bladder concentrations for UTI.
    • Half-life 10–12 h, matching sulfamethoxazole (10 h) → stable fixed-dose ratio.
    • Trimethoprim raises serum creatinine by inhibiting tubular secretion without necessarily reducing GFR (Table 31).

Antifolate toxicity 📌 p. 18

📌 Sulfonamide toxicity and hypersensitivity
  • Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) / TEN: the most feared reaction, painful blisters and skin sloughing. Sulfonamides are classic triggers.
  • Crystalluria and hematuria: precipitation in acidic urine blocks renal tubules → pain, hematuria. Prevent with high fluid intake and urine alkalinization.
  • G6PD deficiency hemolysis: acute RBC oxidative stress → hemolytic anemia.
  • Neonatal kernicterus: sulfonamides displace bilirubin from albumin → free bilirubin crosses the newborn BBB → basal ganglia deposition.
    • Contraindicated in late (3rd trimester) pregnancy and neonates.
  • Also: photosensitivity, mixed hepatocellular/cholestatic hepatitis (p. 21).
📌 Trimethoprim toxicity and AIDS sensitivity
  • Megaloblastic anemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia: DHFR blockade → folate deficiency (bone marrow suppression).
    • Folinic acid (leucovorin) rescue completely prevents or reverses it, because bacteria cannot absorb folinic acid.
  • Hyperkalemia: trimethoprim acts as a weak potassium-sparing diuretic, blocking epithelial sodium channels in distal tubules.
  • AIDS hypersensitivity: 50–80% of AIDS patients on TMP-SMX get severe reactions (high fever, painful rash, profound leukopenia, diarrhea) → alternative therapy.
  • Monitor: potassium, creatinine, CBC (Table 31).
EXAM TRAPS: antifolates
  • Alone = static, together = cidal. The mechanism word for the combination is sequential blockade.
  • Sulfonamide = weak acid (crystalluria in acidic urine, alkalinize). Trimethoprim = weak base (ion trapping in prostate/vagina).
  • Marrow suppression from TMP is reversed by folinic acid (leucovorin), not folic acid, and it does not rescue the bacteria.
  • Kernicterus: sulfonamides and ceftriaxone both displace bilirubin.
  • Review Q1 (p. 28): the agent that prevents bacterial folic acid formation is sulfamethoxazole (PABA mimic), not azithromycin, meropenem or ciprofloxacin.
HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: Antifolates
  • Bacteria synthesize folate de novo (no transporter), mammals absorb it → selectivity.
  • Sulfonamide = PABA analog → DHPS, static, weak acid.
    • Trimethoprim = pteridine/folate analog → DHFR (100,000× bacterial selectivity), static, weak base, prostate/vagina/urine.
  • TMP-SMX 5:1 sequential blockade → bactericidal, slower resistance. DOC: PJP, Nocardia, UTI, CA-MRSA/Listeria backup, toxo (pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine).
  • Resistance: mutated DHPS/DHFR, PABA overproduction (70×), DHFR overproduction, efflux/porins.
  • Sulfa: SJS/TEN, crystalluria, G6PD hemolysis, kernicterus (late pregnancy/neonates). TMP: megaloblastic anemia (leucovorin rescue), hyperkalemia, AIDS reactions 50–80%.

Core idea: semi-synthetic bactericidal agents that inhibit DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II) and topoisomerase IV, trapping the enzyme-DNA cleavable complex → lethal double-strand breaks

Hallmarks: cation chelation, tendon rupture / cartilage damage (no children <18, no pregnancy), QTc, CNS (GABA), dysglycemia, aortic aneurysm.

Mechanism of action MECHANISM p. 19, Fig. 25

Fluoroquinolone
├── Target 1: DNA gyrase (topoisomerase II), primary in Gram-negatives. Gyrase introduces negative supercoils to neutralize the positive supercoils created ahead of the replication fork → FQ blocks it → replication fork progression stops.
├── Target 2: Topoisomerase IV, primary in Gram-positives. Responsible for decatenation, separating the two linked daughter chromosomes after replication → inhibition prevents cell division.
└── Lethal double-strand breaks: FQs do not merely inhibit, they bind and freeze the enzyme-DNA complex mid-cleavage → DNA trapped in a broken state → genomic fragmentation → the cell releases exonucleases → lysis (p. 18) → rapidly bactericidal, concentration-dependent killing with PAE (like aminoglycosides).
  • Dual-targeting strategy: bacteria must mutate both enzymes for high-level resistance, which happens rarely.

Pharmacokinetics MUST KNOW p. 19, Fig. 26

  • Outstanding oral bioavailability 70–95% (cipro, levo, moxi) → easy IV-to-oral transition. Wide tissue penetration: bone, lung, prostate, phagocytic cells.
  • Cation chelation hazard: the quinolone carboxylic acid group chelates Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺ → insoluble, non-absorbable complexes → absorption blocked → therapeutic failure
    • Applies to antacids (Mg, Al), calcium supplements, iron, sucralfate, dairy.
    • Separate dosing by 2–6 h (Fig. 26).
  • Active renal secretion (cipro, levo, oflo): glomerular filtration + active tubular secretion via organic anion transporters → 📌 probenecid blocks this secretion → raises serum FQ levels
    • Renal dose adjustment mandatory if CrCl < 50 mL/min to prevent CNS toxicity.
  • Moxifloxacin hepatic exception: hepatic metabolism, biliary excretion → no renal adjustment but no therapeutic urine levels → cannot treat UTI.

Clinical outlook: the restraint mandate MUST KNOW pp. 20–21

  • FQs are one of the most potent broad-spectrum oral classes, but historic overuse → rising resistance in common Enterobacteria.
    • FDA safety alerts (Achilles rupture, peripheral neuropathy, aortic dissection) frequently outweigh the benefit for minor infections like bronchitis, sinusitis, uncomplicated UTI.
  • Consensus = restraint: do not prescribe when a narrower agent like amoxicillin or nitrofurantoin will work.
    • Reserve for Pseudomonas bacteremia, complicated osteomyelitis, nosocomial pneumonia, MDR-TB.
  • Stewardship roadmap: de-escalate (start narrow, e.g. nitrofurantoin for simple cystitis)
    • Risk-stratify: age >60, CKD, corticosteroids, G6PD status, pre-existing QTc prolongation.
    • Monitor for Achilles tendon pain, severe sunlight sensitivity, skin irritation → promptly discontinue.

  • Moxifloxacin is the odd one: hepatic clearance, no UTI, unreliable Pseudomonas, highest QTc, but adds anaerobes
    • Ciprofloxacin = best Pseudomonas, weak pneumococcus.

    • Only levofloxacin is reliably antipseudomonal among respiratory FQs (Table 32).

HIGH-YIELD SUMMARY: Fluoroquinolones
  • Gyrase (Gram−) + Topo IV (Gram+) → frozen cleavable complex → double-strand breaks → cidal, concentration-dependent, PAE.
  • Cipro: Pseudomonas, UTI, diarrhea, anthrax. Levo/moxi: respiratory (pneumococcus). Moxi/delafloxacin: anaerobes, delafloxacin MRSA. Moxi not for UTI.
  • PK: 70–95% oral, chelated by cations, renal (probenecid ↑), moxi hepatic.
  • Resistance: gyrA/parC QRDR, efflux, porin (OprD) loss, plasmid Qnr. Gonorrhea escaped.
  • Toxicity: tendon rupture (Mg chelation, steroids), cartilage (no <18/pregnancy), aortic dissection, QTc (IKr, no class IA/III), CNS (GABA), dysglycemia (gatifloxacin withdrawn), phototoxicity, C. diff
    • Use with restraint.

C. Superinfection pp. 21–22

  • Prolonged antibiotic use disrupts normal microbiota → secondary infection by opportunists: Clostridioides difficile, Candida.
  • Risk factors: prolonged courses (>7 days), broad-spectrum agents, immunosuppression (chemotherapy, transplant), ICU/critical illness, indwelling devices (catheters, central lines), extremes of age, antibiotic exposure within 90 days.
  • C. difficile colitis: the most clinically significant superinfection.
    • Highest-risk agents: fluoroquinolones, clindamycin, broad-spectrum β-lactams
    • Prevention: restrict high-risk agents, probiotics in select patients.
    • Treatment (Table 36): oral vancomycin or fidaxomicin, IV vancomycin does not reach the lumen.
  • Fungal: oral/vaginal candidiasis, invasive candidiasis in immunocompromised, after broad-spectrum drugs (FQs, carbapenems, extended-spectrum penicillins)
    • Prevention: antifungal prophylaxis in high-risk patients.
  • Bacterial: MRSA, VRE, MDR Gram-negatives (ESBL, CRE). Prevention: stewardship, timely de-escalation.

Commonly Confused Concepts MUST KNOW
Penicillin vs vancomycin (target)
  • Penicillin: binds the enzyme (PBP/transpeptidase), mimics D-Ala-D-Ala.
  • Vancomycin: binds the substrate (D-Ala-D-Ala) → resistance by changing the substrate (D-Ala-D-Lac).
  • Fast: enzyme vs substrate.
Vancomycin vs daptomycin
  • Vancomycin: cell wall precursor, slower, MRSA pneumonia OK.
  • Daptomycin: membrane depolarization, rapidly cidal in blood, inactive in pneumonia (surfactant), CPK myopathy.
Tetracycline vs aminoglycoside (both 30S)
  • Tetracycline: reversible, A-site block, static, no oxygen requirement.
  • Aminoglycoside: irreversible, misreading, cidal, needs O₂/PMF.
Spectinomycin vs aminoglycosides
  • Structurally related, both 30S, but spectinomycin binds reversibly → static, no amino sugars, for resistant gonorrhea only.
Macrolide vs chloramphenicol vs linezolid (all 50S)
  • Macrolide/clinda: translocation, exit tunnel.
  • Chloramphenicol: peptidyl transferase, no peptide bond.
  • Linezolid: initiation complex.
Chloramphenicol marrow suppression vs aplastic anemia
  • Suppression: dose-dependent, reversible, mitochondrial.
  • Aplastic anemia: idiosyncratic, dose-independent, irreversible, nitroso group, delayed.
Gentamicin vs tobramycin vs amikacin
  • Gentamicin: synergy workhorse, vestibular toxicity.
  • Tobramycin: Pseudomonas, inhaled CF, cochlear.
  • Amikacin: resists modifying enzymes, MDR-TB, cochlear.
AG nephrotoxicity vs ototoxicity
  • Nephro: lysosomal, proximal tubule, reversible, first sign = rising trough.
  • Oto: hair cells, irreversible, A1555G, furosemide amplifies both.
Calcium gluconate vs neostigmine (AG NM blockade)
  • Calcium gluconate = first-line (restores Ca-dependent ACh release).
  • Neostigmine = second-line (↑ ACh at the junction).
Sulfonamide vs trimethoprim
  • Sulfa: PABA analog, DHPS, weak acid, crystalluria, SJS, G6PD, kernicterus.
  • TMP: folate analog, DHFR, weak base, prostate/vagina, megaloblastic anemia (leucovorin), hyperkalemia.
Folic acid vs folinic acid (leucovorin)
  • Rescue of TMP marrow toxicity uses folinic acid, which bacteria cannot absorb → host protected, bacteria still blocked.
DNA gyrase vs topoisomerase IV
  • Gyrase (Topo II): negative supercoils, replication fork, Gram-negative primary target, gyrA.
  • Topo IV: decatenation of daughter chromosomes, Gram-positive, parC.
Ciprofloxacin vs levofloxacin vs moxifloxacin
  • Cipro: Pseudomonas/Gram−, weak pneumococcus, UTI.
  • Levo: respiratory + Pseudomonas, renal, QTc.
  • Moxi: respiratory + anaerobes, hepatic, no UTI, no Pseudomonas, highest QTc.
Ceftriaxone vs ceftazidime vs cefepime vs ceftaroline
  • Ceftriaxone: meningitis/pneumococcus/gonorrhea, biliary, no Pseudomonas.
  • Ceftazidime: Pseudomonas, weaker G+, shares side chain with aztreonam.
  • Cefepime: Pseudomonas + MSSA, AmpC-stable, no MRSA.
  • Ceftaroline: MRSA, no Pseudomonas.
Imipenem vs meropenem vs ertapenem
  • Imipenem: needs cilastatin, seizures.
  • Meropenem: CNS use, Pseudomonas, no cilastatin.
  • Ertapenem: once daily, no Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter/Enterococcus.
Aztreonam allergy rule
  • No cross-allergy with penicillins/cephalosporins EXCEPT ceftazidime (same side chain).
Red man syndrome vs true allergy
  • Red man = rate-dependent, non-immunologic histamine release → slow infusion fixes it.
  • True allergy = immune-mediated, stop drug.
Oral vs IV vancomycin
  • Oral: not absorbed, only luminal C. difficile.
  • IV: systemic Gram+ infection, does not reach the gut lumen.
Clindamycin vs metronidazole (anaerobes)
  • Clindamycin: above the diaphragm, no CSF, C. diff black box.
  • Metronidazole: below the diaphragm, no aerobes, metallic taste, warfarin.
Erythromycin/clarithromycin vs azithromycin
  • Erythro/clarithro: 14-member ring, CYP3A4 inhibitors (statins, warfarin, theophylline).
  • Azithro: no CYP3A4 inhibition, 68 h half-life, 3-day course.
Doxycycline vs tigecycline
  • Doxy: versatile oral, fecal elimination, no renal adjustment.
  • Tigecycline: IV only, escapes efflux/Tet(M), low serum/urine levels, no Pseudomonas/Proteus, mortality warning.
Nafcillin/oxacillin/ceftriaxone vs other β-lactams (clearance)
  • These three are biliary → no renal dose adjustment. Most β-lactams are renal (probenecid interaction).
Time- vs concentration- vs exposure-dependent
  • β-lactams: %fT>MIC (frequent/prolonged infusion).
  • Aminoglycosides: Cmax/MIC (once daily, PAE).
  • Vanco, FQ, macrolides, tetracyclines: fAUC/MIC.
MIC vs MBC vs breakpoint
  • MIC: lowest conc. preventing visible growth. MBC: kills 99.9%. Breakpoint: threshold that classifies S/I/R. Lower MIC ≠ better drug.
Look-alike names
  • Cefoxitin (2nd, anaerobes) vs cefotaxime (3rd, meningitis) vs cefotetan (2nd, MTT ring).
  • Cefaclor is 2nd gen despite "FA"-like look. Cefuroxime is 2nd despite "-ime."
  • Clindamycin (lincosamide) vs clarithromycin (macrolide).
  • Sulfadiazine (short) vs sulfadoxine (7-day, malaria) vs sulfasalazine (ulcerative colitis).
  • Neomycin (topical, nephrotoxic) vs neostigmine (antidote).

7. Essential exceptions and traps (numbered)

  1. Aminoglycosides need oxygen: no anaerobes, no abscess, no pneumonia monotherapy, contraindicated in myasthenia gravis.
  2. Daptomycin never for pneumonia (surfactant). Linezolid works there.
  3. Moxifloxacin never for UTI (hepatic clearance) but covers anaerobes.
  4. Ertapenem: no Pseudomonas/Acinetobacter/Enterococcus. Imipenem: cilastatin + seizures.
  5. Aztreonam is allergy-safe except with ceftazidime.
  6. Biliary β-lactams needing no renal adjustment: nafcillin, oxacillin, ceftriaxone. Doxycycline fecal. Probenecid ↑ penicillins and FQs.
  7. Cations (antacids, Ca, Fe, dairy) chelate tetracyclines and FQs.
  8. Linezolid + SSRI → serotonin syndrome (MAOI). Clarithro/erythro + statin → rhabdo (azithro safe).
  9. Antidotes: AG NM blockade → calcium gluconate then neostigmine. TMP marrow → folinic acid. Anaphylaxis → epinephrine. Red man → slow infusion.
  10. FQ no longer for gonorrhea (gyrA). Ceftriaxone is.
  11. Spectinomycin looks like an AG but is static. Chloramphenicol is static except vs H. flu/meningococcus/pneumococcus.
  12. Lower MIC ≠ better drug. Compare with breakpoint, exposure, site.
