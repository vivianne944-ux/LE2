1. NMB Foundations and Normal NMJ Transmission

MUST KNOW

Two groups of skeletal muscle relaxants Page 1

  • Neuromuscular blockers → produce muscle paralysis for surgery, intubation, and ICU use.
  • Spasmolytics and antispasmodics → reduce spasticity or painful muscle spasm.

Muscle nicotinic receptor Pages 1–2

  • Adult muscle nACh receptor has 5 subunits → 2α, β, γ, δ as stated in the lecture.
  • Two ACh binding sites are described at the α–β and δ–α interfaces.
  • Binding at one site increases affinity for the second ACh molecule, then the channel opens.
  • Presynaptic motor axon terminal nicotinic receptors are described as promoting additional ACh transmitter release.
  • Extrajunctional receptors proliferate with prolonged immobilization, paralysis, stroke, bedridden states, and thermal burns.

Shared chemistry of NMBs Page 2

  • They resemble acetylcholine. Succinylcholine is described as two ACh-like molecules joined together.
  • Presence of 1 or 2 quaternary nitrogens → polar and poorly lipid soluble.
  • Consequences → poor GI absorption, parenteral use, limited CNS entry, limited volume of distribution around 80–140 mL/kg.

Their quaternary nitrogens make them highly polar and poorly lipid soluble, causing poor GI absorption and limited entry into the CNS. Page 2

The lecture states that two ACh molecules bind, with the first binding increasing affinity for the second. Page 2

2. Depolarizing NMB — Succinylcholine

MUST KNOW

Core PK Page 3

  • Only clinically available depolarizing NMB in the lecture.
  • Route → IV or IM.
  • Rapid plasma hydrolysis by pseudocholinesterase to succinylmonocholine, an active metabolite.
  • Butyrylcholinesterase in the liver can also metabolize succinylcholine.
  • Further degradation → succinic acid + choline.
  • Metabolites are excreted in urine.

Time profile Page 3

  • Onset 60–90 seconds.
  • Duration 5–10 minutes, shortest of the NMBs discussed.
  • Termination depends on degradation plus diffusion away from the motor end plate.

Phase I block — depolarizing block Page 3

→
Depolarization

→
Membrane stays depolarized
→

→

Why it lasts longer than ACh at the receptor: the lecture states that succinylcholine is hydrolyzed more slowly than acetylcholine.

Phase II block — desensitizing block Page 3

  • Not usually seen.
  • Occurs with continuous exposure, high doses, or prolonged infusion.
  • Membrane repolarizes but becomes desensitized to incoming impulses.
  • Later behaves like a nondepolarizing block.

Prolonged block from low pseudocholinesterase Page 3

  • Pregnancy, burns, oral contraceptives, MAOI.
  • Liver disease and neoplastic disease.
  • Anticholinesterases, echothiopate, cytotoxic drugs, tetrahydroaminacrine, hexafluorenium.
  • Neostigmine inhibits pseudocholinesterase and prolongs succinylcholine.

Genetic atypical pseudocholinesterase Page 3

  • Dibucaine inhibits the normal enzyme much more than the abnormal form.
  • Dibucaine number = percent enzyme inhibited.
  • Lower number → more atypical enzyme → longer paralysis.
  • Mechanical ventilation may be required while paralysis persists.

High yield: the lecture states that intermediate agents are the most commonly used in surgery.

Pharmacokinetics and metabolism Pages 4–5

  • Highly polar due to quaternary nitrogens → parenteral use, poor CNS entry, limited distribution.
  • Rapid initial distribution phase followed by a slower elimination phase.
  • Steroidal derivatives → hepatic metabolism. Liver pathology can prolong action.
  • 3-hydroxy metabolite remains active at about 40–80% potency of the parent drug and can contribute to prolonged paralysis at high concentrations.
  • Atracurium and cisatracurium → liver metabolism plus spontaneous plasma Hofmann elimination.
  • Cisatracurium is less dependent on hepatic metabolism, produces less laudanosine, and releases less histamine than atracurium according to the lecture.
  • Laudanosine can cross the BBB, has a long half-life around 150 min, and at high accumulated doses may cause CNS excitation, seizures, or increased anesthetic requirements.
  • NICE TO KNOW Gantacurium is described as experimental and not clinically available. Its metabolism is nonenzymatic, and the lecture notes histamine-related hypotension at ED95. Page 5

4. Clinical Use, TOF Monitoring, and Reversal

MUST KNOW

Clinical uses of NMBs Page 6

  • Tracheal intubation.
  • Intraoperative skeletal muscle relaxation to expose the operative field.
  • ICU use to control mechanical ventilation.
  • NMBs do not provide central activity or awareness control because of poor CNS entry. The lecture’s COVID-19 section separately stresses deep sleep before deep neuromuscular blockade.

COVID-19 intubation protocol points in the lecture Pages 3 and 6

  • Main goal → prevent aerosolization.
  • Patient should be in deep sleep and receive deep and rapid neuromuscular blockade.
  • The lecture recommends the most experienced intubator and specialized equipment such as video laryngoscopy.
  • Avoid high-flow O₂, unnecessary personnel, and attempting intubation when the patient is not adequately asleep or paralyzed.
  • For rapid intubation, the lecture says either a depolarizing or nondepolarizing NMB can be used depending on the patient and pharmacodynamic considerations.
  • For intraoperative abdominal relaxation, the lecture favors an intermediate-duration nondepolarizing NMB because surgery lasts longer than succinylcholine's 5–10 minute action.

Train-of-four logic Page 7

  • Gold-standard assessment described in the lecture → ulnar nerve stimulation with adductor pollicis contraction.
  • Other nerves named in the lecture → posterior tibial nerve and facial nerve.
  • Four maximal stimulations are delivered.
  • No block → 4 equal twitches.
  • Nondepolarizing block → fade, with progressively smaller twitches.
  • Depolarizing block → equal diminution of amplitudes rather than fade.
  • TOF count can fall 4 → 3 → 1 → 0 as block deepens.
  • The lecture states that TOFR 0 marks complete paralysis and a suitable time for intubation.
  • Recovery requires TOFR >90%.

5. Spasmolytics

MUST KNOW
Do not confuse: spasmolytic vs antispasmodic
  • Spasmolytic → spasticity from UMN lesions, involuntary skeletal muscle contraction, α-motor neuron hyperactivity.
  • Antispasmodic → acute peripheral musculoskeletal spasm such as whiplash or muscle strain.
  • Fast distinction → UMN spasticity = spasmolytic, local peripheral spasm = antispasmodic.
Page 8

General characteristics Page 8

  • Spasmolytics can relieve painful muscle spasms, but the lecture says they may produce little improvement in function such as moving about or working.
  • Central adverse effects such as sedation and dizziness can themselves limit function.
  • Centrally acting examples → diazepam, baclofen, tizanidine.
  • Peripherally acting examples listed → dantrolene sodium, eperisone HCl, pherinamidol.

Main spasmolytics Page 8

DrugSite or receptorKey lecture pointImportant adverse effects
DiazepamGABAA receptor componentsSedative-hypnotic depending on dose. Dependence, withdrawal, tolerance can develop with prolonged use.Drowsiness, dizziness, weakness, mental confusion.
BaclofenGABAB agonistPresynaptic inhibition → decreased release of excitatory neurotransmitter. Less sedation than diazepam.Lecture emphasizes less sedation as an advantage.
Tizanidineα2-adrenoceptor agonistCongener of clonidine. Presynaptic α₂ action is emphasized.Drowsiness, hypotension, dry mouth, asthenia.
DantrolenePeripheral skeletal muscle, RYR1 pathwayInhibits Ca²⁺ release from SR and prevents excitation–contraction coupling.Muscle weakness, sedation, occasional hepatitis.

Spinal cord and muscle targets Page 8

  • Tizanidine → α₂ presynaptic receptors, with a possible postsynaptic inhibitory effect.
  • Baclofen → GABAB receptors pre and postsynaptically.
  • Benzodiazepines such as diazepam → GABAA at the postsynaptic site.
  • Dantrolene → peripheral inhibition of calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  • SHOULD KNOW The lecture states skeletal muscle uses RYR1, while cardiac muscle contains the RYR2 isoform and is only slightly depressed.

Other centrally acting spasmolytics Page 8

  • Gabapentin → antiseizure drug with voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel antagonism, spasmolytic effect especially in multiple sclerosis.
  • Pregabalin → analog of gabapentin, also described as a voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channel antagonist.
  • Progabide → GABAA and GABAB agonist.
  • Glycine → inhibitory amino acid neurotransmitter with more spinal cord action.
  • Idrocilamide and riluzole → newer ALS drugs in the lecture, linked to inhibition of glutamatergic transmission.

6. Antispasmodics

MUST KNOW

General use Page 9

  • Treatment of acute local muscle spasm due to tissue trauma or muscle strain such as whiplash.
  • Lecture describes these agents as centrally acting at the brainstem.
  • NICE TO KNOW The lecture states that these drugs were not listed in the Philippine National Formulary, 2017.
  • Examples → cyclobenzaprine, carisoprodol, chlorphenesin, chlorzoxazone, metaxalone, orphenadrine, methocarbamol.

Cyclobenzaprine — prototype Page 9

  • Structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants.
  • Antimuscarinic adverse effects.
  • Sedation, transient visual hallucinations, confusion.
  • Not given for UMN conditions such as spinal cord injury or cerebral palsy.
  • Abuse potential is present.

Orphenadrine Page 9

  • Oral muscle relaxant.
  • In the Philippines, combined with paracetamol as Norgesic in the lecture.
  • Adverse effects → drowsiness, dizziness, blurred vision, dry mouth, nausea, vomiting, constipation, tachycardia, palpitations.
EXAM TRAP Cyclobenzaprine is not the UMN spasticity drug. It is the prototype antispasmodic for acute local muscle spasm. Page 9

Cyclobenzaprine and serotonin syndrome Page 9

  • Lecture lists agitation, sweating, tremors, and in severe cases seizures, hyperthermia, coma.
  • Concomitant drugs listed → SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, tramadol, bupropion, meperidine, verapamil, MAO inhibitors.
Recall check: Cerebral palsy spasticity or acute whiplash spasm?
Cerebral palsy spasticity → spasmolytic. Acute whiplash or local muscle strain → antispasmodic. Pages 8–9

Commonly Confused Concepts

MUST KNOW
Depolarizing vs nondepolarizing
Depolarizing → succinylcholine, persistent depolarization, fasciculations, equal TOF diminution.
Nondepolarizing → competitive antagonism, TOF fade.
Neostigmine effect
Succinylcholine → can prolong block by inhibiting pseudocholinesterase.
NdNMB → helps reverse block by increasing ACh.
MG vs burns or UMN disease
MG → more sensitive to NdNMB, less sensitive to depolarizing NMB.
Burns or UMN disease → resistant to NdNMB, avoid depolarizing NMB.
Atracurium vs cisatracurium
Both can undergo Hofmann elimination. Cisatracurium is presented as producing less laudanosine and less histamine.
Baclofen vs diazepam
Baclofen → GABAB. Diazepam → GABAA.
Spasmolytic vs antispasmodic
UMN spasticity → spasmolytic. Peripheral acute local spasm → antispasmodic.

Exam Traps

EXAM TRAP
  • Fasciculations occur early in Phase I succinylcholine block, not only late. Pages 3, 10
  • Phase I succinylcholine block is not reversed by AChE inhibitors in the lecture’s review key. Page 10
  • Succinylcholine raises intragastric pressure, which can increase aspiration risk. Pages 4, 10
  • Pancuronium may cause tachycardia and hypertension due to vagolytic action plus increased sympathetic activity. Pages 5, 10
  • Tizanidine is related to clonidine and can cause hypotension through α₂-adrenoceptor effects. Pages 8, 10
  • Dantrolene targets SR calcium release and is the emergency drug of choice for malignant hyperthermia. Pages 8–10
  • Baclofen is GABAB. In the review key, GABAB receptors are linked to K⁺ channels, while GABAA receptors modulate chloride channels. Page 10
  • Diabetes is mentioned in the review key as a setting where delayed gastric emptying can make succinylcholine-related regurgitation and aspiration more likely. Page 10
  • Diazepam is highlighted in the review key as both a spasmolytic and an antiseizure drug, including for seizures from local-anesthetic overdose. Page 10
  • A small nondepolarizing dose such as atracurium can be given before succinylcholine to reduce fasciculations and postoperative myalgia. Pages 4, 10

Last-Minute 5-Minute Cram Sheet

MUST KNOW

Core distinctions

  • Depolarizing → succinylcholine, depolarizes, fasciculates, no fade.
  • Nondepolarizing → competitive nicotinic antagonists, produces fade.
  • Spasmolytic → UMN spasticity.
  • Antispasmodic → acute local peripheral muscle spasm.

Numbers worth memorizing

  • Succinylcholine onset 60–90 s.
  • Duration 5–10 min.
  • Dibucaine number 70–80 normal, 50–60 heterozygous, 20–30 homozygous atypical.
  • Homozygous atypical block 4–8 h.
  • Intermediate NdNMB duration 20–35 min.
  • Recovery TOFR >90%.

High-risk associations

  • Succinylcholine → hyperkalemia in burns, trauma, nerve injury, neuromuscular disease, renal failure, metabolic acidosis.
  • Succinylcholine → increased IOP, increased intragastric pressure, postoperative myalgia, malignant hyperthermia.
  • Pancuronium → tachycardia and hypertension.
  • Atracurium and tubocurarine → hypotension from histamine release.

Reversal and monitoring

  • NdNMB reversal → neostigmine or pyridostigmine increases ACh.
  • Add antimuscarinic to counter ACh muscarinic effects.
  • Sugammadex → steroidal NMBs, tight 1:1 plasma binding.
  • TOF fade = nondepolarizing block.

Spasmolytic map

  • Diazepam → GABAA.
  • Baclofen → GABAB.
  • Tizanidine → α₂.
  • Dantrolene → SR Ca²⁺ release, RYR1, malignant hyperthermia.
  • Botulinum toxin → blocks ACh vesicle release.

Classic exam traps

  • Neostigmine can prolong succinylcholine.
  • MG → more sensitive to NdNMB, less sensitive to depolarizing NMB.
  • Burns or UMN disease → resistant to NdNMB and avoid depolarizing NMB.
  • Cyclobenzaprine is an antispasmodic, not the UMN-spasticity prototype.
