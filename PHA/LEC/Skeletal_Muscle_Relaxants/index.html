Neuromuscular Junction Foundations

Cholinergic transmission, normal function, receptors, and drug structure.

Cholinergic transmission

The somatic motor fiber uses acetylcholine. The skeletal muscle receptor is the muscle-type nicotinic receptor, a ligand-gated ion channel.

  • Preganglionic autonomic fibers use neuronal nicotinic receptors.
  • Somatic motor fibers to skeletal muscle use muscle-type nicotinic receptors.
  • The adult muscle receptor is described as a pentamer with 2α, β, γ, and δ subunits in the lecture.

Normal neuromuscular function

  1. Nerve action potential reaches the motor nerve terminal.
  2. Ca²⁺ enters the nerve terminal.
  3. ACh vesicles fuse with the membrane and release ACh.
  4. ACh binds nicotinic receptors at the motor end plate.
  5. The channel opens and Na⁺ and K⁺ move across it.
  6. The end plate depolarizes and a motor action potential develops.
  7. Muscle contraction follows.
  8. ACh is degraded by acetylcholinesterase into choline and acetate.

Nicotinic receptor activation

The lecture shows two ACh binding sites. Binding at one site increases affinity for another ACh molecule. Occupation of both sites is followed by channel opening and Na⁺ influx.

  • Presynaptic motor axon terminal receptors support additional ACh release.
  • Extrajunctional receptors are not usually involved in normal transmission. They proliferate with prolonged immobilization, paralysis after stroke, bedridden states, and thermal burns.

Shared structural pharmacology of NMBs

  • Neuromuscular blockers resemble acetylcholine.
  • Succinylcholine resembles two ACh molecules joined together.
  • One or two quaternary nitrogens make these drugs highly polar and poorly lipid soluble.
  • They are poorly absorbed from the GIT, so preparations are parenteral.
  • Polarity limits entry into the CNS and contributes to a limited volume of distribution of 80–140 mL/kg.
Clinical CueNMBs produce paralysis but have poor CNS access because of their polarity.
Clinical use includes intubation, operative muscle relaxation, and ICU ventilator control.

Depolarizing Neuromuscular Blocker

Succinylcholine pharmacokinetics, block phases, adverse effects, and interactions.

Must KnowSuccinylcholine or suxamethonium is the only clinically available depolarizing NMB in the lecture.
Onset is 60–90 seconds.
Duration is 5–10 minutes, the shortest among the neuromuscular blockers discussed.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Route: IV or IM.
  • After IV administration, the lecture notes 100% plasma bioavailability.
  • Rapid plasma hydrolysis occurs before much drug reaches the NMJ.
  • Pseudocholinesterase converts succinylcholine to succinylmonocholine, an active metabolite.
  • Butyrylcholinesterase in the liver can also metabolize succinylcholine.
  • Further degradation produces succinic acid and choline.
  • Metabolites are excreted in the urine.

Prolonged succinylcholine block

Decreased pseudocholinesterase level

  • Pregnancy, burns, oral contraceptives, MAOI.
  • Liver disease and neoplastic diseases.
  • Anticholinesterases, echothiopate, cytotoxic drugs, tetrahydroaminacrine, hexafluorenium.

Genetic variant and dibucaine number

  • Normal or homozygous typical: dibucaine number 70–80, normal duration.
  • Heterozygous atypical: dibucaine number 50–60, block increased by about 50–100%.
  • Homozygous atypical: dibucaine number 20–30, block may last 4–8 hours.
Exam TrapNeostigmine prolongs succinylcholine block because cholinesterase inhibition also inhibits pseudocholinesterase.
This differs from its use to reverse nondepolarizing blockade.

Phase I block: depolarizing block

Succinylcholine binds nAChR
Depolarization
Fasciculations
Flaccid paralysis
  • Succinylcholine is hydrolyzed more slowly than ACh and remains bound longer.
  • The membrane remains depolarized and unresponsive to incoming impulses.
  • There is no effective repolarization and repetitive firing.
  • Excitation–contraction coupling cannot proceed normally, so muscle remains paralyzed until the drug effect terminates.

Phase II block: desensitizing block

  • Not usually seen.
  • Occurs with continuous end-plate exposure, high doses, or prolonged infusion.
  • The membrane first depolarizes, then repolarizes, but becomes desensitized to incoming impulses.
  • The muscle remains paralyzed.
  • Later behavior resembles a nondepolarizing block.
Do Not ConfusePhase I is the usual succinylcholine block with persistent depolarization.
Phase II follows prolonged exposure and behaves more like a nondepolarizing block.

Cardiovascular and electrolyte effects

  • Cardiac arrhythmias, especially with halothane.
  • Sinus bradycardia and nodal rhythm.
  • Ventricular arrhythmias.
  • Hyperkalemia due to transient rise in serum K⁺.
WarningSevere potassium rise can cause arrhythmia or cardiac arrest in patients with burns, trauma, nerve damage, neuromuscular disease, renal failure, or metabolic acidosis.

Other effects and interactions

  • Increased intraocular pressure begins in less than 60 sec, lasts 2–4 min, then declines by about 5 min. The lecture advises avoiding succinylcholine in open eye injury or increased intraocular pressure.
  • Increased intragastric pressure from abdominal fasciculations can promote regurgitation and aspiration.
  • Postoperative muscle pain can follow fasciculations.
  • Malignant hyperthermia may occur in susceptible patients.
  • A small dose of a nondepolarizing blocker before succinylcholine can reduce fasciculations.
  • The lecture notes that combining a nondepolarizing blocker with succinylcholine increases the succinylcholine dose requirement.

Absorption and distribution

  • Highly polar due to one or two quaternary nitrogens.
  • Inactive orally and supplied as parenteral preparations.
  • Do not cross membranes readily and have poor CNS entry.
  • Limited volume of distribution.
  • Rapid initial distribution phase followed by a slower elimination phase.

Metabolism by structural class

  • Steroidal agents undergo hepatic metabolism.
  • Liver pathology can prolong their duration of action.
  • The 3-hydroxymetabolite is active with 40–80% of parent potency and can contribute to prolonged paralysis at high concentrations.
  • Atracurium and cisatracurium can undergo hepatic metabolism and spontaneous plasma Hofmann elimination.

Hofmann elimination and laudanosine

  • Hofmann elimination is spontaneous degradation in plasma.
  • This makes atracurium and cisatracurium useful in the lecture context of hepatic or kidney pathology because elimination can still occur in plasma.
  • Cisatracurium is less dependent on hepatic metabolism and produces less laudanosine with less histamine release than atracurium.
  • Laudanosine has a long half-life of about 150 min, crosses the BBB, and can accumulate during prolonged atracurium infusion.
  • High laudanosine levels can produce excitatory CNS effects, including seizures and increased anesthetic requirements.

Experimental agent

Gantacurium is described as an experimental, not yet clinically available NMB.

  • Metabolism is nonenzymatic.
  • Histamine release may cause hypotension at ED95.

Nondepolarizing Pharmacodynamics

Mechanism, cardiovascular effects, interactions, and disease or age modifiers.

Mechanism of action

Nondepolarizing blockers use competitive antagonism against acetylcholine at the nicotinic receptor.

  • Occupation of even one binding site by the blocker is enough to prevent channel opening.
  • ACh in the cleft is rapidly degraded by acetylcholinesterase.
  • Large doses can also block channel pores and prejunctional Na⁺ channels, which interferes with ACh release.
  • The main mechanism remains competitive antagonism at the nicotinic receptor.

Cardiovascular effects

  • Minimal CVS effects: cisatracurium, rocuronium, vecuronium.
  • Hypotension from histamine release: especially atracurium and tubocurarine.
  • Ganglionic blockade: tubocurarine and metocurine at large doses.
  • Increased HR and CO, with or without increased SVR: pancuronium due to vagolytic action, NE release, and blockade of NE uptake.
  • Histamine release order in the lecture: D-tubocurarine > metocurine > atracurium or mivacurium > cisatracurium.
  • Steroidal NMBs are noted not to cause histamine release.

Drugs that enhance nondepolarizing blockade

Myasthenia gravis

Must Know · Red-exclamation lecture pointMyasthenia gravis has fewer functional nicotinic ACh receptors because of antibodies against the receptor.
  • Less susceptible to depolarizing blockers because there are fewer receptors on which succinylcholine can act.
  • More susceptible to nondepolarizing blockers because fewer remaining receptors need to be competitively blocked.

Severe burns and upper motor neuron disease

Must Know · Red-exclamation lecture pointPatients with burns and UMN disease such as stroke are not to be given depolarizing NMBs in the lecture.
  • They develop extrajunctional receptor proliferation.
  • They appear resistant to nondepolarizing blockers.
  • A higher nondepolarizing dose may be required because more nicotinic receptors must be occupied.
Must Know · Elderly >70 yearsDecreased liver and kidney clearance can prolong the action of nondepolarizing blockers.
The lecture states that lower doses are needed.

Clinical Use, Monitoring, and Reversal

Intubation, intraoperative use, Train of Four, cholinesterase inhibitors, and sugammadex.

Clinical uses of NMBs

  • Produce muscle paralysis for tracheal intubation.
  • Provide intraoperative skeletal muscle relaxation to expose the operative field.
  • Support ICU management by controlling mechanical ventilation.
Clinical PearlFor rapid intubation, the lecture allows either a depolarizing or nondepolarizing blocker depending on patient factors.
For intraoperative abdominal relaxation, an intermediate-duration nondepolarizing blocker is ideal because surgery usually lasts longer than succinylcholine's 5–10 min effect.

COVID-19 intubation lecture points

  • Prevent aerosolization.
  • Ensure deep sleep.
  • Provide deep and rapid neuromuscular blockade.
  • Use the most experienced intubator.
  • Use specialized equipment such as video laryngoscopy.
  • Avoid high-flow O₂, unnecessary personnel, and inadequate sleep or paralysis.

Assessment of blockade

  • Monitor before intubation, during surgery, and after surgery to document recovery.
  • The nerve stimulator delivers transdermal electrical stimulation to a superficial nerve.
  • The lecture calls ulnar nerve–adductor pollicis stimulation the gold standard.
  • Posterior tibial and facial nerves can also be used.

Train of Four interpretation

  • Four maximal electrical stimuli are applied and thumb contractions are observed.
  • Without NMB: four equal twitches.
  • After a nondepolarizing blocker: fade develops from the first to the fourth twitch.
  • Depolarizing blockade produces equal diminution rather than fade.
  • As blockade deepens, TOF count falls from 4 to 3 to 1 to 0.
  • TOFR 0 corresponds to complete paralysis in the lecture's intubation example.
  • Recovery requires TOFR > 0.90.

Reversal with cholinesterase inhibitors

  • Cholinesterase inhibitors decrease ACh degradation.
  • More ACh is available to compete with the nondepolarizing blocker.
  • Neostigmine or pyridostigmine may be used. Neostigmine is the IV preparation in the lecture.
  • These agents also increase ACh at muscarinic sites, producing effects such as bradycardia and increased GIT secretions.
  • An antimuscarinic is therefore paired with the cholinesterase inhibitor.
  • Edrophonium is short-acting and is described as being used for diagnosis of myasthenia gravis rather than reversal.

Sugammadex

  • Used for steroidal nondepolarizing blockers, including rocuronium, vecuronium, and pancuronium.
  • The lecture states an onset about 18× faster than neostigmine.
  • Forms a 1:1 tight complex with rocuronium in plasma.
  • Lower plasma rocuronium creates a concentration gradient that pulls rocuronium from the NMJ back into plasma.
  • The complex is excreted in urine within 24 h with normal kidney function.
Must Know · Severe renal impairmentExcretion of the sugammadex–rocuronium complex is impaired, so the complex remains longer in plasma.
Adverse effects and interactionsHypersensitivity symptoms include nausea, pruritus, and urticaria.
Anaphylaxis can occur.
Rare bradycardia leading to cardiac arrest and coagulopathy are noted.
Sugammadex may bind progesterone-based contraceptives. The lecture advises alternative non-progesterone contraception for 7 days after administration.
Toremifene can interact because of its steroidal structure and may displace rocuronium from the complex.

Spasmolytics

UMN-related spasticity and major centrally or peripherally acting agents.

Must Know · Core distinctionSpasmolytics treat spasticity from UMN lesions, involuntary skeletal muscle contractions, and hyperactivity of α-motor neurons.
Antispasmodics are used for peripheral musculoskeletal conditions such as whiplash.
Centrally acting spasmolytics
  • Diazepam
  • Baclofen
  • Tizanidine
Peripherally acting spasmolytics
  • Dantrolene sodium
  • Eperisone HCl
  • Pherinamidol

Diazepam

  • Binds components of GABAA receptors.
  • Sedative-hypnotic effects depend on dose.
  • Adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, weakness, mental confusion.
  • Prolonged use can produce dependence and tolerance.
  • Abrupt discontinuation can produce withdrawal with agitation, nausea, vomiting, or convulsions.
  • Available orally and by IV or IM routes.

Baclofen

  • Agonist at GABAB receptors.
  • Produces presynaptic inhibition and decreases excitatory neurotransmitter release.
  • Main lecture advantage: less sedation than diazepam.

Tizanidine

  • Congener of clonidine.
  • Significant α₂-adrenoceptor agonist effects.
  • Adverse effects: drowsiness, hypotension, dry mouth, asthenia.
  • Available as oral tablets.

Dantrolene

  • Peripherally acting.
  • Directly inhibits Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
  • Prevents excitation–contraction coupling.
  • May bind the skeletal muscle RYR1 channel.
  • Adverse effects: muscle weakness, sedation, occasional hepatitis.
  • Drug of choice for malignant hyperthermia.

Other centrally acting spasmolytic drugs

Antispasmodics and Special Topics

Malignant hyperthermia, botulinum toxin, cyclobenzaprine, and orphenadrine.

Malignant hyperthermia

  • Rare familial or genetic condition with an apparently normal patient before exposure.
  • Triggers in the lecture include succinylcholine and inhalational anesthetics.
  • Uncontrolled Ca²⁺ release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum drives continuous contraction and a hypermetabolic state.
  • Findings include increased CO₂ and pCO₂, acidosis, arrhythmias, and later hyperthermia.
  • The lecture identifies a defect at chromosome 19q13.1 involving RYR1 mutations.
  • Dantrolene is the drug of choice.

Botulinum toxin

  • Prevents release of ACh from cholinergic vesicles.
  • Interferes with vesicle fusion by proteolytically cleaving SNAP proteins.
  • Given by local injection for spastic disorders, with benefits lasting weeks to months.
  • Type A botulinum toxin is used in the lecture.
  • Local facial injections are also described for short-term treatment of wrinkles.

Antispasmodics: general features

These drugs are used for acute local muscle spasm due to tissue trauma or muscle strain, such as whiplash. The lecture describes them as centrally acting at the brainstem.

Examples Cyclobenzaprine, carisoprodol, chlorphenesin, chlorzoxazone, metaxalone, orphenadrine, methocarbamol.

Cyclobenzaprine

PrototypeCyclobenzaprine is the prototypical antispasmodic in the lecture.
  • Structurally related to tricyclic antidepressants.
  • Has antimuscarinic side effects.
  • Can cause sedation, transient visual hallucinations, and confusion.
  • Not given for UMN conditions such as spinal cord injury or cerebral palsy.
  • Has abuse potential.
Serotonin syndrome interactionCan occur with serotonin-related drugs.
Manifestations listed include agitation, sweating, tremors, seizures, hyperthermia, and coma in severe cases.
Associated drugs in the lecture include SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, tramadol, bupropion, meperidine, verapamil, and MAO inhibitors.

Orphenadrine

  • Oral muscle relaxant.
  • In the Philippines, described in combination with paracetamol as Norgesic.
  • Elsewhere it may be combined with aspirin.
  • Adverse effects: drowsiness, dizziness, blurred vision, dry mouth, nausea, vomiting, constipation, tachycardia, palpitations.
Clinical PearlSpastic cerebral palsy → think spasmolytic, given orally in the lecture example.
Acute severe muscle spasm after a fist fight → think antispasmodic.

Cram Sheet

High-yield lecture anchors only.

Neuromuscular transmission

  • ACh is the somatic motor neurotransmitter.
  • Muscle receptor = nicotinic ligand-gated ion channel.
  • Two ACh molecules bind → channel opens → Na⁺ entry → depolarization → contraction.
  • ACh is degraded by acetylcholinesterase.
  • Extrajunctional receptors proliferate with prolonged immobilization, stroke-related paralysis, bedridden states, and burns.

Succinylcholine

  • Only clinically available depolarizing NMB in the lecture.
  • Onset 60–90 sec.
  • Duration 5–10 min.
  • Phase I: depolarization → fasciculations → flaccid paralysis.
  • Phase II: prolonged exposure → repolarized but desensitized membrane.
  • Neostigmine prolongs succinylcholine block.

Succinylcholine warnings
