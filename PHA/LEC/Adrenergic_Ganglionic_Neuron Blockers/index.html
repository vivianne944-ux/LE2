Common Side Effects That Interfere With Sympathetic Neuron Function

1. Postural or orthostatic hypotension p. 1

  • Guanethidine is specifically listed.
  • Ganglionic blockers cause the most profound degree of orthostatic hypotension.
  • Blocking adrenergic neurons and autonomic ganglia produces sympathetic depression.
  • α1 blockade in vascular smooth muscle → vasodilation → ↓ PVR → enhanced blood flow → hypotension.
  • When the patient stands, loss of the vasoconstrictor reflex allows peripheral pooling of blood.

2. Sedation or CNS depression p. 1

  • Produced by activation of centrally acting α2 receptor agonists, with clonidine as the example.
  • Reserpine is listed as an adrenergic neuron blocker that can cause sedation.
  • Phenoxybenzamine is listed as a nonselective α blocker that can cause sedation.
  • The lecturer notes that some β blockers, including propranolol, may also produce sedation.

3. Increased GI motility and diarrhea p. 1

  • Results from sympathetic blockade.
  • In structures innervated by both autonomic divisions, the lecture explains this as parasympathetic dominance.

4. Sexual dysfunction and inhibition of ejaculation p. 1

  • May appear as delayed ejaculation or retrograde ejaculation.
  • Attributed to α1 receptor blockade.
  • Lecture examples: nonselective α blockers, guanethidine, and ganglionic blockers.

Centrally Acting α2 Receptor Agonists: Receptor Location, Actions, Uses

Adrenergic nerve endings p. 2

  • Presynaptic α2 receptors: Regulate or modulate neurotransmitter release from peripheral postganglionic sympathetic nerves.
  • Postsynaptic α2B receptors on vascular smooth muscle: Activation → vasoconstriction.
Lecturer distinction on vascular α receptorsVascular smooth muscle contains both α1 and α2 receptors.α1 receptors lie close to sites of transmitter release and are mainly responsible for neurally mediated vasoconstriction.α2 receptors lie elsewhere on the muscle-fiber surface and are activated by circulating catecholamines.

Centrally located α2 receptors p. 2

  • Brainstem and medulla, presynaptic α2A: Activation → ↓ sympathetic outflow from the vasomotor center → ↓ BP.
  • This is why centrally activating presynaptic α2 receptors is useful for hypertension.
  • Medullary centers controlling salivation: Activation → dry mouth or xerostomia.
Must Know · lecture-markedCentrally acting selective α2 agonists bind more tightly to α2 than α1 adrenoceptors.

Clonidine

Chemistry and pharmacokinetics p. 2

  • Imidazoline derivative.
  • Lipid soluble.
  • Bioavailability: 95%.
  • Rapidly enters the CNS.
  • Peak concentration: 1 to 3 hours after an oral dose.
  • Elimination half-life: 8 to 12 hours.
  • Initially tested as a topical nasal decongestant. Hypotension, sedation, and bradycardia were observed, leading to its antihypertensive indication.

Administration and transdermal behavior p. 2

  • Oral administration.
  • Transdermal patch: Less sedation than clonidine tablets.
  • A single patch application can reduce BP for 7 days.
  • After patch removal, plasma concentration remains stable for about 8 hours.

Mechanism of action p. 2

  • Peripheral presynaptic α2 activation at adrenergic nerve terminals suppresses release of NE, ATP, and NPY from postganglionic sympathetic nerves.
  • May activate imidazoline I1 receptors.
  • Inhibits adenylyl cyclase → decreases cAMP.

CVS actions p. 2

  • ↓ HR and ↓ stroke volume, plus relaxation of capacitance vessels → ↓ CO and ↓ PVR → ↓ arterial BP.
  • The lecturer notes that clonidine lowers HR and CO more than methyldopa.
  • ↓ renal vascular resistance.
  • Like methyldopa, clonidine decreases BP in the supine position and rarely causes orthostatic hypotension.

The lecturer notes it with parenteral use.

Renal and ocular effects p. 2

  • Renin: May decrease renin release.
  • ↓ adenylyl cyclase → ↓ cAMP → ↓ stimulation of JG cells → ↓ renin release.
  • Eyes: ↓ aqueous humor production → ↓ intraocular pressure.
  • The lecture links the ocular effect to the use of Apraclonidine and Brimonidine for glaucoma.

Major adverse effects p. 2

  • Dry mouth or xerostomia and sedation or lethargy are the major adverse effects.
  • These effects are centrally mediated and dose dependent.
  • The lecture states that they occur in 50% of patients taking clonidine.
  • Lower occurrence with transdermal administration.
  • Delayed or retrograde ejaculation: Peripheral presynaptic α2 activation modulates NE release from sympathetic terminals.
  • Marked bradycardia: May occur because of increased parasympathetic tone.

Less common adverse effects and precautions p. 2

  • Sleep disturbance with vivid dreams and nightmares.
  • Restlessness.
  • Mental depression.
  • Contact dermatitis with transdermal use.
  • Avoid in patients at risk for mental depression.
  • TCAs block the antihypertensive effect of clonidine. The lecturer links this to blockade of neuronal NE uptake at sympathetic nerve terminals.

Withdrawal syndrome or rebound phenomenon pp. 2–3

Abrupt discontinuation after protracted high-dose therapy, especially >1 mg/day, can cause a hypertensive crisis.

Methyldopa

Chemistry, activation, and storage p. 3

  • Analog of L-DOPA.
  • Converted to α-methyldopamine and α-methylnorepinephrine.
  • These active metabolites act as false neurotransmitters in peripheral neurons.
  • Stored in adrenergic nerve vesicles and may replace NE.
  • Released by nerve stimulation.
  • Enters the brain through an aromatic amino transporter.

Pharmacokinetics p. 3

  • Bioavailability: 25%.
  • Half-life: about 2 hours.
  • Maximal effect: 4 to 6 hours after an oral dose.
  • The lecture notes that maximal effect may be delayed for 6 to 8 hours after an oral or IV dose.
  • Duration: Effect may persist up to 24 hours after the parent drug disappears from circulation.
  • Duration depends on the amount of stored metabolite in adrenergic vesicles of nerve endings.

Molecular signaling: inhibition of adenylyl cyclase → decreased cAMP production.

CVS effects p. 3

  • ↓ PVR with little change in CO and HR.
  • In patients with SA-node dysfunction, severe bradycardia may occur.
  • In elderly patients, CO may decrease because HR and stroke volume decrease after vascular and venous relaxation and reduced preload.
  • Renal vascular resistance may also decrease.

Renal effects and pseudo-tolerance p. 3

  • ↓ renin secretion from JG cells.
  • Prolonged use may cause salt and water retention.
  • The lecture calls this pseudo-tolerance and relates it to RAAS activation after BP decreases.
  • Concurrent diuretics may overcome this problem.

Adverse effects and precautions p. 3

  • Most common undesirable effect: Sedation at the onset of treatment.
  • Dry mouth through the medullary center that controls salivation.
  • Long-term treatment may cause persistent mental lassitude and impaired concentration.
  • Occasional mental depression, nightmares, vertigo, EPS, decreased libido, and lactation in both men and women.
Lecturer explanation: lactation and EPSLactation: Associated with increased prolactin secretion from inhibition of dopaminergic mechanisms in the hypothalamus.EPS: The lecturer links the extrapyramidal system to dopamine levels and notes that false transmitters such as α-methyldopamine and α-methylnorepinephrine can produce EPS.

Coombs test and hepatotoxicity p. 3

  • Positive Coombs test: May develop in 10% to 20% of patients taking methyldopa for longer than 12 months.
  • The lecture attributes this to autoantibodies against Rh antigen on erythrocytes.
  • Rarely associated with hemolytic anemia.
  • Hepatotoxicity may occur with drug fever.
  • Discontinuation leads to prompt reversal of these abnormalities.
Clinical CueMethyldopa is the preferred drug in this lecture for treatment of hypertension during pregnancy.The lecture bases this on effectiveness and safety in the mother and fetus.

Apraclonidine p. 3

  • Clonidine derivative, also called iodipine.
  • Does not cross the BBB.
  • Peak effect: 2 hours.
  • t½: 8 hours.
  • The lecture describes a hypotensive effect.

Mechanism and ocular actions

  • The exact mechanism is stated as unknown.
  • Related to α2-mediated ↓ aqueous humor secretion by the ciliary body.
  • Also linked to constriction of blood vessels of the afferent ciliary process and ↑ uveoscleral outflow.
  • Topical use decreases IOP with minimal systemic cardiovascular and pulmonary effects.

Therapeutic indications

  • Short-term adjunctive therapy in open-angle glaucoma when IOP is not controlled by other pharmacologic agents.
  • Additional drugs may be required to further reduce IOP.
  • Prevention or reduction of intraoperative and postoperative IOP increase before or after ocular laser trabeculoplasty or iridotomy.

Brimonidine p. 3

  • Not used as an antihypertensive agent.
  • Clonidine derivative.
  • Crosses the BBB, unlike apraclonidine.
  • Can cause hypotension and sedation, but these are milder than with clonidine.

Mechanism and ocular actions

  • ↓ aqueous humor production.
  • ↑ uveoscleral outflow.
  • Lecture notes a neuroprotective action that protects retinal ganglion cells and their projections independent of IOP effects.

Indications

  • Topical treatment to ↓ IOP in ocular hypertension or open-angle glaucoma.
  • May be combined with other topical drugs such as Betaxolol or Dorzolamide.

Rilmenidine and Moxonidine p. 4

  • Belong to the imidazolines group.
  • Therapeutically used as antihypertensive drugs in the lecture.
  • Profiles of action are similar to clonidine.
  • May activate imidazoline I1 receptors.

Tizanidine p. 4

  • Centrally acting muscle relaxant.
  • The lecture lists treatment for muscle spasticity.
  • The same lecture line also lists ocular hypertension and acute or chronic open- and closed-angle glaucoma.

α Adrenergic Receptor Antagonists: General Pharmacologic Considerations

What α1 blockade does to vessels p. 4

  • Inhibits vasoconstriction induced by endogenous catecholamines.
  • Produces vasodilation of arteriolar resistance vessels and veins.
  • Arteriolar and venous tone are determined to a large extent by α receptors on vascular smooth muscle.
  • The lecture identifies cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance as determinants of BP.
  • Some vessels with sympathetic innervation may have α1 receptors or both α1 and β2 receptors.

Other pharmacologic effects p. 4

  • Inhibits responses to serotonin or 5-HT.
  • The lecture lists stimulation of muscarinic, H1, and H2 receptors.
  • Facilitates insulin release by beta islet cells through α2 blockade.
  • Stimulates GI smooth-muscle contraction and gastric-acid secretion through α2 blockade.

Pharmacokinetics p. 4

  • Poor oral absorption.
  • Administered IV or IM.
  • Extensively metabolized in the liver.
  • Rapid onset and short duration of action.
  • t½ about 19 minutes after IV administration.

Adverse effects and toxicity p. 4

  • Major adverse effect: Orthostatic hypotension, which may be acute or prolonged.
  • Compensatory cardiac stimulation → tachycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, and ischemic cardiac events.
  • Tachycardia is driven by the baroreceptor reflex after ↓ BP and by α2 blockade → loss of negative feedback → ↑ NE → β1 stimulation.
  • GI stimulation: Nausea, abdominal pain, exacerbation of peptic ulcer.
  • The lecture links GI effects to histamine and muscarinic stimulation.
  • Atropine antagonizes only acetylcholine effects on muscarinic receptors.
  • Retrograde ejaculation.
  • Nasal congestion from parasympathetic dominance.

Phenoxybenzamine

Identity and mechanism pp. 4–5

  • Haloalkylamine and alkylating agent.
  • Related to nitrogen mustard.
  • Irreversible non-equilibrium α receptor blockade.
  • Covalently conjugates with α receptors.
  • Lecture affinity ratio: α1:α2 = 100:1, so α1 affinity is greater.
  • Inhibits neuronal and extraneuronal tissue uptake of NE.
  • The lecture attributes this to inhibition of the NE transporter or NET, decreasing NE clearance from sympathetic nerves.

Pharmacokinetics p. 4

  • Lipid soluble.
  • Absorbed after oral administration.
  • Bioavailability: about 20% to 30%.
  • Extensively metabolized in the liver.
  • Onset: 1 hour or more, slower than phentolamine.
  • Duration: 14 to 48 hours.
  • t½ about 24 hours.

CVS effects pp. 4–5

  • ↓ PVR → ↓ BP.
  • α1 blockade causes vasodilation because β2 vascular effects become unopposed.
  • The lecture describes a progressive fall in PVR followed by a marked fall in BP.
  • Falling BP produces reflex sympathetic stimulation with reflex tachycardia.
  • This is accentuated by enhanced NE release after α2 blockade → ↑ CO.
  • Epinephrine reversal can occur after α blockade.

Blood-volume compensation with long-term use p. 5

  • β2 vasodilating effects become more pronounced when α1 is blocked.
  • The lecture links the BP change to RAAS activation and a compensatory rise in blood volume and edema formation.
Lecture wording noteThe reviewer preserves the lecture’s RAAS compensation sequence without substituting outside corrections.

CNS and other effects p. 5

  • Slow IV administration: Sedation, fatigue, nausea.
  • Rapid IV administration: CNS excitation and increased motor activity.
  • At higher doses, irreversibly inhibits responses to serotonin, acetylcholine, and histamine.

Adverse effects and toxicity p. 5

  • Major adverse effect: Postural hypotension, often with reflex increase in HR and other arrhythmias.
  • Nasal stuffiness or congestion due to parasympathetic predominance.
  • Ejaculatory dysfunction in the form of retrograde ejaculation.
  • The lecture notes that phenoxybenzamine is mutagenic in the Ames test in experimental animal studies.

Therapeutic Uses of Classical α Receptor Blockers

Pheochromocytoma: major use p. 5

  • Pheochromocytoma is identified as the major use of classical or conventional α blockers.
  • Before surgical removal, the patient may receive Phenoxybenzamine for 1 to 3 weeks.
  • Other α1 blockers such as prazosin or doxazosin and β blockers such as propranolol, nadolol, atenolol, or metoprolol may be given several days after α blockade is started.
Must Know · sequence in pheochromocytomaDo not give β blockers initially or alone.Give α blockers first before β blockers.

Operative manipulation p. 5

  • BP may rise during surgery because of NE release.
  • Phentolamine may be administered for short-term BP control because it is readily titrated and short acting.
  • The lecture states that sodium nitroprusside is preferred nowadays.
  • Phentolamine is described as rarely used now for pheochromocytoma and other hypertensive emergencies.

Chronic inoperable or metastatic pheochromocytoma p. 5

  • Described as a rare occurrence.
  • Phenoxybenzamine may be given.
  • Reversible selective α1 blockers and calcium-channel blockers may also be used.
  • β blockers only after adequate α blockade to prevent the rebound phenomenon.
  • Metyrosine may also be used. It competitively inhibits tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting step in catecholamine synthesis.
  • The lecture notes that metyrosine can traverse the CNS and decrease dopamine, which may lead to EPS.

Other classical uses listed in the lecture p. 5

  • Pseudo-obstruction of bowel: Related to inhibitory catecholamine effects on intestinal smooth muscle. The lecture notes that better drugs are used nowadays.
  • Reversal of local anesthesia with a vasoconstrictor agent such as NE in soft tissue: Phentolamine. The lecture notes US FDA approval in 2008.
  • Prevention of dermal necrosis after inadvertent extravasation of an α agonist: Phentolamine blocks α1, leaving β2 effects unopposed.
  • Hypertensive crisis after clonidine withdrawal or ingestion of large amounts of tyrosine-containing food: Phentolamine may be used, though newer drugs with fewer adverse effects are preferred.
  • Male erectile dysfunction: Intracavernosal phentolamine was used, but long-term use causes fibrotic reactions. The lecture states that it has been superseded by oral PDE5 inhibitors such as sildenafil, tadalafil, and vardenafil.
  • Peripheral vascular diseases: Phenoxybenzamine or prazosin may be used for Raynaud’s phenomenon or Berger’s disease. The lecture states that calcium-channel blockers are preferred because of adverse effects.

Structural and dosing anchors p. 5

  • Quinazoline derivatives: Prazosin, Terazosin, Doxazosin, Alfuzosin.
  • Benzenesulfonamide derivative: Tamsulosin.
  • Prazosin has the shortest half-life and duration, so dosing is more frequent.
  • The lecturer notes that prazosin may be given twice daily, unlike terazosin and doxazosin, which are once daily in the lecture discussion.
  • Doxazosin has the longest half-life in the emphasized table.
  • Long-acting Alfuzosin and Tamsulosin are taken once daily.
  • Among α blockers acting in the prostate, Silodosin has the highest apoptotic effect according to the lecturer.

Common adverse effect: first-dose effect p. 5

  • Development of postural hypotension with syncopal episodes after the initial dose of an α1 blocker.
  • More often seen with quinazolines.
  • Less likely with Tamsulosin and Silodosin.
  • Can occur with Alfuzosin.

Lipid-profile effect p. 5

  • Quinazolines have a good effect.
  • They either improve or do not affect plasma lipid profile.
  • The lecture calls them a drug of choice for dyslipidemic patients.

Therapeutic indications pp. 5–6

  • Mild to moderate hypertension: Nonselective α1A/B/D blockers may be used.
  • Selective α1A blockers are not effective as hypertension treatments in the lecture.
  • LUTS associated with BPH: Selective α1A blockers are primarily used when the condition is still amenable to pharmacologic management.
  • Nonselective α1A/B/D blockers can also be used when BPH is accompanied by mild hypertension.
  • CHF and vasospastic disorders: Prazosin may be indicated for short-term use.

Special adverse effects and contraindications p. 6

  • Alfuzosin: Increased risk of QT prolongation in susceptible individuals.
  • Avoid concomitant CYP3A4 inhibitors listed in the lecture, including cimetidine and ketoconazole, because alfuzosin levels can increase.
  • Tamsulosin and Silodosin: Increased incidence of abnormal retrograde ejaculation.
  • They may also cause intraoperative floppy iris syndrome or IFIS.
  • IFIS description in the lecture: bellowing of a flaccid iris, propensity for iris prolapse, and progressive intraoperative pupillary constriction.
Clinical Cue from the lecturerIf a patient has BPH with no hypertension, use Alfuzosin, Tamsulosin, or Silodosin instead of Prazosin, Terazosin, or Doxazosin.

Prazosin and Its Congeners

Why reflex tachycardia is minimal or absent p. 6

  • Selective α1 blockade in arterioles and venules → vasodilation → ↓ PVR.
  • Presynaptic α2 receptors are not blocked.
  • Therefore, the drugs do not promote NE release from sympathetic nerve endings.
  • Negative feedback or autoinhibition remains intact.
  • ↓ cardiac preload produces little tendency to increase CO and HR.
  • Prazosin relaxes both arteriolar and venous vascular smooth muscle.
  • The lecture also lists decreased central sympathetic outflow as a contributor.

First-dose effect p. 6

  • Occurs within 30 to 90 minutes after an initial dose of prazosin.

Metabolic effects p. 6

  • Either improves or does not change plasma lipid profiles and glucose-insulin metabolism in hypertensive patients at risk for atherosclerotic disease.
  • May increase HDL.
  • May decrease LDL and triglycerides.
  • The lecture states that the mechanism is unknown.
  • Presented as a good choice in patients with dyslipidemia.

Cardiac output, pulmonary congestion, and CHF p. 6

  • CO may increase and pulmonary congestion may decrease in patients with pulmonary edema.
  • Prazosin dilates arteries and veins → ↓ preload and ↓ afterload → ↑ CO and ↓ pulmonary congestion.
  • May be used for short-term effects in congestive heart failure.
  • Has limited application in hypertensive emergencies.
  • The lecture notes that Labetalol, a mixed α1 and β blocker, has been used for this setting.

Therapeutic indications p. 6

  • Terazosin and Doxazosin: Mild to moderate hypertension.
  • Concurrent mild hypertension + LUTS associated with BPH when still amenable to pharmacologic management.
  • Other indications listed by the lecture:
    • Hypertension associated with pheochromocytoma.
    • Off-label medical expulsion therapy for distal ureteral calculi.
    • Alleviation of nightmares associated with post-traumatic stress disorder.
    • Antihydrotic use related to SSRI therapy.
    • Chronic prostatitis.
    • Voiding dysfunction in men and women due to primary bladder-neck obstruction.

Lecture Summary

How sympathetic depressants are classified p. 6

  • By site: Central versus peripheral.
  • By receptor subtype blocked.
  • By adrenergic structure affected: Receptor subtype, adrenergic neuron, or ganglion.

Common adverse-effect pattern p. 6

  • Postural orthostatic hypotension is most severe with conventional α blockers such as Phentolamine and Phenoxybenzamine compared with more selective α1 blockers.
  • More profound orthostatic hypotension = more intense reflex tachycardia.
  • Other adverse effects: sedation, increased GI motility, diarrhea, nasal congestion, sexual dysfunction, and increased blood volume and sodium retention.
  • EPS may result from decreased dopamine levels, as with Methyldopa in the lecture.
  • Intense reflex tachycardia is especially associated with conventional α blockers.
  • β-blocker effects on CVS, respiratory system, and metabolism are listed separately.

Major clinical domains emphasized p. 6

  • Hypertension.
  • LUTS associated with BPH.
  • Peripheral vascular diseases.
  • Pheochromocytoma.
  • Specific effects in the CVS, urinary tract, prostate gland, adrenal gland, and lipid metabolism.
