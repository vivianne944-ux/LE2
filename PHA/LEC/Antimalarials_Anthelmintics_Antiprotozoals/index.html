Malaria Foundations

Introduction and the “3 P’s” p. 1

  • The lecture describes the Philippines as highly endemic for malaria.
  • 3 P’s of Treating Malaria
    • Plasmodium species: identify the species, including P. falciparum, P. vivax, and P. ovale.
    • Patient status: uncomplicated or complicated.
    • Pharmacology: susceptibility, availability, or resistance.

Life cycle and clinically important stages p. 1

Figure 1. Life Cycle of the Malaria Parasite, lecture page 1.
Notice the transition from sporozoite entry to the liver stage, then the human blood cell cycle, followed by gametocyte formation and uptake by another mosquito.
1. EntryAn infected female Anopheles mosquito inoculates sporozoites into skin capillaries.
→
2. Hepatic stageSporozoites invade liver parenchymal cells and form tissue schizonts. This is symptom-free.
→
3. Erythrocytic stageMerozoites invade red blood cells, multiply asexually, and form blood schizonts.
→
4. Sexual stageSome merozoites mature into gametocytes that can be ingested by another feeding mosquito.
High-yield lecture noteOnly the asexual erythrocytic stage causes clinical malaria.
The lecture links high-grade fever to intravascular hemolysis of red blood cells when new merozoites are released into the blood.
Hepatic timing and relapse
  • For P. falciparum, the lecture gives 1–12 weeks from infective bite to liver-cell release of merozoites.
  • P. falciparum and P. malariae have only one cycle of hepatic cell invasion.
  • P. vivax and P. ovale have dormant hepatic forms that may release merozoites over months to years.
Why relapse matters
  • The lecture calls the liver form the exoerythrocytic stage.
  • Dormant parasites may remain quiet for months or years.
  • This dormant stage is the main reason for malaria relapse.

Drug use by stage p. 2

  • Causal prophylaxis: preventive therapy used before entering an endemic area. It targets the pre-erythrocytic stage.
  • Radical cure: targets dormant hepatic forms. The lecture identifies primaquine as the key drug.
  • Suppressive or blood-stage therapy: targets the erythrocytic stage where clinical symptoms occur. The lecture notes that most drugs work here.
  • Gametocidal therapy: targets gametocytes and interrupts continuation of the sexual phase.

Resistance, Patient Status & Tactics

Drug resistance strategy pp. 2–3

  • Antimalarial resistance is presented as a major barrier to effective treatment.
  • Chloroquine was formerly a drug of choice for prophylaxis and treatment of P. falciparum malaria, but the lecture notes widespread resistance.
  • Resistance is also noted with sulfadoxine, pyrimethamine, mefloquine, and halofantrine.
  • Combination therapy is emphasized as a way to reduce development of resistance.
  • The lecture notes that standalone artemisinin products should be avoided in favor of artemisinin-containing combinations.

  • Diaphoresis

Drug sensitivity + severity algorithm p. 3

Figure 5. Antimalarial Treatment Based on Drug Sensitivity and Disease Severity, lecture page 3.
The algorithm separates sensitive versus resistant malaria and then distinguishes uncomplicated versus complicated disease.
  • Uncomplicated + sensitive: chloroquine with or without primaquine.
  • Uncomplicated + resistant: options in the lecture include mefloquine ± primaquine, artemether + lumefantrine, atovaquone + proguanil, quinine + doxycycline or clindamycin, or artemisinin therapy.
  • Complicated + worsening: give IV therapy. Once stable, transition to oral treatment.
  • The lecture identifies artemisinin therapy as the gold standard and states that severe disease needs both parenteral and oral medications across the course.

Antimalarial Drug Targets

The lecture organizes antimalarial mechanisms around heme metabolism, electron transport, protein translation, and folate metabolism.

Heme metabolism p. 4

Figure 7. Heme metabolism, lecture page 4.
  • Plasmodia rely on amino acids released from ingested host hemoglobin.
  • Hemoglobin degradation releases toxic ferriprotoporphyrin IX.
  • The parasite detoxifies this by polymerizing it into hemozoin.
  • Chloroquine, halofantrine, and other quinine-type antimalarials inhibit detoxification of the toxic heme metabolite.
  • Lumefantrine and pyronaridine form a complex with hematin and block spontaneous polymerization to beta-hematin and hemozoin.
Resistance anchorThe lecture attributes chloroquine resistance to mutation of the P. falciparum chloroquine resistance transporter (PfCRT), which pumps chloroquine out of the food vacuole.

Artemisinin free-radical mechanism pp. 4, 7, 14

Figure 13. Mechanism of Action of Artemisinins, appendix page 14.
The visual shows iron-dependent peroxide activation, radical formation, protein alkylation, and widespread disruption of parasite proteins.
1. Hemoglobin digestionReleases reactive Fe(II).
→
2. Peroxide activationFe(II) attacks the artemisinin peroxide bond.
→
3. Oxygen radicalsPeroxide bond cleavage generates unstable oxygen-centered radicals.
→
4. Carbon-centered radicalsOxygen radicals rearrange into carbon-centered free radicals.
→
5. Protein alkylationRadicals alkylate parasite proteins.
→
6. Loss of viabilityMany proteins are affected, disrupting multiple essential functions.
High-yieldArtemisinins are marked as the GOLD STANDARD in treating malaria.
The lecture frames the free radical as destroying parasite enzymes or proteins needed for survival.

Electron transport p. 4

Figure 10. Electron Transport, lecture page 4.
  • The lecture identifies primaquine and atovaquone as electron-transport inhibitors acting around ubiquinone-related processes.
  • Ubiquinone accepts electrons linked to DHODH and supports pyrimidine synthesis.
  • Atovaquone is a structural analog of ubiquinone and inhibits interaction between reduced ubiquinone and the Cytochrome BC1 complex.
  • Primaquine causes nonspecific oxidative damage to plasmodial mitochondria through a quinone metabolite that interferes with ubiquinone function.
  • A single-point mutation in the Cytochrome BC1 complex may render plasmodia resistant to atovaquone.

Protein translation p. 5

Figure 11. Protein Translation, lecture page 5.
  • Doxycycline, tetracycline, and clindamycin disrupt malarial parasite protein synthesis.
  • They target the apicoplast, a prokaryote-like organelle within P. falciparum.
  • Doxycycline works at the 30S subunit.
  • Clindamycin acts at the 50S subunit.
  • The lecture describes a delayed death response after disruption of apicoplast activity.
  • Resistance is linked to increased activity of transport systems that pump drugs out of the cell.

Folate metabolism p. 5

Figure 12. Folate Metabolism, lecture page 5.
  • Folic acid supports biosynthetic pathways including DNA and RNA synthesis.
  • Plasmodial parasites and bacteria synthesize folate de novo, providing a target for selective drug action.
  • Antifolates act on parasite-specific isoforms of DHPS and DHFR.
  • Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine acts slowly against blood schizonts.
  • Resistance is linked to mutations in both DHPS and DHFR.
  • The lecture notes widespread resistance in Southeast Asia, South America, and increasingly in Africa.

Antimalarial Drugs I

Chloroquine p. 5

Core profile
  • Quinolone compound described in the lecture as having been discovered in plants about 2000 years ago.
  • Recommended in the lecture for treatment and chemoprophylaxis of malaria without known chloroquine resistance.
  • Effective blood schizonticide, but relatively inactive against liver-stage parasites or gametocytes.
  • Not active against dormant liver forms of P. vivax and P. ovale.
  • Resistance is associated with PfCRT.
Mechanism + PK
  • Prevents detoxification of ferriprotoporphyrin IX.
  • Prevents biocrystallization of heme into non-toxic hemozoin.
  • Lecture half-life: about 3–5 days.
  • For chloroquine-sensitive malaria during pregnancy, the lecture describes 500 mg once weekly as a common prophylactic regimen.
Adverse effects and cautions
  • Parenteral use may cause severe hypotension, respiratory arrest, and cardiac arrest.
  • High doses may cause irreversible ototoxicity, retinopathy, myopathy, and peripheral neuropathy.
  • Rare effects include hemolysis, confusion, psychosis, seizure, agranulocytosis, and exfoliative dermatitis.
  • May cause pruritus in darkly pigmented individuals and exacerbate psoriasis or porphyria.
  • Caution in neurologic and hematologic disorders such as G6PD deficiency.
Lecture noteAt therapeutic doses, adverse effects are described as generally uncommon.
The lecture states that chloroquine appears relatively safe in pregnancy and in young children and is used for most blood stages.

Amodiaquine and Piperaquine pp. 5–6

  • Chemically related to chloroquine and share its mechanism.
  • Effective blood schizonticides acting on the erythrocytic stage.
  • Can target chloroquine-resistant strains of P. falciparum.
  • Resistance can occur with single-drug therapy.
  • The lecture highlights combinations with artemisinins, such as artesunate + amodiaquine and dihydroartemisinin + piperaquine.
  • Amodiaquine half-life: about 14 days.
  • Piperaquine half-life: about 28 days.
First-line combinations in lectureArtesunate + amodiaquine is highlighted for uncomplicated malaria in many parts of Africa.
Dihydroartemisinin + piperaquine is highlighted for uncomplicated malaria in many parts of Southeast Asia.
Adverse effects
  • Long-term or serious effects may include agranulocytosis, aplastic anemia, and hepatotoxicity.
  • At standard dosage, the lecture describes both agents as non-teratogenic and notes use with artemisinins during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.
  • Short-term therapy is described as generally well tolerated, with anorexia, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and weakness.

Quinine and Quinidine p. 6

  • Originally derived from cinchona bark.
  • Effective blood schizonticides for all types of malarial species and used for parenteral treatment of complicated or severe falciparum malaria.
  • The lecture identifies quinine or quinidine as important drugs for severe malaria.
  • Lecture regimen for oral quinine: 7.5 mg/kg every 8 hours. After recovery begins, an oral dose of 8 mg/kg every 8 hours is given.
High-yield adverse effects: “QUeen B-UCCHHH”Blackwater fever: rare hypersensitivity reaction causing hemolysis.
Uterine contractions: mild, especially in the 3rd trimester, described as unique to quinine and quinidine.
Cinchonism: tinnitus, headache, dizziness, blurring of vision.
Cardiotoxicity: prolonged QT interval.
Hematologic toxicity: leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, agranulocytosis.
Hypoglycemia: increased insulin release.
Hypersensitivity: rash, urticaria, angioedema, bronchospasm.
Contraindications and interactions
  • G6PD deficiency
  • Cardiac abnormalities
  • Renal insufficiency
  • Visual and auditory problems
  • Antacids may prevent drug absorption in the stomach.
  • Fast IV infusion may cause thrombophlebitis and severe hypotension.
  • The lecture says not to combine with mefloquine because of additive cardiac toxicity.

Mefloquine p. 6

  • Quinoline compound structurally related to chloroquine.
  • Used as a first-line treatment for resistant strains and uncomplicated P. falciparum malaria in combination with IV artesunate in the lecture.
  • Suppressive blood schizonticide acting on the erythrocytic stage.
  • Used as first-line weekly prophylaxis in areas with chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum.
  • Half-life: about 14–20 days.
  • Peak plasma concentration: about 18 hours.
  • Given 1–2 weeks before travel and then once weekly in the endemic area.
Mefloquine = “Mind Floats”High doses enter the brain and heart.
The lecture warns about serious neuropsychiatric toxicity including vivid dreams, nightmares, insomnia, depression, seizures, and psychosis.
Cardiac conduction abnormalities may prolong the QT interval and cause arrhythmias.
Contraindications
  • Epilepsy
  • Psychiatric disorders
  • Arrhythmias
  • Cardiac conduction defect
  • CNS and CVS dysfunction
  • Do not co-administer with quinine, quinidine, primaquine, or halofantrine because of additive cardiovascular toxicity.

Antimalarial Drugs II

Artemisinins pp. 6–7

  • Used in uncomplicated and complicated malaria.
  • The lecture recommends use for pregnant patients in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters and for children, with IV artesunate noted for all trimesters in the later summary.
  • Form free-radical compounds that cause plasmodial death through alkylation of many proteins.
  • Rapidly decrease malarial parasites in the blood and produce rapid symptom resolution.
  • Effective against young gametocytes.
  • Used as artemisinin combination therapy to reduce resistance and recrudescence.

  • Artemisinins are always used in combination therapy according to the lecture note.

Pyronaridine pp. 7–8

  • Combined with artesunate as Pyramax.
  • Blood schizonticide against P. falciparum and P. vivax in adults and children.
  • Used for uncomplicated and multidrug-resistant P. falciparum malaria.
  • Mechanism is similar to quinine-type drugs by inhibiting formation of hemozoin.
  • The lecture says the most common adverse effects are similar to malaria symptoms, and increased liver enzymes return to normal after approximately 1 week.
  • Dosed according to body weight.
  • Safety and efficacy of the oral suspension are established in infants and children weighing 5 kg to <20 kg.

Atovaquone + Proguanil (Malarone) p. 8

Atovaquone
  • Structural analog of ubiquinone.
  • Inhibits the Cytochrome BC1 complex and disrupts electron transport.
  • Not used as a single agent because of resistance history.
  • Usual combination in the lecture: Atovaquone 250 mg + Proguanil 100 mg.
  • Targets tissue and erythrocytic schizonts.
  • Used for treatment and prophylaxis of falciparum malaria.
Proguanil
  • Prodrug that inhibits dihydrofolate reductase, interfering with folic-acid synthesis.
  • Active against both tissue and erythrocytic schizonts.
  • The lecture says proguanil alone is cautioned in pregnancy and is preferably used during the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.
Why Malarone is convenient in the lectureIt has a shorter prophylaxis period before and after exposure than mefloquine or doxycycline.

Primaquine pp. 2, 8

  • Tissue schizonticide and gametocidal.
  • Metabolites disrupt mitochondrial metabolic processes, including electron transport activity.
  • Used predominantly as terminal prophylaxis to clear hepatic hypnozoites caused by P. vivax and P. ovale.
  • Drug of choice for P. vivax and P. ovale in the lecture.
  • Active against gametocytes of all four malarial species in the lecture.
  • When used with chloroquine, chloroquine eradicates erythrocytic forms while primaquine targets hepatic forms.
Primaquine safetyAvoid in G6PD deficiency because of risk of hemolysis or methemoglobinemia.
Avoid in pregnancy because the lecture states it may cause in utero hemolysis of fetal erythrocytes.
Discontinue primaquine if there is evidence of hemolysis or anemia.

Antifolate combinations pp. 8–9

  • Highly effective against the blood schizont stages of all four human malarial species in the lecture.
  • Important combinations are sulfadoxine + pyrimethamine (Fansidar) and proguanil + atovaquone (Malarone).
  • The lecture notes convenience as a single dose for treatment and prevention of chloroquine-resistant P. falciparum malaria.
Sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine adverse effects
  • Sulfonamide hypersensitivity may include SJS, erythema multiforme, and TEN.
  • Hematologic adverse effects include megaloblastic anemia, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia.
  • Renal toxicity may occur.
  • Use cautiously in renal dysfunction, hepatic dysfunction, and infants <2 months old.

Fermentation pathway target p. 10

  • Amoebae are obligate fermenters of glucose to ethanol as an energy source.
  • They lack several enzymes used in humans, including lactate dehydrogenase, pyruvate decarboxylase, and oxidative-phosphorylation enzymes.
  • They use pyruvate-ferredoxin oxidoreductase (PFOR) for electron transfer that drives metabolism.
  • Ferredoxins shuttle electrons during reduction reactions.
  • PFOR catalyzes decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA.
  • Acetyl-CoA is reduced to ethanol through alcohol dehydrogenase E (AdHE).
Pivotal drug targetMetronidazole is activated through interaction with reduced ferredoxin or specific nitroreductases.
Nitazoxanide interferes with PFOR-dependent electron transfer essential for anaerobic energy metabolism.

Cram Sheet

Malaria stage anchors
  • Only the asexual erythrocytic stage causes clinical malaria.
    • Fever is linked in the lecture to intravascular hemolysis when merozoites are released.
  • P. vivax and P. ovale have dormant hepatic forms → relapse.
  • Primaquine → pre-erythrocytic + radical cure + gametocidal roles.
  • Blood-stage disease is where most clinical symptoms occur and where most antimalarial drugs act.

Four antimalarial target pathways
  • Heme metabolism → prevent conversion of toxic ferriprotoporphyrin IX to hemozoin.
    • Chloroquine resistance cue: PfCRT.
    • Lumefantrine and pyronaridine block hematin polymerization.
  • Artemisinins → Fe(II) activates peroxide bond → oxygen radicals → carbon-centered radicals → parasite-protein alkylation → loss of viability.
  • Electron transport → atovaquone blocks Cytochrome BC1. Primaquine causes nonspecific oxidative mitochondrial damage.
  • Protein translation → doxycycline 30S, clindamycin 50S in the apicoplast.
  • Folate metabolism → sulfadoxine targets DHPS, pyrimethamine targets DHFR.
Red-exclamation contentArtemisinins = GOLD STANDARD in treating malaria.
Primaquine = DOC for P. vivax and P. ovale in the lecture.
Stop primaquine if hemolysis or anemia develops.
Severely complicated malaria → lecture emphasizes parenteral artemisinins and quinine or quinidine.
Antifolates are contraindicated in infants and young children according to the lecture.
