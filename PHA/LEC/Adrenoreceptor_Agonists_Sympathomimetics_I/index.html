Sympathomimetics: Core Definition

p. 1

The sympathetic nervous system regulates all organ systems and is important in blood pressure regulation. Sympathomimetic drugs mimic endogenous epinephrine and norepinephrine at adrenergic sites by activating adrenoceptors or inducing release of endogenous norepinephrine.

Indirect-Acting Sympathomimetics

p. 1

These agents are poor agonists at adrenoceptors and do not directly activate the receptor. Their effect depends on increasing endogenous catecholamines.

1
Release or displace stored NE.
Agents enter noradrenergic terminals through NET, can enter vesicles through VMAT, displace NE, and promote reverse transport through NET. This release is described as calcium independent and does not require vesicle exocytosis.
2
Decrease clearance of released NE.
Neuronal reuptake inhibitors block uptake 1. Enzyme inhibitors block MAO or COMT, leaving more catecholamine available for adrenergic action.

Receptor Selectivity vs Specificity

p. 2

Receptor selectivity means preferentially binding or activating one receptor subgroup at concentrations too low to interact extensively with another subgroup. It is not absolute and is lost at high concentrations.

Specificity is described in the lecture as near absolute selectivity.

Important to Knowp. 2
Salbutamol is β2 selective at recommended doses and produces bronchodilation without meaningful β1 stimulation. At higher doses, other β receptors may also be stimulated.
Pirenzepine is used in the lecture as a parallel example of dose-dependent selectivity at muscarinic receptors.

Uptake of Catecholamines

p. 4
1
Neuronal uptake or Uptake-1 through NET is the principal removal route.
It quickly captures about 75% of NE released by sympathetic neurons.
About 90% of NE released at cardiac synapses is removed by NET.
In vasculature, removal may still be 60% or more.
2
Extraneuronal uptake or Uptake-2 moves transmitter into extraneuronal cells where it is metabolized by COMT.
3
Some released neurotransmitter escapes into extrasynaptic space and bloodstream.

Circulating catecholamines are usually inactivated by a combination of Uptake-1, Uptake-2, and COMT. Norepinephrine depends mainly on Uptake-1. Epinephrine depends more on Uptake-2. Isoproterenol is removed by Uptake-2 plus COMT and is not a substrate for Uptake-1.

α2 Receptor: Gi → ↓ Adenylyl Cyclase

p. 4

α2 receptors inhibit adenylyl cyclase through Gi, causing decreased intracellular cAMP.

They also regulate ion channels by inhibiting Ca2+ channels and promoting opening of K+ channels.

All β receptor types stimulate adenylyl cyclase through Gs. This raises cAMP and activates PKA.

Desensitization of Responses

p. 5

Desensitization is a GPCR-mediated process in which a cell or tissue becomes less responsive to further catecholamine or sympathomimetic stimulation after exposure for a period of time. It can limit therapeutic response.

Related terms
TermLecture definition
ToleranceDecreasing magnitude of receptor response to the same initial dose over a period of time.
TachyphylaxisAcute form of tolerance that develops rapidly when a drug is given in a few successive doses at close intervals.
Associated with indirectly acting sympathomimetics such as tyramine and amphetamine.
RefractorinessClinical term for therapeutic failure or non-response to drug use in management of a condition.

Homologous Desensitization

p. 5
1
Repeated or sustained agonist activation affects only the agonist-occupied receptors.
2
GRK rapidly phosphorylates the agonist-bound receptor.
3
Arrestin binds and blunts the receptor’s capacity to activate G proteins.
4
Arrestin interaction with clathrin leads to receptor endocytosis.

Heterologous Desensitization

p. 5

An agonist can produce desensitization of another receptor that was not directly stimulated. The lecture attributes this to second-messenger feedback.

For example, β adrenoceptor stimulation raises cAMP and activates protein kinase A or protein kinase C. These kinases may phosphorylate structurally similar receptors that contain appropriate phosphorylation sites.

Do Not Confusep. 5
Homologous stays focused on agonist-occupied receptors.
Heterologous can spread desensitization to another receptor through second-messenger feedback.

Parent Structure and Catecholamines

p. 5

Phenylethylamine is the parent compound with a benzene ring plus an ethylamine side chain.

Catecholamines contain hydroxyl groups at C3 and C4 of the benzene ring plus the ethylamine side chain.

Substitution can occur on the benzene ring, terminal amino group, α carbon, or β carbon. The lecture emphasizes that chemical structure influences receptor affinity, pharmacokinetics, bioavailability, and intrinsic receptor activation.

Noncatecholamine Structures

p. 5

Substitutions on the Benzene Ring

p. 6

Hydroxyl groups at C3 and C4 produce catecholamines. Presence of both ring hydroxyls allows inactivation by COMT in gut and liver.

Removing one hydroxyl greatly reduces potency, especially when C3 lacks substitution. Phenylephrine has about a 100-fold decrease in α-receptor affinity compared with epinephrine according to the lecture and has almost negligible β activity except at very high concentration.

Absence of one hydroxyl also prevents COMT metabolism, increasing oral bioavailability and prolonging duration.

Absence of Both Ring Hydroxyl Groups

p. 6

Loss of both ring hydroxyl groups increases CNS distribution, allows BBB traversal, supports oral activity, and prolongs duration.

This pattern is seen in phenylisopropylamines such as ephedrine and amphetamine.

Substitution on the Amino Group

p. 6

Increasing the size of alkyl substituents on the amino group increases β-receptor activity and lowers α-receptor activity.

Amino-group substitution
ExampleLecture relationship
EpinephrineMethyl substitution on norepinephrine’s amino group increases β-receptor activity.
IsoproterenolLarger isopropyl substitution gives greater β activity than epinephrine and norepinephrine and very weak α activity.
β2 agonistsRequire a larger amino substituent group.

Substitution on the α Carbon

p. 6

α-carbon substitution blocks oxidation by MAO, prolonging duration and enhancing the ability to displace catecholamines from noradrenergic storage sites.

α-carbon substitution
Drugα-carbon implication
PhenylephrineNo α-carbon substitution → susceptible to MAO.

Ephedrine and amphetaminesα-carbon substitution → resistant to MAO and longer acting.

Substitution on the β Carbon

p. 6

β-carbon substitution is important for storage of sympathomimetic amines in neural vesicles and facilitates adrenoceptor activation.

Direct-acting adrenergic agonists have a hydroxyl group substitution on the β carbon. Dopamine does not.

What Determines the Overall Effect?

p. 6

The lecture states that overall pharmacologic effects depend on relative receptor affinity, relative receptor expression in a tissue, and route of administration. Magnitude also depends on receptor number and function, receptor regulation, hormones, other drugs, age, and disease states.

Gastrointestinal Effects

p. 8

Presynaptic α2 and postsynaptic β2 activation relax the smooth muscle wall and reduce intestinal tone and motility. Presynaptic α2 heteroreceptors on cholinergic nerve endings modulate acetylcholine release.

α1 activation contracts pyloric and ileocecal sphincters.

Respiratory Effects

p. 8

β2 activation relaxes tracheal and bronchial smooth muscle → bronchodilation.

α1 activation decreases bronchial secretion and mucosal congestion. β2 stimulation can inhibit histamine release from mast cells.

Genitourinary Effects

p. 8

Urinary hesitancy is difficulty initiating micturition. The lecture notes it is common in older men with enlarged prostate and can be associated with older treatment medicines, nasal decongestants, and anti-allergy medicines.

p. 8

Response varies with sexual-cycle phase, gestational state, and dose. During the last month of pregnancy and parturition, β2 agonists decrease uterine tone and contraction and can delay premature labor.

Sympathomimetics that activate α1, such as norepinephrine or phenylephrine, cause uterine contraction and are stated in the lecture to be avoided during pregnancy.

Metabolic Effects

p. 8

Hyperglycemia and hyperlactacidemia

β activation enhances glycogenolysis in liver and muscle.
Glucagon secretion is enhanced through β2 stimulation of pancreatic α islet cells.
α2 inhibition of insulin secretion can dominate. Epinephrine’s predominant pancreatic effect is inhibition of insulin secretion even though β2 activation can enhance insulin secretion.

Hyperlipidemia

β3 and β1 stimulation in adipose tissue activates glyceride lipase and enhances triglyceride breakdown → increased FFA release.

Calorigenic action

Provides more freely available body fuel and increases O2 consumption by 20–30%.

Epinephrine: Prototype Catecholamine

p. 9

Epinephrine activates all adrenergic receptors. It is polar, poorly penetrates body membranes including CNS, and is not given orally because it is rapidly metabolized in GI mucosa and liver.

Preparations listed in the lecture: IM, SC, IV, nasal, ophthalmic, aerosol.

Blood Pressure Frameworkp. 9
Epinephrine’s BP effect depends on HR, myocardial function, venous return, and PVR at a given dose.
Most vascular smooth muscle contains more α1 influence and therefore vasoconstricts.
Heart rate has predominantly parasympathetic or vagal influence.

Slow IV Infusion or Subcutaneous Injection

p. 9

Systolic pressure rises only moderately, so mean BP is not markedly elevated.

β1 directly raises HR, contractility, and CO. Dominant vascular β2 action lowers PVR and DBP and enhances blood flow.

No reflex bradycardia occurs because direct cardiac effects are not antagonized by compensatory baroreceptor reflex mechanisms in this pattern.

Epinephrine reversal occurs in the presence of an α-receptor blocker.

Faster IV Infusion

p. 9

Blood pressure rises because systolic pressure increases more than diastolic pressure.

1
↑ myocardial or ventricular force of contraction.
2
↑ HR.
3
α1-mediated vasoconstriction in skin and mucosal vessels.

PVR and DBP may rise slightly depending on dose and the net α1 versus β2 vascular effect. The compensatory baroreceptor reflex may antagonize direct cardiac actions.

Core Framework

p. 9
  • Sympathomimetics mimic adrenergic stimulation by activating adrenoceptors or increasing endogenous NE.
  • Direct = receptor activation. Indirect = release, reuptake inhibition, or enzyme inhibition. Mixed = direct activation + NE release.
  • Selectivity is preferential, not absolute. High concentrations can recruit additional receptors.

PK and Structure

p. 4
  • Catecholamines: polar, poor oral absorption, poor CNS penetration, brief duration, rapidly inactivated in gut and liver.
  • Noncatecholamines: generally lipid soluble, orally active, longer acting, may cross BBB.
  • Uptake-1 through NET is the main NE removal route. About 75% of released NE is quickly captured. About 90% at cardiac synapses. In vasculature, 60% or more.
  • MAO + COMT metabolize catecholamines. COMT is absent from noradrenergic neurons.
  • Both ring OH groups at C3 and C4 → catecholamine and COMT metabolism. Missing ring OH → greater bioavailability and longer duration. Missing both → more CNS access.
  • Larger amino-group alkyl substituent → more β activity. α-carbon substitution → MAO resistance and more indirect releasing behavior.

Safety and Traps

p. 8
  • α1 extravasation can cause severe local ischemia, tissue sloughing, and necrosis.
  • High α1 stimulation can produce severe hypertension and cerebral hemorrhage.
  • β1 stimulation can contribute to arrhythmias and ventricular fibrillation.
  • Contraindications: coronary artery disease, arteriosclerosis, concomitant nonselective β blocker.
  • Nonselective β blocker + sympathomimetic can leave α1 action unopposed → severe hypertension and cerebral hemorrhage.
  • α1 uterine contraction from norepinephrine or phenylephrine is stated in the lecture to be avoided during pregnancy.
