I. Adrenoceptor Agonists & Sympathomimetics

The sympathetic nervous system is a division of the autonomic nervous system and is an important regulator of organ systems, including blood pressure regulation and maintenance during stress. p.1

Sympathomimetic drugs mimic adrenergic sympathetic nerve stimulation by activating adrenoceptors or inducing release of endogenous norepinephrine.

B. Uptake, metabolic degradation, and excretion

Uptake-1 is the principal route for NE removal
Neuronal uptake, Uptake-1 occurs through the norepinephrine transporter, NET.
The lecture states that NET quickly captures about 75% of NE released by sympathetic neurons.
In the heart, about 90% of released NE at the synapse is removed by NET.
In vasculature, removal may still be 60% or more. p.4
  1. Neuronal uptake, Uptake-1: NE is captured by NET and recycled, which cuts short neurotransmitter activity.
  2. Extraneuronal uptake, Uptake-2: neurotransmitter enters extraneuronal cells and is metabolized by COMT.
  3. Escape to bloodstream: some released neurotransmitter enters the extrasynaptic space and circulation.
Which removal pathway predominates?
Norepinephrine: mainly Uptake-1.
Epinephrine: more dependent on Uptake-2.
Isoproterenol: Uptake-2 plus COMT and is not a substrate for Uptake-1.

IV. Receptor Regulation

A. Desensitization of responses

Desensitization is the process in which a cell or tissue becomes less responsive to further catecholamine or sympathomimetic stimulation after exposure for a period of time. The lecture also uses the terms tolerance, tachyphylaxis, and refractoriness. p.5

Associated with indirectly acting sympathomimetics such as tyramine and amphetamine

A. Substitutions on the benzene ring

Hydroxyl substitution at C3 and C4 yields catecholamines. Presence of both hydroxyl groups makes catecholamines substrates for COMT, which is found in the gut and liver. p.6

B. Absence of one ring -OH group

  • Greatly reduces potency, particularly when C3 lacks substitution.
  • Phenylephrine is the lecture example under this section.
  • The lecture states that α-receptor affinity is reduced about 100-fold compared with epinephrine.
  • β-receptor activity is almost negligible except at very high concentrations, which supports classification as a selective α1 agonist.
  • Loss of a catechol -OH means the drug is not deactivated by COMT, which increases oral bioavailability and prolongs duration of action.

C. Absence of ring -OH groups

Absence of ring hydroxyl groups increases distribution to the CNS, allows BBB traversal, supports oral activity, and prolongs duration of action. The lecture highlights ephedrine and amphetamine as phenylisopropylamine examples. p.6

D. Substitution on the amino group

Bigger N-alkyl substituent → more β activity
Increasing the size of the alkyl substituent on the amino group increases β-receptor activity and lowers α-receptor activity.
Epinephrine has a methyl substitution on the amino group of norepinephrine, increasing β activity.
Isoproterenol has a larger isopropyl substitution and greater β activity with very weak α activity.
β2 agonists require a larger amino substituent group. p.6

E. Substitution on α-carbon

  • Blocks oxidation by MAO.
  • Prolongs duration of action of noncatecholamines.
  • Enhances the ability to displace catecholamines from storage sites in noradrenergic nerve endings, supporting an indirect mechanism that depends on normal NE stores.
  • Phenylephrine: no α-carbon substitution in the lecture comparison, so it remains susceptible to MAO.
  • Ephedrine and amphetamines: α-carbon substitution → MAO resistance, longer duration, and indirect release of stored catecholamines.

F. Substitution on β-carbon

  • Important for storage of sympathomimetic amines in neural vesicles.
  • Facilitates activation of adrenoceptors.
  • Direct-acting adrenergic agonists are described as having a hydroxyl group substitution on β-carbon.
  • The lecture notes that dopamine does not have this. p.6

The overall effect of a sympathomimetic drug may depend on its relative receptor affinity, the relative expression of receptor subtypes in the tissue, and the route of administration. Cardiovascular Effects

  • β1 in heart: positive inotropy → ↑ CO by ↑ contractility, positive chronotropy → ↑ HR, positive dromotropy → ↑ AV nodal conduction velocity.
  • α2 in vasculature: vasoconstriction can occur with very high oral doses or rapid IV α2 agonist injection. At usual recommended doses, central sympatholytic effects predominate and BP decreases.
  • β2 in selected vascular beds: vasodilation → ↓ PVR. p.8

Nonvascular Smooth Muscle Effects

Gastrointestinal tract

  • Presynaptic α2 and postsynaptic β2 activation decrease intestinal tone and motility.
  • Presynaptic α2 receptors on cholinergic nerve endings modulate acetylcholine release.
  • α1 activation contracts pyloric and ileocecal sphincters.

Respiratory tract

  • β2 stimulation relaxes tracheal and bronchial smooth muscle → bronchodilation.
  • α1 activation decreases bronchial secretion and mucosal congestion.
  • β2 stimulation can inhibit histamine release from mast cells.

Genitourinary tract

Urinary retention pattern
β3 and β2 → detrusor relaxation.
α1 → contraction of trigone and sphincter.
α1 → prostate smooth muscle contraction, which can promote urinary retention in BPH.
β3 agonists mirabegron and vibegron increase bladder storage capacity and are used for OAB in the lecture. p.8

Uterus

  • Response varies with sexual cycle, gestation, and dose.
  • β2 sympathomimetics reduce uterine tone and contractions in late pregnancy and can delay premature labor.
  • α1-activating sympathomimetics such as norepinephrine or phenylephrine cause uterine contraction and are described in the lecture as drugs to avoid during pregnancy.

Other Effects

  • Secretory glands: overall inhibition is not marked. α1 stimulation produces scant salivary mucus secretion, mainly amylase and water. Apocrine glands produce nonthermoregulatory adrenergic sweating, including palms and soles during psychological stress.
  • Iris: α1 contracts radial pupillodilator muscle → mydriasis without cycloplegia. The lecture also notes decreased intraocular pressure when sympathomimetic is instilled into the conjunctival sac.
  • Male sex organs: α1 activation → ejaculation.
  • Potassium: β2 increases uptake of K+ into skeletal muscle cells → hypokalemia.

Metabolic Effects

Hyperglycemia and hyperlactacidemia

  • β activation enhances glycogenolysis in liver and muscle.
  • Glucagon secretion may occur through α1-islet cell stimulation as stated in the lecture.
  • Peripheral glucose uptake decreases because α2-mediated inhibition of insulin secretion is dominant.
  • Epinephrine: α2 inhibits insulin secretion, while β2 can enhance insulin secretion. The lecture states that the predominant epinephrine effect is inhibition of insulin secretion.
  • β2 activation of α-islet cells enhances glucagon secretion.

Hyperlipidemia

β3 and β1 receptor stimulation in adipose tissue activates glyceride lipase and enhances triglyceride breakdown, increasing free fatty acids in circulation.

Calorigenic action

The lecture describes increased free available body fuel with increased O2 consumption by 20–30%. p.8

VII. Epinephrine

  • Prototype catecholamine.
  • Activates all adrenergic receptors.
  • Polar and poorly penetrates body membranes including the CNS.
  • Not given orally because it is rapidly metabolized by enzymes in GI mucosa and liver.
  • Preparations listed: IM, SC, IV, nasal, ophthalmic, aerosol. p.9
Figure 8. Regulation of Blood Pressure. p.9
The lecture frames epinephrine BP effects around HR, myocardial function, venous return, and PVR. Most vascular smooth muscle contains α1, while some beds contain both α1 and β2.

A. Small doses

≈ 0.1 mcg/kg
β2 receptors are more sensitive to epinephrine than α1 receptors.
Vascular smooth muscle relaxes → vasodilation → ↓ PVR → ↓ DBP.
A fall in blood pressure is possible. p.9

B. Large doses

  • Produce a biphasic response from α1 and β2 stimulation.
  • α1: vasoconstriction and ↑ PVR. This effect is more predominant but transient.
  • β2: vasodilation and ↓ PVR. This effect is more prolonged.
  • Overall PVR and DBP may slightly increase, decrease, or remain unchanged depending on concentration and net receptor effects.
  • Direct β1 cardiac activation increases inotropy, chronotropy, dromotropy, conduction velocity, cardiac output, and systolic pressure.
  • The lecture notes that mean BP is not markedly elevated in this setting, so bradycardia is not observed. p.9

C. Slow IV infusion or subcutaneous injection

  • Systolic pressure is only moderately increased, so mean BP is not markedly elevated.
  • Direct β1 activation → ↑ HR, myocardial force, and cardiac output.
  • Dominant β2 vascular action in skeletal muscle beds → ↓ PVR and ↓ DBP.
  • β2 vasodilation enhances blood flow.
  • No reflex bradycardia in the lecture description because direct cardiac effects are not antagonized by compensatory baroreceptor reflex mechanisms.
  • Epinephrine reversal occurs in the presence of an α-receptor blocker. p.9

D. Faster IV infusions

Blood pressure rises because systolic pressure increases markedly through direct β1 cardiac stimulation. The lecture gives three mechanisms for the marked BP rise:

  1. ↑ myocardial or ventricular force of contraction.
  2. ↑ heart rate.
  3. α1-mediated vasoconstriction of skin and mucosal vessels.

PVR and DBP may be slightly increased depending on dose and the net α1 versus β2 vascular effect. At faster infusion rates, the compensatory baroreceptor reflex may oppose direct cardiac actions. p.9

E. Therapeutic uses of epinephrine

Anaphylaxis / hypersensitivity reactions
Used as a physiologic or functional antagonist.
Activates α1, β1, and β2 to reverse the pathophysiologic process described in the lecture.
Concentration: 1:1000 aqueous solution.
Dose: 0.3–0.5 mg.

  • Complete heart block or cardiac arrest: resuscitation to return spontaneous rhythm in remote areas without available defibrillators, as stated in the lecture.
  • Topical hemostatic agent: examples include nasal packing for epistaxis and gingival strings during gingivectomy or after multiple tooth extractions.

Cram Sheet

Core classification
Direct: directly activates adrenoceptors.
Indirect: raises endogenous catecholamines by release, reuptake inhibition, or enzyme inhibition.
Mixed: direct receptor activation + NE release.
Selectivity rule
Receptor selectivity is dose-dependent and not absolute.
At high concentrations, selectivity can be lost.

Pharmacokinetics and metabolism

Catecholamines
Polar.
Poor oral absorption.
Poor CNS penetration.
Brief duration.
Rapid inactivation in intestinal mucosa and liver.
Noncatecholamines
Generally more lipid soluble.
May cross BBB.
Longer duration.
Oral administration may be possible.
NE removal numbers
~75% of NE released by sympathetic neurons is quickly captured by NET.
~90% at cardiac synapses is removed by NET.
≥60% may still be removed in vasculature.
Enzymes
MAO + COMT metabolize catecholamines.
MHPG → mostly VMA or conjugates → urine.

SAR must-knows

  • C3 + C4 hydroxyls → catecholamine and COMT susceptibility.
  • Loss of one ring -OH → lower potency, COMT resistance, better oral bioavailability, longer action.
  • Loss of ring -OH groups → more CNS distribution, oral activity, longer action.
  • Larger amino substituent → more β activity and less α activity.
  • α-carbon substitution → MAO resistance, longer duration, better catecholamine displacement from storage.
  • β-carbon substitution supports vesicular storage and receptor activation.

Epinephrine must-knows

Dose / route pattern
Small dose ≈ 0.1 mcg/kg: β2 more sensitive than α1 → vasodilation → ↓ PVR and ↓ DBP.
Large dose: biphasic α1 vasoconstriction then longer β2 vasodilation.
Slow IV / SC: direct β1 cardiac effects + dominant β2 vascular effects → no reflex bradycardia in the lecture description.
Faster IV: marked systolic BP rise, α1 vasoconstriction contributes, baroreceptor reflex may oppose direct cardiac action.
α blocker present: epinephrine reversal.
Anaphylaxis numbers from the lecture
1:1000 aqueous solution.
0.3–0.5 mg.
Preferably IM, may also be SC in the lecture.

Safety traps

Remember
Strong α1 agonist extravasation → severe local ischemia → tissue sloughing / necrosis.
High α1 effect → severe hypertension and possible cerebral hemorrhage.
β1 excess → tachycardia, arrhythmias, ventricular fibrillation.
Nonselective β blocker + sympathomimetic → unopposed α1 action → severe hypertension / cerebral hemorrhage.
Contraindications listed: coronary artery disease, arteriosclerosis, concomitant nonselective β blocker.
