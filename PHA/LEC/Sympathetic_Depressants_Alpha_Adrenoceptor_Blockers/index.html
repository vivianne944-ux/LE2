II. Common side effects that interfere with sympathetic neuron function

1. Postural orthostatic hypotension

  • Guanethidine and ganglionic blockers can cause profound orthostatic hypotension.
  • Adrenergic neuron and autonomic ganglion blockade produces sympathetic depression.
  • Alpha1 blockade in vascular smooth muscle causes vasodilation → decreased PVR → enhanced blood flow → hypotension.
  • Loss of the vasoconstrictor reflex on standing causes peripheral pooling of blood.

2. Sedation or CNS depression

  • Produced by centrally acting alpha2 agonists such as Clonidine.
  • Also listed with Reserpine and Phenoxybenzamine.
  • Some beta blockers such as Propranolol may also produce sedation.

3. Increased GI motility and diarrhea

  • Results from sympathetic blockade.
  • Parasympathetic dominance occurs in structures innervated by both ANS divisions.

4. Sexual dysfunction

  • May appear as delayed or retrograde ejaculation.
  • Attributed to alpha1 receptor blockade.
  • Produced by non-selective alpha blockers, Guanethidine, and ganglionic blockers.

5. Increased blood volume and sodium retention

Listed with non-selective alpha blockers, Guanethidine, and ganglionic blockers.

6. Nasal stuffiness

  • Results from parasympathetic predominance.
  • Lecture examples include Clonidine, Methyldopa, Reserpine, Guanethidine, and ganglionic blockers.

7. Extrapyramidal symptoms

  • Attributed to decreased dopamine levels.
  • Lecture examples include Methyldopa and Reserpine, which deplete catecholamines.

Alpha-blocker-limited addition

  • Reflex tachycardia may follow profound orthostatic hypotension through the compensatory baroreceptor reflex.
  • The lecture notes no EPS in the list limited to alpha blockers.

Effects listed as limited to beta adrenoceptor blockers pp. 1–2

  • CVS: Bradycardia, decreased force of contraction, and decreased cardiac output through direct beta1 blockade.
  • Respiratory: Beta2 blockade → bronchoconstriction, especially in hypersensitive individuals with parasympathetic predominance.
  • Metabolic: Hypoglycemia and decreased lipolysis. The lecture links decreased lipolysis to beta3 blockade and beta1 blockade to some extent. It also links hypoglycemia to inhibition of glycogenolysis and reduced glucagon release, including decreased beta2-mediated glucagon release from pancreatic alpha cells.

Centrally Acting alpha2 Receptor Agonists and Clonidine

Lecture section III A–E. p. 2

Location of alpha2 receptors and their actions

Must Know Marker from the LectureCentrally acting selective alpha2 agonists bind more tightly to alpha2 than alpha1 adrenoceptors.

Common adverse effects and uses

Common adverse effects

  • Drowsiness or sedation
  • Xerostomia
  • Decreased blood pressure
  • Bradycardia
  • Withdrawal syndrome or rebound phenomenon after abrupt cessation of a chronically administered high dose

Common therapeutic uses

  • Hypertension: Clonidine, Methyldopa, Guanfacine
  • Guanabenz was discontinued because of side effects related to lethargy
  • Pregnancy hypertension: Methyldopa
  • Ocular hypertension and glaucoma: Apraclonidine, Brimonidine

The lecture also lists Tizanidine as a centrally acting muscle relaxant for muscle spasticity and Guanadrel under alpha2 receptor agonists.

Clonidine: chemistry and pharmacokinetics

  • Imidazoline derivative and lipid soluble.
  • Bioavailability: 95%.
  • Rapidly enters the CNS.
  • Peak concentration after an oral dose: 1–3 hours.
  • Elimination half-life: 8–12 hours.
  • Initially tested as a topical nasal decongestant. Hypotension, sedation, and bradycardia were observed, leading to its antihypertensive use.
  • Administration can be oral or by transdermal patch.
  • The transdermal patch causes less sedation than tablets. A single application reduces BP for 7 days. After patch removal, plasma concentration remains stable for about 8 hours.

Clonidine mechanism of action

  • Peripheral presynaptic alpha2 activation suppresses release of NE, ATP, and NPY from postganglionic sympathetic nerves.
  • May activate imidazoline I1 receptors.
  • Inhibits adenylyl cyclase → decreased cAMP.

Clonidine pharmacologic actions and effects

CVS

  • Decreased HR, stroke volume, and relaxation of capacitance vessels → decreased CO and PVR → decreased arterial BP.
  • Clonidine lowers HR and CO more than Methyldopa.
  • Decreases renal vascular resistance.
  • Like Methyldopa, it decreases BP in the supine position and rarely causes orthostatic hypotension.

Renal and eye effects

  • May decrease renin release.
  • Decreased adenylyl cyclase → decreased cAMP → decreased JG cell stimulation → decreased renin.
  • Decreased aqueous humor production → decreased intraocular pressure.
  • Apraclonidine and Brimonidine are used for glaucoma.
IV Clonidine SequencePostsynaptic alpha2B activation in vascular smooth muscle → transient vasoconstriction → acute rise in BP.This is followed by a more prolonged hypotensive response from decreased central sympathetic outflow, partly through presynaptic alpha2A activation in the brainstem and medulla.The hypertensive response is generally not seen with oral administration and is described by the lecturer as occurring with parenteral administration.

Clonidine adverse effects, precautions, and withdrawal

  • Dry mouth and sedation are major adverse effects. They are centrally mediated, dose dependent, and occur in 50% of patients taking clonidine. The lecture notes a lower occurrence with transdermal use.
  • Delayed or retrograde ejaculation can result from modulation of NE release through peripheral presynaptic alpha2 receptors.
  • Marked bradycardia can occur in some patients because of increased parasympathetic tone.
  • Less common CNS effects include vivid dreams, nightmares, restlessness, and mental depression.
  • Transdermal clonidine can cause contact dermatitis.
  • Avoid in those at risk for mental depression.
  • TCAs can block the antihypertensive effect of clonidine. The lecture links this to blockade of neuronal NE uptake at sympathetic nerve terminals.
Withdrawal SyndromeAbrupt discontinuation after protracted high-dose use, especially >1 mg/day, may cause a hypertensive crisis.Symptoms listed by the lecture: headache, nervousness, apprehension, tremors, abdominal pain, sweating, tachycardia, and rebound hypertension.

Treatment of clonidine rebound and other therapeutic uses p. 3

  • Reinstitute oral clonidine at a slow or minimal effective dose.
  • Alpha and beta adrenoceptor blockers may be combined.
  • Beta blockers with alpha blocking properties such as Labetalol and Carvedilol may be used.
  • Sodium nitroprusside has a more rapid effect and is readily titrated.
Red High-Yield MarkerAdminister the alpha blocker first.Do not give beta blockers alone or before alpha blockers.The lecture explains that leaving vascular smooth muscle alpha activity unopposed can cause vasoconstriction → increased PVR and BP.

Therapeutic uses listed by the lecture

  • Major use: Hypertension.
  • Off-label: diabetes-associated diarrhea and diabetic neuropathy. The lecture states Clonidine increases NaCl and fluid absorption and decreases bicarbonate secretion.
  • Smoking cessation and opioid, alcohol, and tobacco withdrawal.
  • Menopausal hot flushes, using the transdermal patch.
  • Restless leg syndrome.
  • Tourette’s syndrome.

Methyldopa, Guanfacine, and Guanabenz

Lecture section III F–H. p. 3

Methyldopa

Chemistry and pharmacokinetics

  • Analog of L-DOPA.
  • Converted to alpha-methyldopamine and alpha-methylnorepinephrine.
  • These are active metabolites that act as false neurotransmitters in peripheral neurons.
  • They are stored in adrenergic vesicles, may replace NE, and are released by nerve stimulation.
  • Enters the brain through an aromatic amino transporter.
  • Bioavailability: 25%.
  • Half-life: about 2 hours.
  • Maximal effect after oral dosing: 4–6 hours. The effect may be delayed for 6–8 hours after oral or IV dosing.
  • Effect can persist up to 24 hours even after the parent drug disappears from circulation because the effect depends on stored metabolite in adrenergic vesicles.

Mechanism

alpha-methyl NE
→
Presynaptic alpha2 stimulation in brainstem
→
Decreased NE release
→
Decreased central sympathetic outflow → decreased BP

Molecular signaling: inhibition of adenylyl cyclase → decreased cAMP.

Methyldopa actions, adverse effects, and use

Actions and effects

  • Decreases PVR without much change in CO or HR.
  • Severe bradycardia may occur in patients with SA node dysfunction.
  • In elderly patients, CO may decrease because of decreased HR and stroke volume secondary to relaxation of vascular smooth muscle and veins with decreased preload.
  • Renal vascular resistance may decrease.
  • Decreases renin secretion from JG cells.
  • Prolonged use can cause salt and water retention or pseudo-tolerance through RAAS activation after decreased BP. The lecture notes this may be overcome by concurrent diuretic use.

Adverse effects and precautions

  • Most common undesirable effect: sedation at the onset of treatment.
  • Dry mouth.
  • Long-term persistent mental lassitude and impaired concentration.
  • Occasional mental depression, nightmares, vertigo, EPS, decreased libido, and lactation in men and women.
  • Lactation is associated with increased prolactin due to inhibition of dopaminergic mechanisms in the hypothalamus.
  • Positive Coombs test in 10–20% of patients after more than 12 months. Rarely associated with hemolytic anemia.
  • Hepatotoxicity associated with drug fever.
  • Discontinuation leads to prompt reversal of these abnormalities.
Therapeutic UseMethyldopa is the preferred drug for treatment of hypertension during pregnancy, based on effectiveness and safety in the mother and fetus.

Guanfacine

Pharmacokinetics

  • Well absorbed orally.
  • Large volume of distribution, which the lecture says implies extensive tissue uptake.
  • 50% excreted unchanged in urine.
  • Elimination half-life: 12–24 hours.

Mechanism and effects

  • Stimulates alpha2A receptors in the brainstem → decreased central sympathetic outflow → decreased BP.
  • Decreases adenylyl cyclase activity → decreased cAMP.
  • More selective for alpha2 receptors than Clonidine.
  • Similar long-term antihypertensive efficacy and adverse-effect pattern to Clonidine, but adverse effects are milder and less frequent.
  • Withdrawal can occur, but is less frequent and milder than Clonidine withdrawal. The lecture relates this to the longer half-life.

Therapeutic use

  • Hypertension, although the lecturer notes it is seldom used clinically.
  • ADHD in children 6–17 years old.
  • Most effective in children 12 years old or younger.
  • Second-line drug for ADHD and used with other ADHD medications.

Guanabenz

  • Chemically and pharmacologically related to Guanfacine.
  • Extensively metabolized by the liver. Half-life: 4–6 hours.
  • Blood pressure reduction is similar to Clonidine and Guanfacine.
  • Central presynaptic alpha2A activation → modulation of NE release → suppressed central sympathetic outflow → decreased BP.
  • Adverse effects such as dry mouth and drowsiness are similar to Clonidine.
  • Dose adjustment may be needed in hepatic cirrhosis.
  • Discontinued because of lethargy-related side effects.

Apraclonidine, Brimonidine, Rilmenidine, Moxonidine, and Tizanidine

Lecture section III I–L. pp. 3–4

Apraclonidine

  • Clonidine derivative also known as iodipine.
  • Does not cross the BBB.
  • Peak effect: 2 hours.
  • Hypotensive effect.
  • Half-life: 8 hours.

Mechanism

The exact mechanism is stated as unknown. The lecture relates its effect to alpha2 receptor-mediated decreased aqueous humor secretion by the ciliary body, constriction of blood vessels of the afferent ciliary process, and increased uveoscleral outflow.

Therapeutic indications

  • Used topically to decrease intraocular pressure with minimal systemic cardiovascular and pulmonary effects.
  • Short-term adjunctive therapy for open-angle glaucoma when IOP is not controlled by other pharmacologic agents.
  • Prevention or reduction of intraoperative and postoperative IOP increases before or after ocular laser trabeculoplasty or iridotomy.

Brimonidine

  • Not used as an antihypertensive agent.
  • Clonidine derivative similar to Apraclonidine.
  • Crosses the BBB, unlike Apraclonidine.
  • Can cause hypotension and sedation, but these are milder than with Clonidine.

Mechanism and indications

  • Decreases aqueous humor production and increases outflow through the uveoscleral pathway.
  • The lecture states a neuroprotective action that protects retinal ganglion cells and their projections independent of IOP effects.
  • Topically administered for ocular hypertension or open-angle glaucoma.
  • May be combined with a topically administered beta blocker such as Betaxolol or a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor such as Dorzolamide.

Rilmenidine and Moxonidine p. 4

  • Belong to the imidazolines group.
  • Used therapeutically as antihypertensive drugs.
  • Profiles of action similar to Clonidine.
  • May activate imidazoline I1 receptors like Clonidine.

Tizanidine

  • Centrally acting muscle relaxant.
  • The lecture lists treatment for muscle spasticity, ocular hypertension, and acute or chronic open- and closed-angle glaucoma.

Alpha Adrenergic Receptor Antagonists: General Pharmacology and Blockade

Lecture section IV. p. 4

Blockade of alpha1 adrenergic receptors

  • Inhibits catecholamine-induced vasoconstriction.
  • Produces vasodilation of arteriolar resistance vessels and veins.
  • Arteriolar and venous tone are determined largely by alpha receptors on vascular smooth muscle.
  • Blood pressure depends on cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance.

Alpha blockade in BPH and other effects

  • BPH: Alpha1 blockade decreases smooth muscle tone in the prostate and bladder neck → decreased resistance to urine outflow → increased urine flow.
  • The lecture notes a high proportion of alpha1A receptors in the prostate and lower urinary tract tissue.
  • May reduce hepatic glucose release, although catecholamine-driven glucose output is predominantly beta mediated with alpha contribution.
  • Alpha2 blockade facilitates insulin release by pancreatic beta islet cells so the beta2 response is unopposed.
  • Alpha1 blockade in radial smooth muscle promotes parasympathetic effects → miosis.
  • Alpha1 blockade in mucous membranes → nasal stuffiness.

Conventional Alpha Receptor Antagonist: Phentolamine

Lecture section V A. p. 4

Identity and receptor action

  • Synthetic imidazoline.
  • Reversible non-selective alpha receptor blocker.
  • Competitive antagonist at both alpha1 and alpha2 receptors with similar affinity at a ratio of 1:1.

Other effects and pharmacokinetics

Other effects

  • Inhibits responses to serotonin or 5-HT.
  • Stimulates muscarinic H1 and H2 receptors as written in the lecture.
  • Facilitates insulin release through alpha2 blockade.
  • Stimulates GI smooth muscle contraction and gastric acid secretion through alpha2 blockade.

Pharmacokinetics

  • Poor oral absorption.
  • Administered IV or IM.
  • Extensively metabolized in the liver.
  • Rapid onset and short duration.
  • Half-life after IV administration: about 19 minutes.

Adverse effects and toxicity

  • Major adverse effect: orthostatic hypotension, which may be acute or prolonged.
  • Compensatory cardiac stimulation → tachycardia, arrhythmias, and ischemic cardiac events.
  • Tachycardia is linked to both baroreceptor reflex and alpha2 blockade with increased NE release and beta1 stimulation.
  • GI stimulation can cause nausea, abdominal pain, and exacerbation of peptic ulcer. The lecture links this to histamine and muscarinic receptor stimulation.
  • Atropine antagonizes acetylcholine effects on muscarinic receptors only.
  • Contraindications listed: coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and history of peptic ulcer or acid peptic disease.
  • Male reproductive effect: retrograde ejaculation.
  • Nasal congestion due to parasympathetic dominance.

Phenoxybenzamine and Therapeutic Uses of Classical Alpha Blockers

Lecture section V B and class uses. pp. 4–5

Phenoxybenzamine identity and mechanism

  • Haloalkylamine alkylating agent related to nitrogen mustard.
  • Irreversible non-equilibrium blockade.
  • Covalently conjugates with alpha receptors with alpha1 to alpha2 affinity ratio of 100:1, favoring alpha1.
  • Inhibits neuronal and extraneuronal NE uptake through inhibition of the NE transporter or NET.
  • This decreases clearance of NE from sympathetic nerves.

Pharmacokinetics and pharmacologic effects

Pharmacokinetics

  • Lipid soluble.
  • Absorbed orally.
  • Bioavailability: about 20–30%.
  • Extensively metabolized in the liver.
  • Onset: 1 hour or more and slower than Phentolamine.
  • Duration: 14–48 hours.
  • Half-life: about 24 hours.

CVS effects

  • Alpha1 blockade → vasodilation from unopposed beta2 activity → decreased PVR → decreased BP.
  • Decreased BP triggers reflex sympathetic stimulation and reflex tachycardia.
  • Alpha2 blockade enhances NE release, accentuating the reflex increase in CO.
  • Produces epinephrine reversal.
  • Long-term use can produce a compensatory increase in blood volume and edema through RAAS activation.

CNS and other effects

  • Slow IV administration: sedation, fatigue, nausea.
  • Rapid IV administration: CNS excitation and increased motor activity.
  • At higher doses, the lecture states irreversible inhibition of responses to serotonin, acetylcholine, and histamine.

Phenoxybenzamine adverse effects

  • Major adverse effect: postural hypotension, often with reflex tachycardia and other arrhythmias.
  • Nasal stuffiness or congestion.
  • Retrograde ejaculation.
  • Mutagenic in the Ames Test, noted in experimental animal studies.

Therapeutic uses of classical alpha blockers

Pheochromocytoma

  • Major use of classical alpha blockers.
  • Before surgical removal, Phenoxybenzamine may be given for 1–3 weeks.
  • Other alpha1 blockers and beta blockers can be added several days after alpha blockade has begun.
  • During operative manipulation, Phentolamine can provide short-term control, but the lecture states Sodium nitroprusside is preferred because it is readily titrated and has a short duration.
  • For chronic inoperable or metastatic pheochromocytoma, Phenoxybenzamine may be used. Reversible selective alpha1 blockers and calcium channel blockers may also be used.
  • Beta blockers are used only after adequate alpha blockade.
  • Metyrosine may also be used. The lecture describes it as a competitive tyrosine hydroxylase inhibitor that can cross the CNS, decrease dopamine, and cause EPS.
Sequence RuleDo not give beta blockers initially or alone.Give alpha blockers before beta blockers.

Other lecture-listed uses

  • Pseudo-obstruction of the bowel, although the lecture notes better drugs are now used.
  • Reversal of local anesthesia with vasoconstrictor such as NE in soft tissues. Phentolamine was approved in 2008 by the US FDA for this use.
  • Prevention of dermal necrosis after inadvertent extravasation of an alpha agonist by blocking alpha1 and leaving beta2 effects unopposed.
  • Hypertensive crisis after Clonidine withdrawal or ingestion of large amounts of tyrosine-containing food. The lecture notes newer drugs with fewer adverse effects are preferred.
  • Male erectile dysfunction. Intracavernous Phentolamine can cause fibrotic reactions with long-term use. The lecture states it has been superseded by oral PDE5 reductase inhibitors such as Sildenafil, Tadalafil, and Vardenafil.
  • Peripheral vascular diseases such as Raynaud’s phenomenon and Berger’s disease. Phenoxybenzamine or Prazosin may be used, but calcium channel blockers are preferred because of adverse effects.

Common adverse effects and therapeutic indications

First-dose effect

  • Postural hypotension with syncopal episodes after the initial dose of an alpha1 blocker.
  • More often with quinazolines.
  • Less likely with Tamsulosin and Silodosin, but can occur with Alfuzosin.

Lipid profile

  • Quinazolines either improve or do not affect the plasma lipid profile.
  • The lecture calls them a drug of choice for dyslipidemic patients.

Indications

  • Mild to moderate hypertension: non-selective alpha1ABD blockers may be used.
  • Selective alpha1A blockers are described as not effective antihypertensive treatments and are used primarily when BPH is still amenable to pharmacologic management.
  • LUTS associated with BPH: primarily selective alpha1A blockers.
  • Non-selective alpha1ABD blockers can also be used when BPH is accompanied by mild hypertension.
  • Congestive heart failure and vasospastic disorders: Prazosin may be indicated for short-term use.

Contraindications and special adverse effects p. 6

  • Alfuzosin: increased risk of QT prolongation in susceptible individuals.
  • Do not use Alfuzosin concomitantly with CYP3A4 inhibitors such as Cimetidine and Ketoconazole because the lecture states these can increase Alfuzosin plasma levels.
  • Tamsulosin and Silodosin: increased incidence of abnormal retrograde ejaculation.
  • Tamsulosin and Silodosin may cause intraoperative floppy iris syndrome or IFIS.
  • The lecture describes IFIS as bellowing of a flaccid iris with tendency for iris prolapse and progressive intraoperative pupillary constriction.
Clinical Selection Cue from the LectureIf BPH is present without hypertension, use Alfuzosin, Tamsulosin, or Silodosin instead of Prazosin, Terazosin, or Doxazosin.

Prazosin and Its Congeners

Lecture section VI C. p. 6

Why reflex tachycardia is minimal or absent

  • Selective alpha1 blockade in arterioles and venules causes vasodilation and decreased PVR.
  • These drugs do not block presynaptic alpha2 receptors, so they do not promote NE release from sympathetic nerve endings.
  • They do not remove negative feedback or autoinhibition.
  • Decreased cardiac preload causes little tendency to increase CO and HR.
  • Prazosin relaxes both arteriolar and venous vascular smooth muscle.
  • Decreased central nervous system sympathetic outflow also contributes.

Metabolic and cardiac effects

  • Either improves or does not change plasma lipid profile and glucose-insulin metabolism in hypertensive patients at risk for atherosclerotic disease.
  • May increase HDL and decrease LDL and triglycerides. The mechanism is stated as unknown.
  • The lecture calls it a good drug choice in dyslipidemia.
  • In patients with pulmonary edema, Prazosin can increase cardiac output and decrease pulmonary congestion by dilating arteries and veins, reducing preload and afterload.
  • Prazosin may be used for short-term treatment of congestive heart failure.
  • Has limited application in hypertensive emergencies. The lecture notes Labetalol has been used for this condition.

Therapeutic indications

  • Non-selective alpha1ABD blockers such as Terazosin and Doxazosin: mild to moderate hypertension.
  • Concurrent mild hypertension and LUTS associated with BPH when still amenable to pharmacologic management.
  • Hypertension associated with pheochromocytoma.
  • Off-label medical expulsion therapy for distal ureteral calculi.
  • Alleviation of nightmares associated with post-traumatic stress disorder.
  • Antihydrotic in relation to SSRI use.
  • Chronic prostatitis.
  • Voiding dysfunction in men and women due to primary bladder neck obstruction.

Core receptor logic

  • Central alpha2A activation → decreased sympathetic outflow → decreased BP.
  • Alpha1 activation → vasoconstriction → increased TPR.
  • Beta2 activation → vasodilation → decreased TPR.
  • Alpha1 blockade → vasodilation → decreased PVR → decreased BP.
  • Presynaptic alpha2 activation allows negative feedback.
  • Alpha2 blockade removes autoinhibition → increased NE → increased HR and renin.

Must-know sequence

Red Lecture MarkersGive the alpha blocker first.Do not give beta blockers alone or before alpha blockade.

Applied in the lecture to Clonidine rebound treatment and pheochromocytoma preparation.

Clonidine

  • BA 95%, t½ 8–12 h.
  • Central alpha2A activation suppresses sympathetic outflow.
  • Dry mouth and sedation are major effects. Lecture frequency: 50%.
  • Transdermal patch gives less sedation. One application reduces BP for 7 days.
  • IV administration can produce transient vasoconstriction and an acute BP rise before prolonged hypotension.
  • Abrupt withdrawal after high-dose chronic use, especially >1 mg/day, can cause rebound hypertensive crisis.

Methyldopa

  • Analog of L-DOPA → alpha-methyldopamine and alpha-methylnorepinephrine false neurotransmitters.
  • BA 25%, t½ about 2 h, effect may persist 24 h.
  • Decreases PVR with little change in CO and HR.
  • Can cause sedation, EPS, lactation, positive Coombs test, and hepatotoxicity.
  • Preferred for hypertension during pregnancy.

Conventional alpha blockers

  • Phentolamine: reversible, alpha1 = alpha2, rapid and short acting, t½ about 19 min IV.
  • Phenoxybenzamine: irreversible, alpha1:alpha2 = 100:1, duration 14–48 h, t½ about 24 h.
  • Both can produce marked orthostatic hypotension and reflex tachycardia.
  • Pheochromocytoma: Phenoxybenzamine before surgery for 1–3 weeks. Phentolamine can be used intraoperatively, though Nitroprusside is preferred.

Selective alpha1 blockers

  • Prazosin, Terazosin, Doxazosin: alpha1A/B/D group used for mild hypertension and BPH.
  • Tamsulosin, Alfuzosin, Silodosin: primarily BPH.
  • First-dose effect = postural hypotension with syncope after initial dose.
  • Prazosin first-dose effect can occur at 30–90 min. Lecture risk reduction example: 1 mg at bedtime.
  • Alfuzosin: QT prolongation risk and avoid CYP3A4 inhibitors.
  • Tamsulosin and Silodosin: retrograde ejaculation and IFIS.
  • BPH with no hypertension: choose Alfuzosin, Tamsulosin, or Silodosin over Prazosin, Terazosin, or Doxazosin.

Yohimbine

  • Selective presynaptic alpha2 blocker.
  • Removes autoinhibition → increases NE → increases BP and HR.
  • t½ 0.25–2.5 h.
  • Contraindications: hypertension, angina, renal impairment.

Common adverse-effect map

  • Orthostatic hypotension.
  • Sedation or CNS depression.
  • Increased GI motility and diarrhea.
  • Sexual dysfunction with delayed or retrograde ejaculation.
  • Increased blood volume and sodium retention.
  • Nasal stuffiness.
  • EPS with catecholamine depletion, as listed for Methyldopa and Reserpine.
  • Conventional alpha blockers: intense reflex tachycardia.
