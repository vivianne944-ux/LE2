Overview

Lecture pp. 1–12

Lesson in one view

Sympathomimetic or adrenomimetic drugs mimic sympathetic nervous system activity by activating adrenergic receptors or by increasing the actions of endogenous catecholamines. The lecture organizes these agents by mode of action, receptor selectivity, and chemical structure.

The major clinical themes are control of blood pressure and shock, cardiac stimulation, bronchodilation, tocolysis, overactive bladder treatment, nasal decongestion, and selected CNS applications.

I–II

Foundations & Receptor Affinity

Lecture pp. 1–3, 12

Autonomic nervous system context p. 1

  • The sympathetic nervous system participates in regulation of all organ systems and is important in maintaining blood pressure, including during minor stress.
  • Sympathomimetic or adrenomimetic drugs are sympathetic nervous system agonists.
  • They mimic endogenous epinephrine and norepinephrine at corresponding adrenergic sites by activating adrenergic receptors and producing effects similar to adrenergic nerve stimulation.
  • They may be selective or nonselective.

  • Direct acting: catecholamines, phenylephrine, α1, α2, β2, β3 receptor agonists.
  • Mixed acting: ephedrine and phenylpropanolamine are lecture examples. The detailed table also includes pseudoephedrine, mephentermine, and metaraminol.
  • Indirect acting:
    • Release or displace catecholamines from adrenergic nerve endings.
    • Decrease catecholamine clearance by inhibiting neuronal reuptake.
    • Prevent enzymatic metabolism of norepinephrine.

Classification by receptor sensitivity p. 1

  • Selective: agonists preferentially act at α1, α2, β1, β2, β3, or dopamine receptors.
  • Nonselective: lecture examples are norepinephrine, epinephrine, isoproterenol, and dopamine.

Classification by chemical structure pp. 1, 9–10

  • Catecholamines: direct-acting agents with a catechol structure.
  • Endogenous catecholamines: NE, EPI, dopamine.
  • Synthetic catecholamines: isoproterenol, dobutamine.
  • Noncatecholamines: lack catechol structure and may be direct, indirect, or mixed acting.

III

Norepinephrine

Lecture pp. 2–3

Pharmacokinetics

  • Endogenous catecholamine and the primary neurotransmitter of the noradrenergic terminal.
  • Poorly absorbed across body membranes including the GI tract, so it is ineffective orally.
  • Given as an IV drip.
  • Mainly and rapidly inactivated by neuronal uptake through NET or uptake 1 and by enzymatic degradation through COMT and MAO.
  • Small amounts are normally found in urine. Excretion is greatly increased in pheochromocytoma.

Selectivity

  • α1 = α2 and β1 >> β2.
  • Activates β1 receptors with potency similar to epinephrine but has relatively little effect on β2 receptors.
  • It is a potent α agonist, although somewhat less potent than epinephrine at α receptors in most organs.
Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Vascular Resistance

Blood-pressure framework

  • α1 activation contracts vascular smooth muscle → vasoconstriction → ↑ PVR.
  • β2 activation relaxes selected vascular beds → vasodilation and increased flow in blood vessels of skeletal muscle.
  • Relevant sympathetic variables include CO, PVR, HR, SV, myocardial force of contraction, and venous tone.

Cardiovascular effects

  • ↑↑↑ systolic pressure, ↑↑ diastolic pressure, and ↑↑ mean arterial pressure.
  • The systolic rise reflects β1 activation in the heart with increased myocardial contraction and stroke volume.
  • β1 activation directly gives positive inotropic, chronotropic, and dromotropic actions.
  • ↑↑↑ PVR results from α1-mediated constriction of most vascular beds in venules and arterioles.
  • CO may decrease or remain unchanged.
  • At the height of the BP rise, the compensatory baroreceptor vagal reflex may overcome the direct positive chronotropic action and produce reflex bradycardia.
  • The positive inotropic effect is maintained because ventricular muscle receives little, if any, parasympathetic innervation.
Important to know: atropine before norepinephrine If atropine is given before norepinephrine, the typical vagal reflex may not be observed. Atropine blocks acetylcholine at cardiac muscarinic receptors and favors an increase in heart rate.

Toxicity, adverse effects, and precautions

  • Adverse effects are similar to epinephrine, but NE produces a greater BP increase because of the increase in PVR.
  • This can produce severe hypertension attributed to α stimulation.
  • Avoid extravasation. Intense α1-mediated vasoconstriction may cause tissue necrosis and sloughing.
📌 First-line vasopressor in shock Norepinephrine is the lecture’s first-line vasopressor when vasopressor support is needed for shock with sustained hypotension and evidence of hypoperfusion. Its predominantly α-adrenergic action with modest β-adrenergic effects can increase MAP with little change in HR or CO. Adequate fluid replacement and treatment of the underlying disease remain mainstays of shock treatment.

Clinical applications

  • Shock vasopressor: acceptable balance of α and β activity.
  • Local vasoconstrictor adjunct: may be incorporated with some local anesthetics to delay absorption, prolong nerve block duration, and reduce systemic toxicity of the local anesthetic.
Why pure α or pure β stimulation is not advised in shock Pure β stimulation can increase blood flow through β2 action but also increase myocardial ischemia risk through β1 stimulation. Pure α stimulation can raise vascular tone and BP but may decrease CO and impair tissue blood flow through vasoconstriction. The lecture states that dopamine has no advantage over NE because it is associated with a higher incidence of arrhythmias and mortality.
IV–V

Dopamine & Other Dopamine Receptor Agonists

Lecture pp. 3–4
📌 Dopamine as a CNS neurotransmitter Dopamine is important in movement regulation and is described in the lecture as participating in memory, mood, sleep, motivation, learning, attention, concentration, and emotional responses. The lecture also links dopamine to pleasure and satisfaction as part of the reward system.

Core identity and pharmacokinetics

  • Endogenous catecholamine, CNS neurotransmitter, and metabolic precursor of NE and EPI.
  • Polar and poorly absorbed across body membranes including the CNS.
  • Ineffective orally. Usually administered parenterally by IV route.
  • Substrate for MAO and COMT.
  • Injected dopamine usually has no central effects because it does not cross the BBB.

  • At intermediate doses, dopamine also causes NE release from nerve terminals, which contributes to cardiac effects.
Study aid derived from the lecture: D1 → β1 → α1 as the dose rises.

Renal effects at low dose

  • D1 receptor stimulation in renal tubules produces natriuresis.
  • The lecture links this to inhibition of the Na+–H+ exchanger and the Na+K+–ATPase pump.
  • The effect is augmented by increased renal blood flow and a small increase in GFR.

Adverse effects

  • Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea.
  • Anginal pain and arrhythmias.
  • Headache and hypertension.

Precautions and interactions

  • Correct hypovolemia before dopamine is given in shock.
  • Avoid extravasation during large infusions because α1 action can cause ischemic necrosis and tissue sloughing.
  • Avoid or reduce dose after MAO inhibitors.
  • Adjust dose with TCAs, which can inhibit NE reuptake through NET.

Clinical applications

  • Severe congestive heart failure, especially with oliguria or low or normal PVR.
  • Cardiogenic and septic shock, although the lecture emphasizes no advantage over norepinephrine in acute hypotension and notes higher arrhythmia incidence and mortality.
  • Parkinson disease is treated using levodopa + carbidopa rather than injected dopamine for central effect.
  • Levodopa is converted to dopamine in the brain.
  • Carbidopa, a decarboxylase inhibitor, prevents peripheral conversion of levodopa before it reaches the brain.

Fenoldopam

  • Benzazepine derivative.
  • Selective peripheral D1 agonist with moderate affinity for α2 adrenoceptors.
  • No significant affinity for D2, α1, or β1 receptors.
  • Produces vasodilation in selected vascular beds.
  • Rapidly acting IV vasodilator used primarily for severe hypertension, including malignant hypertension with end-organ damage.

Dopexamine

  • Synthetic analog of dopamine.
  • Intrinsic activity at D1, D2, and β2 receptors.
  • Inhibits catecholamine uptake.
  • Lecture lists therapeutic uses as the same as dopamine.
VI

β-Adrenergic Receptor Agonists

Lecture pp. 4–5

Isoproterenol: identity and pharmacokinetics

  • Considered a pure β receptor agonist with potent nonselective β activity and very low α affinity.
  • Synthetic catecholamine.
  • Not taken up by sympathetic neurons through NET.
  • Metabolized in liver and other tissues by COMT through uptake 2.
  • Relatively poor substrate for MAO.
  • Lecture half-life is approximately 2 hours.

Isoproterenol: cardiovascular effects

  • β2 activation: ↓↓ PVR through vasodilation in vascular smooth muscle supplying skeletal muscle and in renal and mesenteric vascular beds.
  • There is no opposing α1 effect, so diastolic pressure falls.
  • β1 activation: ↑↑ CO from positive inotropic, chronotropic, and dromotropic effects, increased myocardial contractility, direct sinus node stimulation, increased HR, and increased SV.
  • The lecture links cardiac effects to increased calcium influx in cardiac cells.
  • Net effect: slightly increased or unchanged systolic pressure, decreased diastolic pressure, decreased venous tone, decreased PVR, and decreased mean BP.

Isoproterenol: smooth muscle, adverse effects, and uses

  • Relaxation is prominent in bronchial, GI, and selected vascular smooth muscle.
  • β2 activation produces bronchodilation and enhanced skeletal muscle blood flow.
  • In asthma, part of its effect is attributed to inhibition of antigen-induced histamine and other inflammatory mediator release.
  • Adverse effects: palpitations, tachycardia, headache, flushing, cardiac ischemia, and arrhythmias.
  • Limited clinical use: emergency bradycardia or heart block while awaiting pacemaker insertion and selected ventricular arrhythmias such as torsade de pointes.
  • It has been replaced by other sympathomimetics for asthma, COPD, acute hypotension, and shock.

Dobutamine

  • Partial agonist used clinically as a racemate.
  • The (+) isomer is the more potent β1 agonist and is an α1 antagonist.
  • The (−) isomer is a potent α1 agonist.
  • Its structure resembles dopamine, but its actions are mediated mainly through α and β receptors.
  • Dobutamine is about 10-fold less effective at β2 receptors.
  • It has a more prominent inotropic than chronotropic effect compared with isoproterenol.
  • PVR does not decrease significantly because α1 effects can counterbalance α1 antagonistic action.

Dobutamine adverse effects

  • May increase BP and HR because of potent β1 activation.
  • Ventricular ectopic activity may develop.
  • Tolerance may develop.

Dobutamine therapeutic uses

  • Short-term treatment of cardiac decompensation after cardiac surgery or in CHF or acute MI.
  • Inotropic agent of choice when increased CO is needed.
  • Pharmacologic cardiac stress testing when the patient cannot exercise.
📌 Dobutamine high-yield Short-term cardiac decompensation is a key lecture indication. In stress testing, increased myocardial contractility and myocardial work can reveal ischemic areas by echocardiography or nuclear medicine techniques.
VII

β2-Selective Adrenoceptor Agonists

Lecture pp. 5–6

Class features

  • Selectivity is relative, not absolute.
  • Noncatecholamines and not substrates for COMT.
  • Differ from epinephrine by hydroxyl-group position on the aromatic ring and larger substitution on the terminal amino group.
  • Routes include parenteral, inhaled, and oral.
  • Longer duration of action and greater oral bioavailability than epinephrine.
  • The lecture notes that 40% of β receptors in the human heart are β2 receptors.
  • MOA: activation of adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP.

📌 Main clinical application Most SABAs and LABAs in this lecture are used for bronchospastic or bronchial-airway disease such as asthma and COPD.

General clinical applications

  • Bronchodilation in asthma and COPD: β2 activation relaxes bronchial smooth muscle and decreases airway resistance.
  • The lecture also lists suppression of leukotriene and histamine release from mast cells, enhanced mucociliary function, decreased microvascular permeability, and possible phospholipase A2 inhibition.
  • Tocolysis: uterine smooth muscle relaxation to delay premature labor. Lecture examples are terbutaline and ritodrine.
  • Peripheral vasodilation: β2 activation in vascular smooth muscle supplying skeletal muscle can enhance blood flow, although other drugs are now used for this purpose.

Adverse effects and precautions

  • Physiologic skeletal muscle tremor from increased muscle spindle discharge.
  • Restlessness, apprehension, anxiety, and tachycardia.
  • Cardiovascular risk is increased in the presence of MAO inhibitors. The lecture advises a 2-week interval between MAOI use and β2 agonists.
  • Severe pulmonary edema has been reported with ritodrine and terbutaline when used for premature labor.
  • Prolonged use is linked in the lecture to possible death or near death from asthma because of β2 receptor downregulation, bronchial hyperreactivity, and loss of efficacy.
  • May worsen hyperglycemia in some patients with diabetes.
  • Adverse effects are less likely with inhalation than with oral or parenteral therapy.

Terbutaline

  • Effective orally, subcutaneously, or by inhalation.
  • Noncatecholamine. Not metabolized by MAO or COMT.
  • Rapid onset after inhaled or parenteral administration. Oral onset is delayed 1–2 hours.
  • Duration 3–6 hours.
  • Uses: acute asthma or COPD bronchodilation and tocolysis.

Salbutamol or Albuterol

  • Pharmacologic properties and indications similar to terbutaline.
  • Inhaled bronchodilation within 15 minutes.
  • Effect persists 4–6 hours.
  • Used for acute breakthrough asthma symptoms.
  • Toxicity: tremor and tachycardia.

Formoterol

  • LABA, lipophilic, high affinity for β2 receptors.
  • Rapid onset within minutes after inhalation.
  • May persist for 12 hours.
  • Used with inhaled steroids such as budesonide or fluticasone for acute relief and maintenance therapy or MART in asthma and COPD.
  • Lecture calls it a drug of choice for nocturnal asthma.
  • Also used for prophylaxis of exercise-induced bronchospasm or asthma.

Salmeterol

  • Prolonged action >12 hours and relatively highly selective for β2 receptors.
  • Highly lipophilic.
  • Slow onset compared with formoterol.
  • Not suitable for monotherapy of acute breakthrough bronchospasm because of slow onset and lack of anti-inflammatory action.
  • Lecture dose note: not more than twice daily.
  • Used for nocturnal asthma and COPD.

Ritodrine and other long-acting β2 agonists

  • Ritodrine: β2-selective tocolytic used to arrest premature labor and prolong pregnancy. The lecture notes voluntary withdrawal from the US market and possible increased maternal cardiovascular morbidity or mortality.
  • Other uterine relaxants listed: terbutaline, magnesium sulfate, nifedipine, atosiban, and nitric oxide donors.
  • Arformoterol: long-term treatment of asthma and COPD in the lecture text. The lecture notes removal of the black-box warning from ICS/LABA combinations while LABA monotherapy in asthma remained a concern.
  • Indacaterol: ultra-long acting with fast onset and once-daily dosing. Used as monotherapy for COPD and combined with ICS for asthma in the lecture.
  • Carmoterol: rapid onset and long duration. Five times more selective for β2 than β1. For asthma and COPD.
  • Olodaterol and Vilanterol: ultra-long-acting agents approved for COPD in the lecture.
VIII

β3-Selective Adrenoceptor Agonists

Lecture p. 6

Mirabegron

  • MOA: activates adenylyl cyclase → ↑ cAMP.
  • Prejunctional β3 receptors inhibit acetylcholine release from parasympathetic neurons during bladder filling.
  • Reduces bladder tone and relaxes detrusor smooth muscle during the storage phase → ↑ bladder storage capacity.
  • Lecture dose: oral initial 25 mg once daily.
  • Elimination half-life: about 50 hours.
  • Adverse effect: possible hypertension.
  • Use: overactive bladder with urinary incontinence, urgency, and frequency.

Vibegron

  • Approved in Japan in 2018 and by the US FDA in 2020 according to the lecture.
  • Lecture dose: 75 mg once daily for overactive bladder.
  • Elimination half-life: approximately 60–70 hours.
  • Adverse-event incidence is described as generally comparable with mirabegron.
  • Improves micturition similarly to mirabegron.
  • Metabolized independently from CYP3A4, 2D6, and 2C9. Predominantly by oxidation and glucuronidation.

Agents under clinical development

  • Solabegron: for treatment of overactive bladder and irritable bowel syndrome.
  • Ritobegron: listed as a prodrug.
If you see overactive bladder Think β3 agonists: mirabegron and vibegron. Lecture mechanism: detrusor relaxation during storage → increased bladder capacity.

The lecture emphasizes that α activity is unopposed by β2 vasodilation in these vascular smooth muscles.

📌 Phenylephrine caution Be careful in hypertensive patients. Phenylephrine can increase blood pressure through α1-mediated vasoconstriction.

Phenylephrine: pharmacology and cardiovascular effects

  • Selective α1 agonist. β receptors are activated only at higher concentrations.
  • Noncatecholamine, so it is not inactivated by COMT.
  • Greater bioavailability and longer duration than catecholamines.
  • Lipid soluble and orally bioavailable.
  • Cardiovascular profile resembles norepinephrine in the lecture: ↑ PVR, ↑ systolic pressure, ↑ diastolic pressure, ↑ mean BP, and ↑ venous tone.
  • Myocardial contractile force is not directly increased at usual doses because β1 receptors are not activated.
  • May produce reflex bradycardia and reduced CO.

Phenylephrine clinical applications

  • Nasal decongestant by oral, drop, or spray routes.
  • Lecture OTC examples: Bioflu and Neozep contain phenylephrine with chlorphenamine maleate and paracetamol.
  • Adjunct to local anesthetic: delays absorption, prolongs duration, and reduces systemic toxicity.
  • Mydriatic without cycloplegia: contracts the radial muscle of the iris and can assist fundus examination.
  • Hemostatic agent: topical use in nasal packs for epistaxis or gingival string for gingivectomy.
  • Vasopressor: increases vascular tone and BP but may impair tissue blood flow. The lecture notes that it is not widely used for this purpose because better drugs can provide both α and β effects.
📌 Topical nasal decongestants: rebound congestion Repeated use of high-concentration topical sprays for more than 3–5 days can produce intense vasoconstriction and tissue hypoxia or ischemia. Local mediator release may then cause vasodilation, hyperemia, and rebound congestion or rhinitis medicamentosa.

Topical versus oral nasal decongestants

  • Topical α1 activation in nasal mucosa causes vasoconstriction, decreases mucosal volume, and reduces resistance to airflow.
  • α2 receptors may mediate arteriolar contraction and decrease nutrient supply to nasal mucosa.
  • Possible rebound mechanisms listed in the lecture are receptor desensitization and structural mucosal damage.
  • Long acting topical: oxymetazoline, xylometazoline.
  • Short acting topical: phenylephrine, tetrahydrozoline, naphazoline.
  • Oral agents have longer duration and lower local concentrations but greater potential for cardiac and CNS effects than topical preparations.
  • Other oral mixed-acting decongestants listed are pseudoephedrine and ephedrine.

Phenylpropanolamine in the nasal-decongestant discussion

  • High-dose phenylpropanolamine can precipitate hemorrhagic stroke through intense α1 vasoconstriction, increased PVR, and increased BP.
  • The lecture contrasts high doses used elsewhere with lower dose strengths in Philippine cold preparations.

Midodrine

  • Selective α1 agonist and a prodrug.
  • Produces active metabolite desglymidodrine.
  • Peak concentration around 1 hour after an oral dose.
  • Duration 4–6 hours.
  • Primary indication: autonomic insufficiency and chronic postural hypotension.
  • Frequent complication: supine hypertension.
  • The lecture suggests minimizing supine hypertension by avoiding dosing before bedtime and elevating the head of the bed.
  • Droxidopa is a synthetic molecule converted to NE and is presented as another approach for neurogenic postural hypotension.

Mixed-Acting Sympathomimetic Agents

Lecture pp. 7–8

Core mechanism

Mixed-acting agents act on adrenergic pathways both directly and indirectly.

  • Direct: stimulate adrenergic receptors on target tissues.
  • Indirect: promote release of norepinephrine at sympathetic nerve endings.

Ephedrine: pharmacokinetics and mechanism

  • Noncatecholamine.
  • Weak base. Urinary excretion can be enhanced by acidification of urine.
  • High bioavailability because it is lipid soluble.
  • Relatively long duration and can enter the CNS.
  • Eliminated in urine largely unchanged.
  • Lecture half-life: 3–6 hours.
  • Direct agonist at α and β receptors.
  • Indirectly enhances release of NE from storage vesicles at sympathetic nerve endings.
  • Potent CNS stimulant.

Ephedrine: pharmacologic effects

  • Effects resemble epinephrine but are less efficacious.
  • CVS: ↑ CO because of ↑ HR, variably ↑ PVR, and ↑ BP.
  • PVR depends on the balance between α1 and β2 activation and is not as dramatically increased as with some other sympathomimetics.
  • Respiratory: bronchodilation through β2 receptors.
  • Genitourinary: urinary retention through α1 stimulation of bladder base, urethral sphincter, and prostate smooth muscle.
  • Eyes: mydriasis through α1 activation.

Ephedrine adverse effects

  • Tachyphylaxis with repetitive dosing because indirect action can deplete releasable substrate.
  • Hypertension from increases in CO and PVR.
  • Insomnia because the lipid-soluble drug crosses the BBB and stimulates the CNS.

Ephedrine uses

  • Replaced by more selective β2 agonists for asthma.
  • Stimulant in narcolepsy.
  • Promote urinary continence.
📌 Herbal ephedrine warning The lecture warns that ingestion of large amounts of Ma huang or Ephedra preparations containing ephedrine can be a greater toxicity concern.

Pseudoephedrine

  • Stereoisomer of ephedrine.
  • Can stimulate both α and β receptors.
  • Effective oral nasal decongestant.
  • Regulated because it can be misused as a precursor for illicit methamphetamine manufacture.
  • The lecture lists a loratadine combination, Clarinase, while noting uncertainty about current Philippine availability.

Phenylpropanolamine

  • Common oral nasal decongestant in the lecture.
  • Pharmacologic properties similar to ephedrine and approximately equal potency.
  • Less CNS stimulation than ephedrine.
  • High doses can markedly raise BP through intense α1 activation and increase the risk of hemorrhagic stroke.

Lecture-specific classification note The lecture explicitly notes that reuptake inhibitors and enzyme inhibitors in this scheme are not actually sympathomimetic drugs, although they can produce sympathomimetic-like effects.

Amphetamine

  • Pharmacokinetics resemble ephedrine, but it enters the CNS more readily.
  • D-isomer is more potent than the L-isomer.
  • MOA: release of NE and, to some extent, dopamine.
  • CNS effects: increased mood and alertness, appetite suppression, decreased sense of fatigue, increased ability to concentrate, euphoria, increased motor and speech activity, and improved physical performance.
  • Adverse effects: large prolonged doses are followed by depression and fatigue. Chronic use can produce psychological dependence. Serious effects include psychotic reactions with vivid hallucinations and paranoid delusions. Higher doses can cause overt psychosis.
  • Uses: narcolepsy and ADHD.

Methamphetamine and phenmetrazine

  • Methamphetamine: similar to amphetamine with more pronounced central effects and less prominent peripheral reactions. High abuse potential. Also promoted as an anorexiant.
  • Phenmetrazine: promoted as an appetite suppressant for weight reduction in some countries and described as a popular drug of abuse.

Methylphenidate

  • Structurally related to amphetamine.
  • Pharmacologic properties and abuse potential are similar.
  • Mild CNS stimulant with more prominent effects on mental than motor activity.
  • Uses: narcolepsy and ADHD.
  • Contraindicated in patients with glaucoma.

Modafinil

  • Psychostimulant and CNS stimulant with a structure and neurochemical profile different from amphetamine.
  • MOA is not fully known in the lecture.
  • Inhibits NE and dopamine transporters, leading to increased synaptic NE, dopamine, 5-HT, and glutamate with decreased GABA.
  • Can cause mild increases in BP and HR.
  • Uses: narcolepsy, obstructive sleep apnea hypopnea syndrome, and shift-work sleep disorder.

Tyramine

  • Vasoactive amine and not a drug.
  • Normal by-product of tyrosine metabolism and can be produced by fermentation of protein-rich foods.
  • Lecture food examples: aged cheese, cured meats, pickled foods, beer, and yeast supplements.
  • Normally has low oral bioavailability because MAO in the liver and gut wall produces a high first-pass effect.
  • At high concentrations, tyramine can enter nerve terminals through NET, enter storage vesicles through monoamine transport, and promote release of stored NE.
  • Spectrum of action resembles norepinephrine.
  • Large intake can precipitate a hypertensive crisis.
  • Patients taking MAO inhibitors should avoid tyramine-containing foods because reduced tyramine metabolism can markedly increase BP and precipitate hypertensive crisis.

XII

Clinical Uses of Adrenergic Drugs

Lecture p. 9

α receptor stimulation

  • Acute hypotension and shock: norepinephrine is the lecture’s first-choice vasopressor because of balanced α and β activity.
  • Chronic orthostatic hypotension: midodrine.
  • Ophthalmic mydriasis without cycloplegia: phenylephrine.
  • Glaucoma: brimonidine and apraclonidine.
  • Nasal decongestion: phenylephrine. Topical naphazoline, tetrahydrozoline, oxymetazoline, xylometazoline. Oral phenylpropanolamine. Regulated pseudoephedrine.
  • Adjunct to local anesthetic: epinephrine, norepinephrine, phenylephrine, levonordefrin. Lecture dilution range: 1:100,000 to 1:200,000.
  • Local hemostatic: epinephrine. Cocaine is also listed for selected oronasopharyngeal procedures because it combines local anesthetic and local hemostatic actions.
  • Paroxysmal atrial tachycardia: slow IV phenylephrine, although the lecture notes that safer alternatives generally exist.
  • Hypertension: centrally acting α2 agonists such as clonidine, methyldopa, and guanfacine.
Do not confuse mydriasis patterns Antimuscarinic drugs can produce mydriasis with cycloplegia. Sympathomimetic drugs can produce mydriasis without cycloplegia.

β receptor stimulation

  • Asthma and COPD: SABA and LABA bronchodilators. The lecture uses formoterol + budesonide as an example for MART.
  • Peripheral vascular disease: β-mediated vasodilation, although the lecture says better drugs are now used.
  • Tocolysis: ritodrine, terbutaline, salbutamol, isoxsuprine, magnesium sulfate, and atosiban are listed for premature labor. Terbutaline and ritodrine are highlighted for threatened abortion or delaying premature labor.
  • Overactive bladder: β3 agonists mirabegron and vibegron. The lecture also lists antimuscarinics solifenacin, darifenacin, and imidafenacin.
  • Complete heart block: epinephrine.
  • Resuscitation from cardiac arrest: epinephrine, especially where cardioversion and defibrillation are unavailable.
  • Acute decompensated heart failure: dobutamine and dopamine.
📌 Anaphylaxis Drug of choice: epinephrine as a physiologic or functional antagonist. Lecture concentration: 1 mg/mL or 1:1000 aqueous solution. Lecture dose: 0.3–0.5 mg.
📌 Cardiogenic shock Dobutamine is the inotropic agent of choice when increased cardiac output is needed.

Other lecture-listed applications

  • Cardiac stress test: dobutamine.
  • ADHD: methylphenidate, atomoxetine, possible modafinil, and α2 agonists clonidine and guanfacine in children.
  • Narcolepsy: modafinil and armodafinil.
  • Obesity: amphetamine and amphetamine-like preparations. The lecture notes phenylpropanolamine is not approved in the Philippines for this indication.
XIII + lecture summary

Integration & Schematic Effects

Lecture pp. 9–11

How the lecture says to predict effects

  • The overall pharmacologic effect depends on relative receptor affinity, receptor expression in the tissue, and route of administration.
  • The magnitude of response depends on receptor number and function at the cell surface, receptor regulation by catecholamines and other hormones or drugs, dose, age, and the number of diseases.

Mode

  • Direct: activate receptors.
  • Mixed: activate receptors + release NE.
  • Indirect: increase endogenous catecholamine availability.

Structure

  • Catecholamines: generally polar and poorly absorbed orally.
  • Noncatecholamines: generally more lipid soluble, more orally bioavailable, and longer acting.

Selectivity

  • Selective: preferential effect at one subtype at concentrations with little effect on another.
  • Nonselective: interact with more than one subtype at usual concentrations.

Pharmacokinetic integration

  • Catecholamines: polar, poor oral absorption, rapid inactivation in GI mucosa and liver, poor CNS distribution, usually given topically, by inhalation, or parenterally.
  • Noncatecholamines: generally lipid soluble, orally bioavailable, longer acting, may cross the BBB, and can be given by oral, parenteral, topical, or inhaled routes.
  • Direct and indirect sympathomimetics may be subject to MAO and COMT metabolism.
  • Phenylephrine and β2 agonists are not metabolized by COMT.

Fast integration cues from the lecture

  • Norepinephrine: α1, α2, β1. Raises PVR, systolic BP, and diastolic BP. Reflex bradycardia can overcome direct positive chronotropy.
  • Dopamine: D1 at low dose, β1 at intermediate dose, α1 at high dose.
  • Isoproterenol: strong β action with essentially no α effect. Mean BP and diastolic BP fall as vasodilation develops.
  • Dobutamine: more inotropic than chronotropic. Inotropic agent of choice when CO must be increased.
  • β2 agonists: bronchodilation, selected vascular smooth muscle relaxation, tremor and tachycardia as common adverse effects.
  • α1 agonists: vasoconstriction and BP elevation. Phenylephrine can cause reflex bradycardia.

Cram Sheet

Lecture pp. 1–12
BP = CO × PVR

Receptor → effect

  • α1: vasoconstriction → ↑ PVR and BP.
  • β1: positive inotropy, chronotropy, and dromotropy.
  • β2: bronchodilation and selected vascular smooth muscle relaxation.
  • β3: detrusor relaxation during storage → ↑ bladder capacity.
  • D1: renal, splanchnic, coronary, and cerebral vasodilation.

Mode of action

  • Direct: activate adrenoceptors.
  • Mixed: activate receptors + release NE.
  • Indirect: release NE, inhibit reuptake, or inhibit MAO or COMT.
