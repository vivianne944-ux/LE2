Absorption · Distribution · Metabolism

Pharmacokinetics of β Blockers

Absorption and bioavailability p. 2

  • Most β blockers are well absorbed orally. Peak concentration is usually reached in 1 to 3 hours.
  • Propranolol and Metoprolol have sustained-release preparations that allow once-daily intake.
  • Oral bioavailability is limited to varying degrees for most drugs.
  • The lecture lists Betaxolol, Pindolol, Penbutolol, and Sotalol as exceptions to the usual limitation in bioavailability.
Propranolol first-pass metabolism Propranolol has low oral bioavailability because of extensive hepatic first-pass metabolism. Its hepatic extraction mechanism can become saturated. As dose increases, plasma level and oral bioavailability can rise because hepatic extraction declines. The lecture uses a large oral-versus-IV dose difference as a clue to extensive first-pass metabolism.

Lipophilic drugs p. 2

  • Nonpolar and highly lipid soluble.
  • Cross body membranes and have a large volume of distribution from extensive extravascular tissue uptake.
  • Can traverse the blood-brain barrier and produce CNS effects.
  • Highly lipophilic: Propranolol, Penbutolol.
  • Moderately lipophilic: Metoprolol, Carvedilol.

Hydrophilic drugs p. 2

  • Poorly lipid soluble.
  • Have a lower volume of distribution.
  • The general pharmacokinetic section states that they do not traverse the BBB.
  • Examples: Nadolol, Atenolol, Esmolol.

Inverse agonism Betaxolol, Metoprolol, Propranolol, and Nadolol are listed as exhibiting inverse agonism in some tissues. The lecture describes them as stabilizing the inactive receptor conformation and reducing constitutive β-receptor activity below baseline.

Cardiovascular effects of β1 blockade p. 2

  • ↓ myocardial force of contraction and ↓ heart rate.
  • Usually does not lower BP much in normotensive individuals, but it lowers BP in patients with hypertension.
  • During exercise or stress, β1 blockade blunts the expected sympathetic rise in heart rate.
  • Short term: ↓ CO from β1 blockade. Nonselective β2 blockade can increase PVR through unopposed α1 vasoconstriction.
  • Long term: the lecture links the antihypertensive effect to adaptation in resistance vessels and a delayed or sustained decrease in peripheral resistance.
  • Indicators of propranolol β blockade include resting bradycardia and reduced exercise heart rate.

Nonselective β blockade and epinephrine p. 3

BP = CO × PVR. The lecture uses this hydraulic equation to explain that α1 stimulation raises PVR through vasoconstriction, while β2 stimulation lowers PVR through vasodilation.

Nonselective β blockers can augment the pressor response to epinephrine because β2-mediated vasodilation is blocked and α1-mediated vasoconstriction remains unopposed.

Pheochromocytoma · red-exclamation point Use β blockers only after adequate α receptor blockade has been established. Giving β blockade first can drive catecholamine effects toward unopposed α receptor agonism. The lecture warns of fatal vasoconstriction and hypertensive crises.

Clinical behavior of selectivity · ISA · MSA

Selectivity, Intrinsic Sympathetic Activity, and Membrane Stabilizing Activity

Cardioselective β1 blockers p. 3

The lecture prefers β1-selective blockers when a β blocker is needed in patients with:

  • Bronchospasm
  • Diabetes
  • Peripheral vascular disease or Raynaud’s phenomenon

NAME CAB: Nebivolol, Atenolol, Metoprolol, Esmolol, Celiprolol, Acebutolol, Betaxolol.

Metoprolol is described as equipotent to propranolol at β1 blockade, but 50–100 fold less potent at β2 blockade.

Asthma caveat No currently available β1 antagonist is described as sufficiently specific to completely avoid β2 interaction. The lecture therefore states that β blockers should be avoided in asthma if possible. Some patients with COPD or concomitant ischemic heart disease may have benefit that exceeds risk.

CNS effects p. 4

  • Mild sedation
  • Vivid dreams and nightmares
  • Fatigue
  • Depression is listed as rare

The lecture states that CNS effects are less likely with hydrophilic β blockers such as Nadolol, Atenolol, and Esmolol.

Other systemic effects p. 4

  • Worsening of pre-existing asthma and other obstructive airway disease with nonselective β blockers
  • Hypoglycemia, more often with nonselective blockers
  • Slight weight gain
  • Mild negative metabolic and lipid changes, described as absent in vasodilating β blockers
  • Sexual dysfunction
Bronchospasm · red-exclamation point Major respiratory adverse effect: bronchoconstriction from β2 blockade in bronchial smooth muscle. The effect can be life-threatening in asthma or COPD. The lecture states that selective β1 blockers and drugs with β2 ISA are less likely to cause bronchospasm, but β blockers should still be avoided in asthma if possible.

Carbohydrate metabolism and hypoglycemia p. 3

  • β blockade can partially inhibit hepatic glycogenolysis, especially through β2 blockade.
  • Nonselective blockade can delay recovery from insulin-induced hypoglycemia.
  • β2 receptors involved in glucagon secretion can also be blocked by nonselective agents.
  • Nonselective β blockers can mask warning signs of hypoglycemia such as tremor, tachycardia, sweating, and nervousness.
  • Propranolol causes more prolonged hypoglycemia than a selective β1 blocker such as Metoprolol.
Diabetes caution · red-exclamation point Use with great caution in insulin-dependent diabetes. Use with great caution in patients prone to hypoglycemic reactions. Use with great caution in pancreatectomized patients and those with inadequate glucagon reserves. The lecture states β blockers are much safer in Type 2 diabetes without hypoglycemic episodes.

Lipid effects p. 3

  • β1 and β3 blockade can reduce triglyceride lipase activity and reduce release of free fatty acids.
  • Nonselective β blockers are described as causing ↓ HDL, ↑ VLDL, ↑ triglycerides, and generally no LDL change.
  • The lecture links this to a lower HDL to LDL ratio and increased coronary artery disease risk.
  • These changes are less likely with β blockers that have ISA.
  • Newer β blockers listed as less likely to have negative metabolic effects: Carvedilol, Nebivolol, Metoprolol, Bisoprolol.
Withdrawal syndrome · red-exclamation point Abrupt discontinuation after long-term use can worsen angina and increase risk of sudden death. Manifestations include nervousness, tachycardia, increased BP, and increased angina intensity. Gradually decrease the dose over several weeks and restrict exercise during this period.

Third-generation drugs with α1 + β activity

Mixed Alpha and Beta Receptor Blockers

Class concept pp. 1, 5–6

These drugs competitively block β receptors and, with lower affinity, α1 receptors. The lecture repeatedly emphasizes that β blockade is stronger than α1 blockade for this mixed group.

Main examples: Carvedilol, Labetalol, Bucindolol.

Final tab · Rapid high-yield recall

Cram Sheet

Red-exclamation essentials Pheochromocytoma: α blockade first, then β blockade. β blockade first can cause unopposed α effects and hypertensive crisis. MSA: do not use significant local-anesthetic activity topically in the eye because protective corneal reflexes can be eliminated. Asthma: β2 blockade can cause life-threatening bronchospasm. β1 selectivity lowers but does not eliminate the risk. Avoid β blockers in asthma if possible. Withdrawal: do not abruptly stop chronic β blockers. Taper over several weeks and restrict exercise during the taper. Diabetes: nonselective blockers can delay recovery and mask hypoglycemia. Use great caution in insulin-dependent patients, patients prone to hypoglycemia, pancreatectomized patients, and those with poor glucagon reserve. Propranolol: not recommended for pregnant women in the lecture. Not for hypertensive emergency or crisis. Migraine use is prophylaxis, not acute treatment. Esmolol: ultra short acting β1-selective IV blocker with a 10–15 minute half-life and RBC esterase metabolism. Topical glaucoma β blockers: use caution even topically in bradycardia, heart block, heart failure, asthma, or obstructive airway disease.

Exam traps and cautions
TrapCorrect lecture cue
“β1-selective means no β2 effect”False. Selectivity diminishes at higher concentrations.
“Give β blocker first in pheochromocytoma”Do not. Establish α blockade first.
“β blockers treat acute migraine”No. The listed drugs are for prophylaxis.
“Topical β blocker has no systemic caution”No. Caution remains in bradycardia, heart block, heart failure, asthma, and obstructive airway disease.
“Stop chronic β blocker suddenly if symptoms improve”No. Taper gradually because rebound can worsen angina and raise sudden-death risk.
“Nonselective β blocker is ideal in recurrent hypoglycemia”No. Nonselective blockade can prolong hypoglycemia and mask warning signs.
