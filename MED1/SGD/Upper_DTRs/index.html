1. Conceptual rundown BEFORE the demonstration

You do not need to begin with the reflex hammer. For a graded demonstration, it is stronger to first give the examiner a short conceptual framework that explains the bedside technique, then immediately demonstrate it.

Suggested balance: about 2–3 minutes of concepts, then make the live demonstration the majority of your assigned time. Keep detailed pathology and localization for the post-demo discussion or for questions.
New presentation order: terminology → reflex arc → grading & asymmetry → technique → reinforcement → live demonstration → interpretation. This means your audience knows how you will judge and report the response before you start tapping.
~40–50 sec

A. Terminology: why DeMyer says MSR instead of DTR

DeMyer prefers muscle stretch reflex (MSR) because the tendon is not the receptor. The tendon is struck because it transmits a sudden stretch to the attached muscle. The stretch is detected by muscle spindles.

“Although these are traditionally called deep tendon reflexes, DeMyer uses the term muscle stretch reflex because the receptor is the muscle spindle. The tendon is simply a convenient way to transmit a rapid stretch to the muscle.”

Link to your demo: your goal is not “hit the tendon hard.” Your goal is “produce a sudden, controlled stretch.”

~50–60 sec

B. What happens physiologically after the sudden stretch

Rapid muscle stretch→ Muscle spindle discharge→ Afferent volley→ Activation of appropriate LMNs→ Muscle contraction

In DeMyer’s explanation, a slow stretch produces less synchronized spindle discharge and the spindles adapt to the new muscle length. A rapid, nearly instantaneous stretch makes many spindles discharge together, producing a strong afferent volley that activates the relevant lower motor neurons.

“A rapid stretch causes many muscle spindles to discharge almost simultaneously, producing a strong afferent volley. The resulting motor response is contraction of the muscle being tested.”

Link to your demo: this is why a crisp swing works better than a slow push or stiff peck.

~60 sec

C. How the physiology determines your examination technique

What you doWhy it matters
Support and relax the limbGives a controlled resting condition and prevents voluntary holding from altering the response.
Use an intermediate joint angleDeMyer notes that joint angle changes muscle/tendon mechanics and reflex response; about 90° at the elbow is a useful starting position.
Locate the tendon before strikingMakes the stimulus accurate and reproducible instead of random.
Use a loose-wrist, crisp swingThe adequate stimulus is sudden stretch, not brute force.
Compare the same reflex right vs leftDeMyer emphasizes direct comparison because normal reflex magnitude varies and asymmetry is clinically meaningful.
“Because the adequate stimulus is rapid stretch, I need the patient relaxed, the limb supported, and the hammer delivered as a crisp swing rather than a peck. I will also compare corresponding reflexes on both sides.”
~20 sec

D. Tell the audience what you are going to demonstrate

DeMyer’s upper-extremity MSR outline includes biceps C5–C6, brachioradialis C5–C6, triceps C7–C8, and finger flexion C7–T1.

“With those principles in mind, I’ll now demonstrate the upper-extremity muscle stretch reflexes and correlate each maneuver with the segment assessed and the expected motor response.”
Concepts established → pick up the reflex hammer → proceed to the demonstration.

What should stay AFTER the demo?

Do not front-load every cause of hyporeflexia, hyperreflexia, and upper- versus lower-motor-neuron disease before touching the patient. For a demonstration-focused presentation, keep the pre-demo section to terminology, muscle-spindle physiology, the reflex pathway, relaxation, hammer mechanics, and symmetry. Use the later background section for abnormal-reflex interpretation.

If the doctor asks: “Why explain the physiology first?”

“Because the physiology determines the technique. If the adequate stimulus is sudden muscle stretch, then relaxation, limb position, accurate tendon localization, and a crisp hammer swing are necessary to create and interpret the reflex properly.”

2. MSR physiology and reflex arc — deeper review

DeMyer prefers the term muscle stretch reflex (MSR) instead of “deep tendon reflex,” because the receptor is the muscle spindle, not the tendon. The tendon is only the structure you strike to transmit a sudden stretch to the muscle.

Sudden stretch→ Muscle spindle activated→ Afferent volley→ LMN activation→ Muscle contraction
Clinical line to memorize: “The hammer is not meant to hit hard. It is meant to create a sufficiently rapid stretch.”
If the doctor asks: “Why must the tap be rapid?”

DeMyer explains that a slow stretch allows spindle discharges to occur less synchronously and adapt to the new muscle length. A rapid stretch makes many spindles fire together, producing a strong enough afferent volley to generate the visible reflex.

If the doctor asks: “Why not strike the muscle belly instead?”

Direct muscle percussion can cause percussion irritability, which is not the same as an MSR. Striking the tendon is used to produce sudden spindle stretch and to test the reflex arc more specifically.

3. Grading, symmetry, and reporting — explain this before the demo

Before you start the live maneuvers, tell the audience how the responses will be described. This makes your demonstration easier to follow because they already know what a “2+ symmetric” or “3+ on the right” means when you report it.

Most important DeMyer principle: a right-to-left comparison is often more informative than the absolute number. Do not look at “2+” or “3+” in isolation; compare the corresponding reflex on the two sides.

How to report NORMAL symmetric upper-extremity reflexes

“Biceps, brachioradialis, and triceps reflexes are 2+ and symmetric bilaterally.”

If reporting each separately during the demo:

“Biceps reflexes are 2+ bilaterally and symmetric.”
“Brachioradialis reflexes are 2+ bilaterally and symmetric.”
“Triceps reflexes are 2+ bilaterally and symmetric.”

How to report ASYMMETRY

Do not average the two sides into one grade. State the reflex, the right grade, the left grade, and which side is relatively increased or decreased.

Relatively reduced on the left
“Biceps reflex: right 2+, left 1+; asymmetric, reduced on the left.”
Relatively brisk on the right
“Triceps reflex: right 3+, left 2+; asymmetric, brisker on the right.”
Absent on one side
“Brachioradialis reflex: right 2+, left 0; asymmetric, absent on the left.”

How to summarize a pattern involving several reflexes

“Upper-extremity MSRs are asymmetric: biceps R 2+/L 1+, brachioradialis R 2+/L 1+, and triceps R 2+/L 2+, with relative hyporeflexia of the left biceps and brachioradialis responses.”
Do not jump straight from asymmetry to a diagnosis. First report exactly what you found. Localization comes after you correlate the reflex pattern with strength, tone, atrophy, fasciculations, and sensory findings.

What you can say before the live demo

“I will grade the reflexes from 0 to 4+ and compare corresponding reflexes on both sides. Because reflex magnitude varies between individuals, asymmetry is particularly important. If the two sides differ, I will report the grade for each side rather than assigning a single averaged value.”

4. Why technique matters — setup and hammer mechanics

Before you test

  • Explain the maneuver.
  • Seat the patient comfortably.
  • Support the limb yourself.
  • Make the limb truly relaxed.
  • Use an intermediate joint position (about 90° at the elbow).
  • Test the same reflex on both sides for comparison.

How to swing

  • Loose grip
  • Loose wrist
  • Swing, don’t peck
  • One crisp impact
  • Let the hammer rebound
Most common student error: stiff, short “pecking” movements. DeMyer specifically teaches a loose-wrist swinging strike.

Suggested opening line: “I’m going to test the reflexes in your arms. I’ll support your arm and lightly tap a few areas with the reflex hammer. Please keep your arm completely relaxed.”

5. Reinforcement — Jendrassik principle and counterpressure

Reinforcement is used when the reflex is difficult to elicit despite an adequate first attempt. DeMyer distinguishes the two methods by which muscles the patient contracts and how strongly.

Jendrassik / pull principle

Rule: strongly contract a set of muscles not being tested.

Classic DeMyer example: while the examiner tests the quadriceps reflex, the patient locks the hands together and pulls apart hard.

Upper-extremity adaptation: for a difficult biceps reflex, DeMyer’s clinical example says to ask the seated patient to tense the leg muscles, keeping the arm itself relaxed.

Counterpressure

Rule: weakly / slightly contract the muscle being tested against a small examiner-applied force.

Classic DeMyer example: apply slight pressure against the tibia and ask the patient to generate only enough quadriceps tension to counterbalance it, then strike the patellar tendon.

Upper-extremity limitation: DeMyer specifically says counterpressure is inconvenient for arm reflexes.

DeMyer Fig. 7-14 — Pull method of Jendrassik
The patient locks the hands and pulls apart hard while the examiner strikes the tendon. This illustrates strong contraction of muscles not being tested.
DeMyer Fig. 7-15 — Counterpressure method
The examiner applies slight pressure against the tibia; the patient counteracts it with only slight quadriceps tension while the tendon is struck.

Why reinforcement can help

Functionally, reinforcement makes a difficult MSR easier to elicit by increasing the readiness of the reflex motor system. The practical distinction you should remember for DeMyer is:

JENDRASSIK = STRONG contraction ELSEWHERE   |   COUNTERPRESSURE = SLIGHT contraction HERE

Exact bedside language

Remote reinforcement for an arm reflex
“Keep this arm completely relaxed. At the same time, tense your leg muscles strongly while I tap the tendon.”
Counterpressure concept
“Push against my hand only enough to match my pressure — do not push hard.”

Why “too much” counterpressure is wrong

Counterpressure is intentionally slight. If the patient contracts the tested muscle strongly, the muscle/tendon unit becomes too tense for the hammer blow to create the desired sudden spindle stretch cleanly.

7. Biceps reflex — C5–C6

Muscle
Biceps
Nerve association
Musculocutaneous
Expected response
Biceps contraction with elbow flexion
Visual guide — Biceps reflex (DeMyer Fig. 7-7)
The examiner’s thumb places slight tension on the biceps tendon and bicipital aponeurosis, and the hammer strikes the examiner’s thumbnail with a crisp blow.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Support the forearm with the elbow about 90° and completely relaxed.
    “For the biceps reflex, I support the patient’s forearm and keep the elbow in an intermediate position while ensuring the arm is completely relaxed.”
    Why: DeMyer says the part should be at rest and usually in an intermediate joint position.
  2. Palpate and locate the biceps tendon.
    “I palpate and identify the biceps tendon in the antecubital region.”
  3. Place your thumb over the tendon and aponeurosis, creating slight tension.
    “I place my thumb over the biceps tendon and bicipital aponeurosis to create slight tension.”
    Why: This is exactly how DeMyer sets up the maneuver.
  4. Strike your thumbnail with one crisp swing.
    “I then strike my thumbnail with a rapid, crisp blow.”
    Concept: The blow is transmitted to the tendon, rapidly stretching the biceps spindles.
  5. Observe and feel the response.
    “I observe for biceps contraction and flexion at the elbow.”
  6. Repeat on the opposite side and compare.
    “I perform the same maneuver on the other side using similar positioning and stimulus to compare symmetry.”
  7. Grade the response.
    Example: “Biceps reflexes are 2+ and symmetric bilaterally.”
Conceptual correlation: “The tendon tap creates a sudden stretch, the biceps muscle spindles fire, the C5–C6 reflex pathway is activated, and the result is biceps contraction with elbow flexion.”

Visual guide — Brachioradialis reflex (DeMyer Fig. 7-9)
DeMyer explicitly contrasts the correct technique—placing your thumb over the radius and striking your own thumbnail—with the wrong technique of repeatedly striking the patient’s unprotected radius.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Cradle the forearm completely so it is relaxed.
    “For the brachioradialis reflex, I cradle the patient’s forearm so it is fully supported and relaxed.”
  2. Keep the forearm in a comfortable neutral / semipronated position.
  3. Place your thumb over the radius.
    “I place my thumb over the radius in the area of the reflex.”
  4. Strike your own thumbnail.
    “I strike my own thumbnail rather than the patient’s exposed radius.”
    Key point: This is a very testable DeMyer detail.
  5. Observe the response and compare side to side.
    “I observe the forearm response, repeat on the opposite side, and compare symmetry.”
  6. Grade and record.
Likely grading pearl: if your examiner knows DeMyer, protecting the radius with your thumb and striking your thumbnail is exactly the kind of technique detail they may want to see.

Visual guide — Triceps reflex (DeMyer Fig. 7-8)
DeMyer shows two acceptable positions: (A) let the forearm dangle over your hand, or (B) cradle the forearm in your hand; in both methods, strike the triceps tendon directly.

Step-by-step procedure

  1. Support the arm so the forearm is relaxed.
    “For the triceps reflex, I support the patient’s arm and allow the forearm to remain completely relaxed.”
  2. Use either of DeMyer’s two accepted positions.
    “I may either let the forearm dangle over my hand or cradle it in my hand, depending on which position gives better relaxation.”
  3. Identify the triceps tendon.
    “I palpate and identify the triceps tendon.”
  4. Strike the triceps tendon directly with a crisp blow.
    “I strike the triceps tendon directly with a rapid, crisp blow.”
    Concept: This rapidly stretches the triceps spindles.
  5. Observe the response.
    “I observe for triceps contraction and elbow extension.”
  6. Repeat on the opposite side, then grade.
Doctor asks: “Which triceps position is correct?”

Both are correct. DeMyer illustrates both the dangling-forearm and cradled-forearm methods. Use the one that gives better relaxation and tendon access.

10. Finger-flexion reflexes — Trömner and Hoffmann (C7–T1)

Important source distinction:
DeMyer presents Trömner and Hoffmann as methods of eliciting the finger-flexion MSR.
UERM manual separately labels Hoffmann’s sign as the “Babinski of the upper extremity.”
Because your two course sources frame Hoffmann differently, report the actual response clearly and interpret it with the rest of the neurologic examination.

A. Trömner method

DeMyer Fig. 7-10 — Trömner finger-flexion reflex
Support the completely relaxed hand and briskly flip the distal phalanx upward. DeMyer describes flexion of the patient’s fingers and thumb in response to stretch of the finger flexor muscles.
  1. Support the hand completely. The patient should not actively hold the fingers or wrist.
  2. Make sure the fingers are relaxed.
  3. Briskly flip the distal phalanx upward.
    “Using Trömner’s method, I support the relaxed hand and briskly flip the distal phalanx upward.”
  4. Observe the fingers and thumb.
    Expected DeMyer response: flexion of the fingers and thumb caused by stretch of the finger flexor muscles.
  5. Repeat on the other side and compare.

How to report Trömner

Response elicited symmetrically
“Trömner finger-flexion response is present and symmetric bilaterally.”
Asymmetric response
“Trömner response is more brisk on the right than the left.”
or “Trömner response is elicited on the right and not elicited on the left.”

What counts as “pathologic” for Trömner?

What DeMyer actually supports

Trömner is presented as a finger-flexion MSR technique. DeMyer also states that finger-flexion MSRs may be unobtainable in normal people.

Therefore

Do not call the simple presence or absence of a Trömner response pathologic by itself based on DeMyer.

When it becomes concerning

Interpretive correlation A distinctly brisk or asymmetric finger-flexion response is more meaningful when it accompanies a broader pattern of hyperreflexia or other upper-motor-neuron findings.

Do not say: “Positive Trömner automatically means an UMN lesion.” That standalone claim is not what DeMyer states in this section.

B. Hoffmann method / Hoffmann’s sign

DeMyer Fig. 7-11 — Hoffmann finger-flexion method
Depress the distal phalanx and allow it to flip upward. The sudden extension stretches the flexors and causes flexion of the fingers and thumb. DeMyer notes that this method is effective only with very brisk MSRs.
  1. Support the patient’s relaxed hand.
  2. Hold the middle/distal finger securely without making the patient tense.
  3. Depress the distal phalanx / nail and let it suddenly flip upward.
    “I depress the distal phalanx and allow it to flip upward suddenly.”
  4. Observe for flexion of the fingers and thumb.
    DeMyer: the sudden extension stretches the finger flexors; this method is effective only when the MSRs are very brisk.
  5. Repeat on the opposite side.

How to report Hoffmann

No flexor response
“Hoffmann response is not elicited bilaterally.”
In sign language: “Hoffmann’s sign is negative bilaterally.”
Flexion of fingers/thumb occurs
“Hoffmann response is present bilaterally and symmetric.”
In UERM sign language: “Hoffmann’s sign is positive bilaterally.”

If Hoffmann is asymmetric

“Hoffmann’s sign is positive on the right and negative on the left.”
or, if both are present but unequal: “Hoffmann responses are bilateral but more brisk on the right.”

Pathologic significance: how to answer safely from your sources

UERM framing

The manual calls Hoffmann’s sign the “Babinski of the upper extremity” and places it with pathologic reflexes.

UERMMMCI Manual p. 56
DeMyer framing

DeMyer presents it as a finger-flexion MSR method and specifically says it is effective only when MSRs are very brisk.

DeMyer Fig. 7-11, p. 255
What to say if asked “Is it pathologic?”

“A Hoffmann response indicates a brisk finger-flexion reflex. In our UERM manual it is treated as a pathologic upper-extremity sign, but I would correlate it with symmetry and the rest of the UMN examination rather than interpret it in isolation.”

High-yield distinction: “positive” means you actually observed the finger/thumb flexion response. “Pathologic” is an interpretation of that finding in clinical context—not a substitute for describing what happened.

11. If a reflex is difficult to obtain — troubleshooting

Do not immediately call a reflex “0.” DeMyer’s clinical problem gives a practical order of operations.

  1. Check the stimulus.
    “Was my blow crisp enough, or was I pecking?”
  2. Recheck relaxation. Make sure the patient is not voluntarily holding the limb.
  3. Change the joint angle slightly. This changes the mechanical tension on the muscle/tendon.
  4. For biceps, alter thumb pressure on the tendon. DeMyer specifically suggests more or less pressure and slight elbow repositioning.
  5. Use reinforcement. Choose Jendrassik-type remote contraction or, where practical, counterpressure.
  6. Only after adequate attempts should you call the reflex absent.
For a hard-to-obtain biceps reflex specifically: DeMyer’s worked example says to change thumb pressure, change the elbow angle, and ask the patient to tense the leg muscles for reinforcement. He notes that counterpressure is inconvenient for arm reflexes.

12. Interpretation and localization — after the demo / if asked

If the reflex is truly decreased or absent

Adequate stimulus?→ Muscle spindle / afferent limb→ Spinal segment / LMN→ Peripheral motor axon→ NMJ→ Muscle

DeMyer’s approach is to think through the reflex arc. A defect anywhere in that chain can reduce or abolish the MSR. If the afferent limb is interrupted, sensory loss often accompanies the areflexia. If the lesion is purely LMN / efferent, weakness and atrophy may occur with preserved sensation.

Doctor asks: “Does 3+ automatically mean pathology?”

No. Your UERM manual explicitly says 3+ is brisker than average and may or may not indicate disease. Interpret it with symmetry, tone, weakness pattern, and other UMN signs.

Doctor asks: “What would make you think of an afferent-arc lesion?”

Areflexia together with sensory loss suggests interruption of spindle afferents or the sensory root/nerve, because the afferent pathway carries other sensory fibers as well.

Doctor asks: “What would make you think of an LMN lesion?”

Weakness, atrophy, and loss of the MSR, with or without sensory loss depending on whether the lesion is purely motor or part of a mixed peripheral nerve lesion.

13. Full live script you can rehearse

Opening: “I will demonstrate the upper-extremity muscle stretch reflexes. I first position the patient comfortably and ensure that the upper limbs are completely relaxed. I will test corresponding reflexes on both sides for direct comparison.”

Biceps: “For the biceps reflex, C5 to C6, I support the forearm with the elbow in an intermediate position. I palpate the biceps tendon, place my thumb over the tendon and bicipital aponeurosis to create slight tension, and strike my thumbnail with a rapid crisp blow. I observe for biceps contraction and elbow flexion, then repeat on the opposite side.”

Triceps: “For the triceps reflex, C7 to C8, I support the patient’s arm and allow the forearm to remain relaxed. I identify the triceps tendon and strike it directly with a crisp blow. I observe for triceps contraction and elbow extension, then repeat on the opposite side.”

Brachioradialis: “For the brachioradialis reflex, C5 to C6, I cradle the relaxed forearm, place my thumb over the radius, and strike my thumbnail rather than the patient’s exposed radius. I observe the response and compare the two sides.”

Optional finger flexion: “Using Trömner’s method for the finger flexion reflex, C7 to T1, I support the completely relaxed hand and briskly flip the distal phalanx upward. The response is flexion of the fingers and thumb.”

Closing: “I then grade the responses and assess their symmetry. In this patient, the upper-extremity reflexes are ___ and symmetric/asymmetric.”

If your volunteer is asymmetric, change the closing line. Never say “2+ bilaterally” if that is not what you actually found.
Asymmetric example:
“The biceps reflex is 2+ on the right and 1+ on the left; the response is asymmetric and relatively reduced on the left. I would correlate this with the remaining motor and sensory examination.”
Trömner reporting:
“Trömner finger-flexion responses are present and symmetric bilaterally.”
or “The Trömner response is more brisk on the right than the left.”
Hoffmann reporting:
“Hoffmann’s sign is negative bilaterally.”
or “Hoffmann’s sign is positive on the right and negative on the left.”
