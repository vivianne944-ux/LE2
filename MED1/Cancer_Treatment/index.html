I. Detection, Diagnosis & Staging

pp. 529–530
Must know
  • Early diagnosis matters most. Patients have the best chance of cure or longer life when cancer is caught early. So if a sign or symptom is persistent, keep cancer in the differential even if it's statistically unlikely.
  • "Tumor" means "swelling" (Latin). The swelling comes from ↑ interstitial fluid pressure plus ↑ cell and stromal mass.
  • Leukemia is the exception. It's a cancer of blood-forming tissue, so it's spread throughout the body from the start and often has no mass.
  • Tumors can present through organ dysfunction: dyspnea from anemia (leukemia replacing marrow), cough (lung cancer), jaundice (bile duct blockage), neuro signs (glioma), or bleeding.
  • Paraneoplastic syndrome = symptoms caused by substances the tumor secretes, not by the mass itself.
  • Diagnosis needs a biopsy with pathologic confirmation in most cases. The rare exception: a likely brainstem glioma, where biopsy would be too dangerous and imaging is already clear.

Biopsy options (best to least reliable)

TypeWhat is removedWhy it matters
ExcisionalThe whole mass plus a margin of normal tissueOptimal. Gives the full picture.
IncisionalA wedge covering most of the tumor's diameter2nd choice. A wide wedge lowers sampling error.
Core needleA thin core, much less tissueUsually still enough to plan treatment.
FNAA suspension of loose cellsIf positive, it can start treatment. But it's unreliable as the only test, and a negative FNA does NOT rule out cancer.

Cutting into a tumor can help it spread, so the surgeon should be involved early to plan a biopsy that won't compromise a later curative surgery.

Staging (TNM)

Staging tells you how far the disease has spread, which decides whether to start with local treatment, combined treatment, or systemic treatment.

T = tumor size or invasion into nearby structures
N = number and nature of involved lymph node groups
M = presence of metastases

Clinical stage comes from imaging. Pathologic stage comes from tissue removed at surgery.

Stage I (T1, N0–N1, M0): small, little or no node spread → usually curable with local treatment.

Stage IV: distant mets or unresectable invasion → treated with palliative intent, except cancers that are unusually sensitive to chemo or immunotherapy.

Exam trap

TNM is not useful for leukemia (marrow involvement is never "localized") or CNS tumors (histology and how much can be resected predict outcome better).

II. Treatment Goals & Tumor Growth

pp. 530–531

Curative intent

Goal is to eradicate the cancer. Because cure is at stake, severe toxicity is accepted.

Palliative intent

Cure isn't possible, so the goal is symptom relief and quality of life while extending life. Here minimizing toxicity becomes a main goal.

Two main types of treatment
├── Local: surgery, radiation (incl. photodynamic therapy), ablation (radiofrequency, cryosurgery)
└── Systemic: chemotherapy (incl. hormonal and targeted drugs) and biologic therapy (incl. immunotherapy)

Gompertzian tumor growth

Tumors don't grow at a constant rate. They grow fastest when small, then slow down as they run short of nutrients.

Small tumor → most cells are dividing (high growth fraction)
↓
Fastest growth happens at ~37% of max size, which is before the tumor can be detected
↓
Detectable at ~10⁹ cells (1 cm³). By now only 1–4% of cells are dividing in many solid tumors
↓
Kills the patient at ~10¹² cells (1 kg)
Exam trap

By the time you diagnose a solid tumor, its growth fraction is already low (1–4%). This is part of why many drugs that target dividing cells work poorly on large tumors.

III. Surgery

p. 531

Surgery is the most effective cancer treatment and cures at least 40% of cancer patients. Its limit: many solid-tumor patients already have metastases that can't be removed.

Even when it can't cure, surgery helps with local control, preserving organ function, debulking (so later therapy works better), and more accurate staging.

R0 resection
Tumor fully removed with an adequate margin of normal tissue (the goal for cure)
R1 / R2
Tumor left at the margin: R1 = microscopic, R2 = visible (macroscopic). May need reoperation
Sentinel node biopsy
Inject dye or radioisotope, remove the first node it drains to. Gives node status without removing all nodes, avoiding lymphedema and lymphangiosarcoma
Adjuvant therapy
Chemo given after surgery removed all visible disease, to kill cells that may have already spread
Neoadjuvant therapy
Chemo and/or radiation given before surgery to shrink the tumor (e.g., bulky testicular cancer, stage III breast cancer)
  • Thanks to better adjuvant therapy, smaller surgeries work as well: lumpectomy + radiation = modified radical mastectomy, and limb-sparing surgery replaced amputation for most childhood rhabdomyosarcomas and osteosarcomas.
  • Surgery can even cure some metastatic disease: osteosarcoma with limited lung mets, and colon cancer with fewer than 5 liver mets in one lobe (hepatic lobectomy gives ~25% long-term disease-free survival).
  • Hormone removal: oophorectomy removes estrogen (breast cancer), orchiectomy removes androgen (prostate cancer).
  • Prevention: prophylactic mastectomy, colectomy, oophorectomy, and thyroidectomy in genetic cancer syndromes.
  • Hospitals doing more of a procedure tend to have better outcomes.

IV. Radiation Therapy

pp. 531–532

How it kills cells

Ionizing radiation hits water in the cell
↓
Water is split into hydroxyl free radicals (OH•), and DNA strands break directly
↓
Radicals damage DNA, membranes, proteins → cell death

Oxygen makes radiation work better because it boosts free radical damage. So hypoxic tumor cells are more resistant.

Dose unit
Measured as energy absorbed by the tissue, not what the machine puts out. 1 Gy = 1 J/kg = 100 cGy. The old unit, 1 rad = 1 cGy
What determines effect
Total dose, number of fractions, and treatment time
Why split into fractions?
Dividing cells are more sensitive than resting cells. Giving small doses repeatedly catches more cells as they enter division. Typical curative course: 150–200 cGy per day, 5 days a week
Delivery
Teletherapy: external beam aimed from a distance (most common). Brachytherapy: radioactive source placed in or next to the tumor. Systemic: radioactive drug given IV
Beam types
High-energy (megavoltage) beams scatter less → skin-sparing. Protons deposit energy in a narrow range. Electrons barely penetrate → used for skin tumors
Radiosensitizers
Drugs like cisplatin that get into DNA and boost the radiation effect (head & neck, cervix, rectal cancer)

Most radiosensitive

Testis, ovary, bone marrow. Any marrow inside the radiation field is wiped out.

Most radioresistant

Bone. Its main effect is in children: early closure of growth plates. Heart, muscle, and nerves (low cell turnover) are also resistant.

ToxicityWhat happens
Whole-bodyFatigue, poor appetite, nausea, vomiting, because injured tissue releases cytokines
AcuteMucositis, skin redness, marrow suppression. Usually improve with a break in treatment
Chronic (more serious)Head & neck RT → hypothyroidism, cataracts, and loss of saliva → dental caries. Chest RT → coronary artery disease. Also lung fibrosis, constrictive pericarditis, radiation cystitis or enteritis
Second cancersNew solid tumors in or near the field, about 1% per year, starting in the second decade after treatment

Other local options: RFA uses nonionizing microwave heat (e.g., liver mets). Cryosurgery freezes lesions (prostate, kidney). Photodynamic therapy: cancer cells absorb porphyrins, then laser light turns them into free radicals.

V. Systemic Therapy Basics

pp. 532–535
TypeWhat it isWhat it attacks
Cytotoxic chemoSmall molecules found by screening for tumor-killing activityDNA or microtubules (cell division)
Targeted therapySmall molecules designed against one moleculeA specific protein the tumor depends on
Biologic therapyAntibodies, cells, cytokines, virusesTumor antigens or the host immune system
Therapeutic index
How far apart the toxic dose and the effective dose are. Cytotoxics have a narrow index because normal cells also have DNA and microtubules, so they get hit too
Oncogene addiction
Some tumors depend on one overactive pathway to survive. Blocking that "driver" with a targeted drug pushes the tumor cell toward death
Synthetic lethality
A tumor with a DNA-repair defect survives on its own, but becomes lethally vulnerable when a drug knocks out its backup repair or damages DNA (e.g., PARP inhibitors in BRCA tumors)

Using chemo

  • Adjuvant = after local treatment, to clear hidden spread (can cure some breast, colorectal, lung cancers).
  • Neoadjuvant = before local treatment, to make it work better.
  • High-dose chemo can cure more but is life-threatening, so it needs stem cell support (from patient or donor) or a rescue drug.
  • Usual doses cause reversible side effects, mainly temporary low blood counts and nausea.

Drug development

Phase 1: find the safe dose. Start at 1/6–1/10 of the dose toxic in the more sensitive animal, then escalate to the maximum-tolerated dose (MTD)
↓
Phase 2: test whether it works at a fixed dose in one tumor type
↓
Phase 3: compare against standard treatment for survival or time to progression

Tumor response

  • Complete response: all tumor disappears
  • Partial response (RECIST): ≥30% decrease in tumor size (older definition: 50%)
  • Progression: >25% increase or any new lesion (the figure uses 20%)
  • Stable disease: none of the above
  • Immunotherapy can make tumors look bigger first (inflammation) before they shrink

Toxicity grades

  • 1: no treatment needed
  • 2: needs symptom treatment, not life-threatening
  • 3: could become life-threatening if untreated
  • 4: actually life-threatening
  • 5: causes death

Performance status (ECOG)

PSPatient is...
0Fully active, no symptoms
1Walking around, only limited in strenuous activity
2Up and about ≥50% of the day but can't work
3Up <50% of the day, limited self-care
4Stuck in bed or chair, can't care for self
Exam trap

Palliative chemo is generally only for PS 0–2. But if the cancer is curable, even a poor-PS patient may be treated (their outcome is just worse).

Which advanced cancers can chemo still cure?

Acute leukemias (ALL, AML) · Hodgkin's and some lymphomas · germ cell tumors (e.g., seminoma, choriocarcinoma) · gestational trophoblastic neoplasia · pediatric tumors (Wilms', Ewing's, neuroblastoma, embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma) · small-cell lung · ovarian.

Respond poorly when advanced: pancreatic, biliary, thyroid, prostate, liver (HCC), non-small-cell lung, some melanomas.

VI. Immunotherapy & Biologics

pp. 535–539

Unlike chemo, biologics only work if the tumor or immune system does its part (e.g., the tumor expresses the target antigen, or T cells engage). So the best dose may be an "optimal biologic dose," not the maximum tolerated.

Checkpoint inhibitors: releasing the brakes

Antigen-presenting cells show tumor antigen to T cells → T cells activate (gas pedal)
↓
Two brakes stop them: CTLA4 (on T cells) and PD-1 (on T cells) binding PD-L1 (made by the tumor to switch T cells off)
↓
Antibody blocks the brake → T cells stay active → kill tumor cells
Brake blockedDrugs
CTLA4Ipilimumab: first proof that this approach works. Used in metastatic melanoma and lung cancer
PD-1Nivolumab, pembrolizumab. Pembrolizumab replaced chemo as first-line for metastatic NSCLC that expresses PD-L1
PD-L1Atezolizumab, avelumab, durvalumab
Must know
  • They work best in tumors with many mutations (high tumor mutational burden or mismatch-repair deficiency). More mutations → more abnormal proteins → immune system sees the tumor as foreign. This led to the first "tissue-agnostic" approval: treat based on the mutation profile, regardless of organ.
  • Side effects are autoimmune (hepatitis, endocrine problems, rash, colitis, neuro) and can appear late, even months after.
  • Severe cases need steroids, which don't seem to reduce the anticancer response.

Other antibodies

Drug (target)Used forKey side effect
Rituximab (CD20 on B cells)B-cell lymphomasWipes out B cells → infections and hepatitis B/C reactivation, so screen for hepatitis first
Cetuximab, panitumumab (EGFR)Colorectal, head & neckAcne-like rash, photosensitivity
Trastuzumab, pertuzumab (HER2)HER2+ breast cancerHeart damage, worse if patient had anthracyclines → check heart function before and during
Bevacizumab (VEGF, a blood vessel growth signal)Kidney, colorectal, lung, gliomaHypertension, proteinuria, clots, bleeding, GI perforation
Blinatumomab (links CD19 on B cells to CD3 on T cells)Acute lymphoblastic leukemiaCytokine release syndrome (fever, low BP, fast HR) and neurotoxicity, first seen as worsening handwriting

Any antibody can cause an infusion reaction, usually only at the first infusion. Prevent with steroid/antihistamine premedication.

Cell therapy, cytokines, vaccines

TherapyKey point
Allogeneic bone marrow transplantStrongest proof the immune system can kill cancer: donor T cells attack the tumor (graft-versus-tumor). Main risk: graft-versus-host disease
CAR-T cellsPatient's own T cells are engineered to target CD19, then given back. Used for refractory B-cell lymphomas. Side effects: cytokine release syndrome + neurotoxicity
IL-2Boosts T and NK cells. High doses shrink metastatic melanoma and kidney cancer, with durable cure in 2–5%. Very toxic (may need ICU for low BP)
Interferon-αNever curative, but gives partial responses (hairy cell leukemia, CML, melanoma, Kaposi's). Side effects: flu-like illness, depression
Sipuleucel-TVaccine made from patient's immune cells for prostate cancer. Tumors don't shrink, but survival improves by ~4 months
IMiDs (thalidomide, lenalidomide)Cornerstone for multiple myeloma. Side effects: blood clots, neuropathy, severe birth defects
BCGInstilled into the bladder for early (preinvasive) bladder cancer
HBV and HPV vaccinesPrevent liver and cervical cancer, but cannot treat an existing one

VII. Cytotoxic Chemotherapy

pp. 539–543

Phase-nonspecific

Kill cells in any phase of the cell cycle. Example: alkylating agents.

Phase-specific

Need cells in a certain phase. S phase (DNA copying): antimetabolites, topoisomerase I drugs. M phase (mitosis): vincas, taxanes.

Alkylating agents

They chemically attach to DNA bases, cross-linking the strands so the cell can't divide and triggers apoptosis. Shared side effects: low counts, hair loss, infertility, mouth sores, lung fibrosis, and secondary leukemia years later.

DrugWhat to remember
CyclophosphamideInactive until the liver activates it. A byproduct, acrolein, irritates the bladder → hemorrhagic cystitis. Prevent with good hydration and mesna
IfosfamideSame drug family, so mesna is always required. Can also cause confusion or psychosis
CisplatinDamages kidneys (give lots of fluid + mannitol), causes low magnesium → low calcium → tetany, hearing loss in 50%, nerve damage, and severe vomiting. Relatively little marrow suppression
CarboplatinGentler on kidneys, ears, nerves, but more marrow suppression. Cleared only by kidney, so dose is based on creatinine clearance
OxaliplatinUsed in colon cancer. Mainly neurotoxic
NitrosoureasMarrow suppression that is delayed and cumulative
ProcarbazineDisulfiram-like reaction with alcohol. Also acts as an MAOI
TemozolomideOral. Works best in brain tumors lacking the DNA repair enzyme AGT

Antitumor antibiotics & topoisomerase drugs

Topoisomerases untangle DNA during copying. Topo I cuts one strand, topo II cuts both. These drugs trap the enzyme so the breaks don't reseal.

DrugWhat to remember
DoxorubicinInserts into DNA, blocks topo II, and makes free radicals. Main danger: heart failure, 10% risk once the total lifetime dose exceeds 550 mg/m² (risk tracks with peak blood levels, so slower infusions are safer). Dexrazoxane protects the heart. Risk rises with trastuzumab. Severe tissue damage if it leaks from the IV. Reduce dose in liver disease
MitoxantroneRelated drug with less heart toxicity. Turns urine, nails, sclerae blue
BleomycinMakes free radicals using iron and oxygen. Main danger: lung fibrosis, more common above 300 units total. Lung and skin are hit because they have little of the enzyme that breaks bleomycin down. Earliest sign: ↓ DLCO or cough. Does not suppress marrow. Keep oxygen low during surgery even years later
IrinotecanTopo I drug turned into its active form SN-38. People with Gilbert's syndrome (UGT1A1 defect) can't clear SN-38 → more toxicity. Early diarrhea (acetylcholine) → atropine. Late diarrhea (48–72 h) → loperamide
EtoposideTopo II drug. Drops BP if infused too fast

Antimetabolites

They mimic building blocks of DNA, blocking DNA synthesis. They hit S-phase cells, and toxicity grows the longer the exposure. Common side effects: mouth sores, diarrhea, low counts.

DrugWhat to remember
MethotrexateBlocks dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), so cells run out of folate needed to make thymine. Leucovorin (a form of folate) bypasses the block and rescues normal cells. Cleared by kidney, so NSAIDs, aspirin, probenecid increase toxicity. High doses can crystallize in kidneys → alkalinize the urine and hydrate
5-FUBlocks thymidylate synthase. Patients lacking the enzyme DPD can't break it down → severe toxicity. Here leucovorin boosts the drug's effect. Capecitabine = oral version (hand-foot syndrome)
PemetrexedFolate blocker given with folate and B12 to protect normal tissue
6-MPBroken down by xanthine oxidase, which allopurinol blocks → must lower the 6-MP dose. TPMT deficiency also causes toxicity
CytarabineCan cause conjunctivitis and cerebellar ataxia
AsparaginaseEnzyme that removes asparagine leukemia cells need. Causes allergy, pancreatitis, high sugar, and clotting problems (bleeding and clots)

Microtubule drugs

Vinca alkaloids: break down microtubules

  • Vincristine: mainly nerve damage (numbness, constipation/ileus, jaw pain, SIADH) and no marrow suppression at usual doses
  • Vinblastine: more marrow suppression
  • Both damage tissue if they leak. Reduce dose in liver disease

Taxanes: freeze microtubules in place

  • Paclitaxel: its solvent causes allergic reactions → premedicate with dexamethasone + diphenhydramine + cimetidine
  • Docetaxel: fluid retention
  • Both: neuropathy and marrow suppression

pp. 543–553

Breast cancer must be tested for hormone receptors before hormonal therapy. Prostate and lymphoid tumors always have their receptor. Interesting twist: stopping an antiestrogen or antiandrogen can make tumors shrink, because mutant receptors start using the drug as a growth signal.

Must know · APL

Acute promyelocytic leukemia has t(15;17), which creates a fusion protein (PML-RARα) that stops leukemia cells from maturing. ATRA (a vitamin A form) releases this block so the cells mature. ATRA + arsenic trioxide gives very high remission rates.

  • ATRA risk: differentiation syndrome (fever, lung infiltrates, effusions from maturing cells releasing cytokines) → treat with steroids.
  • Arsenic risk: QT prolongation → keep K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ normal.

Why treatments stop working (resistance)

  • Drug pumped out: the MDR gene makes P-glycoprotein, a pump that removes many drugs from cells.
  • More target made: e.g., DHFR gene amplification overwhelms methotrexate.
  • Cell won't die: loss of apoptosis genes (e.g., low BAX in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer).
  • Target mutates or the tumor finds a bypass route, and different tumor subclones respond differently.

IX. Supportive Care

pp. 553–555

Low blood counts

Cells with the shortest lifespan drop first, because chemo stops new production:

CellLifespanHow much chemo affects it
Neutrophils6–8 hoursMost
Platelets5–7 daysLess
Red cells120 daysLeast
  • Counts usually bottom out (nadir) 6–14 days after chemo. With nitrosoureas, DTIC, and procarbazine, it can be delayed to 6 weeks.
  • Febrile neutropenia = fever + fewer than 1500 granulocytes/μL. Granulocyte transfusions are not used (cells die within hours and can harm the lungs).
  • G-CSF boosts neutrophil production. Give it preventively if the chance of febrile neutropenia is ≥20%, or in high-risk patients (age >65 with lymphoma, poor PS, previous febrile neutropenia). Start 24–72 hours after chemo, never at the same time.
  • It shows no benefit once the patient is already neutropenic. GM-CSF may be harmful in AML.
  • Platelets: transfuse at ≤10,000/μL (keep above 20,000 in leukemia with fever). Avoid NSAIDs.
  • Red cells: transfuse when Hb <8 g/dL (or keep >9 if coronary disease). Erythropoietin may help tumors survive.

Nausea & vomiting (most common chemo side effect)

Can be acute (within 24 h), delayed (after 24 h), or anticipatory (before chemo).

Vomiting riskDrugsPrevention
High (>90%)Cisplatin, DTIC, high-dose cyclophosphamide (>1500 mg/m²)Three drugs: 5-HT3 blocker (ondansetron) + dexamethasone + NK1 blocker (aprepitant)
Moderate (30–90%)Carboplatin, ifosfamide, anthracyclines5-HT3 blocker + dexamethasone
Low (10–30%)5-FU, taxanes, etoposideDexamethasone alone
Minimal (<10%)Antibodies, bleomycin, vincasUsually none

Diarrhea

Hydrate. Loperamide 4 mg first, then 2 mg every 2 h until 12 h without loose stools (max 16 mg/day). Octreotide if it fails.

Mouth sores

Palifermin (a growth factor for mucosal cells) prevents severe mucositis with high-dose chemo + stem cell transplant.

Hair loss

Worst with anthracyclines, alkylators, topo drugs. Scalp cooling is controversial because the scalp can hide tumor cells.

Pregnancy & fertility

  • The first trimester is the most dangerous time for chemo, so delay if possible.
  • Antimetabolites (especially folate blockers like methotrexate) are harmful throughout pregnancy. Most other regimens can be given in the 2nd and 3rd trimesters.
  • Alkylators and topo drugs reliably stop ovulation and sperm production → offer sperm banking.
  • Periods usually return if treatment ends before age 30, rarely after 35, and early menopause is common.

Palliative care: when chemo no longer helps, focusing on comfort and quality of life (hospice, family and spiritual support) is a valid treatment plan, not "giving up".

Traps & Confusions

Adjuvant vs neoadjuvant
Adjuvant = after surgery. Neoadjuvant = before surgery ("neo" comes first).

Vincas vs taxanes
Vincas break down microtubules. Taxanes freeze them. Both stop mitosis.

Vincristine vs vinblastine
Vincristine = nerves, spares marrow. Vinblastine = marrow ("vinBlastine hits Blood").

Cisplatin vs carboplatin
Cisplatin = kidney, ear, nerve, vomiting. Carboplatin = marrow.

CTLA4 vs PD-L1
CTLA4 is on T cells. PD-L1 is on tumor cells.

Leucovorin
Rescues from methotrexate but boosts 5-FU.

A negative FNA doesn't rule out cancer.

Bone is most radioresistant. Testis, ovary, marrow are most sensitive.

Bleomycin hits the lung, not the marrow. Keep oxygen low in surgery even years later.

On allopurinol? Lower the 6-MP dose.

T315I mutation: only ponatinib works.

G-CSF helps prevent neutropenia but doesn't help once it's present. GM-CSF may harm in AML.
